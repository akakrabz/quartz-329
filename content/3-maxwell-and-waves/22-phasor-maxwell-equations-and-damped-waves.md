> [!abstract] In one breath
> If every field and source oscillates at one frequency, each time derivative in Maxwell's equations becomes a factor $j\omega$, and the equations become algebra for the phasors of [[3-maxwell-and-waves/21-monochromatic-waves-and-phasor-notation|Lecture 21]]. With no conductivity this reproduces the plane waves of [[3-maxwell-and-waves/18-the-wave-equation-and-plane-tem-waves|Lecture 18]] in a few lines. The wave equation becomes the Helmholtz equation $\nabla^2\tilde{\mathbf{E}} + \omega^2\mu\epsilon\,\tilde{\mathbf{E}} = 0$, solved by $e^{\mp j\beta z}$. Conductivity adds a current $\sigma\tilde{\mathbf{E}}$ beside the displacement current $j\omega\epsilon\tilde{\mathbf{E}}$, so Ampère's law carries $\sigma + j\omega\epsilon$ where it had $j\omega\epsilon$. That single replacement turns $j\beta$ into the complex **propagation constant** $\gamma = \alpha + j\beta = \sqrt{j\omega\mu(\sigma + j\omega\epsilon)}$ and makes the **intrinsic impedance** $\eta = \sqrt{j\omega\mu/(\sigma + j\omega\epsilon)}$ complex. The wave now decays as $e^{-\alpha z}$, by $1/e$ over the **penetration depth** $\delta = 1/\alpha$, and $\mathbf{H}$ lags $\mathbf{E}$ by the angle $\tau$ of $\eta$. Everything hangs on one ratio, $\sigma/\omega\epsilon$: conduction current over displacement current, or, in the language of [[1-electrostatics/08-conductors-dielectrics-and-polarization|Lecture 8]], $1/\omega$ against the relaxation time $\epsilon/\sigma$. A small ratio gives an imperfect dielectric, almost lossless. A large one gives a good conductor, in which the wave dies within a fraction of a wavelength. [[3-maxwell-and-waves/23-imperfect-dielectrics-and-good-conductors|Lecture 23]] works out those two limits in detail.

## 1. Maxwell's equations at one frequency

[[3-maxwell-and-waves/21-monochromatic-waves-and-phasor-notation|Lecture 21]] replaced a cosine of one frequency by a complex number, its **phasor** ([[concepts/phasors]]). This lecture does the same to every field and every source in Maxwell's equations at once. Suppose all of them oscillate at the same angular frequency $\omega$: they are **monochromatic**, each with its own amplitude and phase at each point. Then

$$
\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{r},t) = \text{Re}\{\tilde{\mathbf{E}}(\mathbf{r})\,e^{j\omega t}\},
$$

and likewise for $\mathbf{H}$, $\mathbf{D}$, $\mathbf{B}$, $\mathbf{J}$ and $\rho$. A phasor depends on position only; all of the time dependence sits in $e^{j\omega t}$. Because $\omega$ is real, a time derivative passes through $\text{Re}$ and acts on $e^{j\omega t}$ alone:

$$
\begin{gathered}
\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\text{Re}\{\tilde{\mathbf{E}}e^{j\omega t}\} = \text{Re}\{j\omega\tilde{\mathbf{E}}e^{j\omega t}\}\\[4pt]
\Longrightarrow\qquad\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\ \to\ j\omega .
\end{gathered}
$$

Space derivatives do not touch $e^{j\omega t}$, so $\nabla\cdot$ and $\nabla\times$ act on the phasors unchanged. Write each law for the real fields and gather everything under one $\text{Re}\{\cdots e^{j\omega t}\}$. An expression $\text{Re}\{\tilde Ae^{j\omega t}\}$ that vanishes at every instant has $\tilde A = 0$ (look at $\omega t = 0$ and $\omega t = \pi/2$). So the phasors themselves obey:

> [!key] Maxwell's equations in phasor form
> | law | time domain | phasor domain |
> |---|---|---|
> | Gauss | $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{D} = \rho$ | $\nabla\cdot\tilde{\mathbf{D}} = \tilde\rho$ |
> | no magnetic charge | $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B} = 0$ | $\nabla\cdot\tilde{\mathbf{B}} = 0$ |
> | Faraday | $\nabla\times\mathbf{E} = -\partial\mathbf{B}/\partial t$ | $\nabla\times\tilde{\mathbf{E}} = -j\omega\tilde{\mathbf{B}}$ |
> | Ampère–Maxwell | $\nabla\times\mathbf{H} = \mathbf{J} + \partial\mathbf{D}/\partial t$ | $\nabla\times\tilde{\mathbf{H}} = \tilde{\mathbf{J}} + j\omega\tilde{\mathbf{D}}$ |
> | continuity | $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{J} + \partial\rho/\partial t = 0$ | $\nabla\cdot\tilde{\mathbf{J}} + j\omega\tilde\rho = 0$ |
>
> Implied in the phasor column (the notes' words): $\tilde{\mathbf{D}} = \epsilon\tilde{\mathbf{E}}$, $\tilde{\mathbf{B}} = \mu\tilde{\mathbf{H}}$, $\tilde{\mathbf{J}} = \sigma\tilde{\mathbf{E}}$, where $\epsilon$, $\mu$ and $\sigma$ are the values at this frequency. The notes list the first four rows in their margin; the continuity row is [[3-maxwell-and-waves/16-charge-conservation-displacement-current-and-maxwells-equations|Lecture 16]]'s, converted the same way.

**What the phasor form implies.**

- **Material constants are numbers at each frequency.** Strictly, $\epsilon$, $\mu$ and $\sigma$ all depend on $\omega$, as the notes point out. [[1-electrostatics/11-lorentz-drude-models-for-conductivity-and-susceptibility|Lecture 11]] derived the Drude $\sigma(\omega)$ and noted that an AC susceptibility follows the same way. In the time domain such a medium turns $\mathbf{D} = \epsilon\mathbf{E}$ into a convolution: the response at one instant depends on the field's history. At one frequency it is a multiplication by a number, possibly complex. That is one reason phasors are the natural language for real materials (§8).
- **One frequency at a time, in linear media.** The method needs a linear, time-invariant medium, in which a cosine in gives a cosine out at the same frequency. Any other waveform is a sum of frequencies (Fourier, ECE 210), each solved separately with its own phasor. This is the superposition argument of [[3-maxwell-and-waves/18-the-wave-equation-and-plane-tem-waves|Lecture 18]] §4.
- **The divergence laws come for free.** The divergence of a curl is zero. Applied to phasor Faraday, $0 = -j\omega\nabla\cdot\tilde{\mathbf{B}}$, so $\nabla\cdot\tilde{\mathbf{B}} = 0$ whenever $\omega\neq0$. Applied to phasor Ampère, with continuity: $0 = \nabla\cdot\tilde{\mathbf{J}} + j\omega\nabla\cdot\tilde{\mathbf{D}} = j\omega(\nabla\cdot\tilde{\mathbf{D}} - \tilde\rho)$, so $\nabla\cdot\tilde{\mathbf{D}} = \tilde\rho$. At any nonzero frequency the two curl laws, plus charge conservation, already contain the two divergence laws. Lecture 16 made the time-domain version of the first argument ($\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B}$ can only be constant in time); a phasor field has no constant part.
- **No oscillating charge inside a uniform conductor.** With $\tilde{\mathbf{J}} = \sigma\tilde{\mathbf{E}}$ and constant $\epsilon$, continuity and Gauss give $-j\omega\tilde\rho = \nabla\cdot\tilde{\mathbf{J}} = \sigma\nabla\cdot\tilde{\mathbf{E}} = (\sigma/\epsilon)\tilde\rho$. That is $(\sigma + j\omega\epsilon)\tilde\rho = 0$, so $\tilde\rho = 0$. It is the relaxation equation $\partial\rho/\partial t + (\sigma/\epsilon)\rho = 0$ of [[1-electrostatics/08-conductors-dielectrics-and-polarization#2-conductors-in-electrostatics|Lecture 8 §2]] with $\partial/\partial t\to j\omega$. A charge density in a uniform conductor relaxes away in the time $\epsilon/\sigma$ and cannot be kept oscillating. So the notes' $\tilde\rho = 0$ for conducting media in §3 is not an extra assumption. The combination $\sigma + j\omega\epsilon$ appears here for the first time; it runs the rest of the lecture.

Notation: the notes write $E_o$ for a complex amplitude and $\tau$ for the angle of $\eta$. This page writes $E_0$ and $\tau$. The $\tau$ here is not the relaxation time of Lecture 8 or the collision time of Lecture 11. This page always writes the relaxation time out as $\epsilon/\sigma$.

## 2. No conductivity: the Helmholtz equation and Lecture 18's waves

First the notes check the method on known ground. With $\tilde\rho = 0$, $\tilde{\mathbf{J}} = 0$ and constant $\mu$, $\epsilon$, the phasor laws read

$$
\begin{gathered}
\nabla\cdot\tilde{\mathbf{E}} = 0,\qquad \nabla\cdot\tilde{\mathbf{H}} = 0,\\[4pt]
\nabla\times\tilde{\mathbf{E}} = -j\omega\mu\tilde{\mathbf{H}},\qquad \nabla\times\tilde{\mathbf{H}} = j\omega\epsilon\tilde{\mathbf{E}} .
\end{gathered}
$$

Take the curl of Faraday's law as in Lecture 18, with $j\omega$ in place of $\partial/\partial t$: $\nabla\times(\nabla\times\tilde{\mathbf{E}}) = -j\omega\mu\,\nabla\times\tilde{\mathbf{H}} = -j\omega\mu\,(j\omega\epsilon\tilde{\mathbf{E}}) = \omega^2\mu\epsilon\,\tilde{\mathbf{E}}$. The left side is $\nabla(\nabla\cdot\tilde{\mathbf{E}}) - \nabla^2\tilde{\mathbf{E}} = -\nabla^2\tilde{\mathbf{E}}$. Hence:

> [!key] The phasor wave equation (Helmholtz equation)
> $$
> \nabla^2\tilde{\mathbf{E}} + \omega^2\mu\epsilon\,\tilde{\mathbf{E}} = 0 .
> $$
> This is Lecture 18's $\nabla^2\mathbf{E} = \mu\epsilon\,\partial^2\mathbf{E}/\partial t^2$ with $\partial^2/\partial t^2\to(j\omega)^2 = -\omega^2$. An equation in space and time has become one in space only. $\tilde{\mathbf{H}}$ obeys the same equation ([[concepts/wave-equation]]).

For an $x$-polarized field that varies only along $z$, $\tilde{\mathbf{E}} = \hat x\,\tilde E_x(z)$, it is an ordinary differential equation with constant coefficients:

$$
\frac{d^2\tilde E_x}{dz^2} + \omega^2\mu\epsilon\,\tilde E_x = 0 .
$$

The notes try $\tilde E_x = e^{-\gamma z}$ and $e^{\gamma z}$, with $\gamma$ to be found. Both give $(\gamma^2 + \omega^2\mu\epsilon)\tilde E_x = 0$, so $\gamma^2 = -\omega^2\mu\epsilon$. One root is

$$
\gamma = j\beta,\qquad \beta = \omega\sqrt{\mu\epsilon}\ \ [\text{rad/m}],
$$

and the other root, $-j\beta$, only swaps the two exponentials. The solutions and their time-domain forms are

$$
\begin{gathered}
\tilde E_x(z) = E_0e^{\mp j\beta z}\\[4pt]
\Longleftrightarrow\quad E_x(z,t) = \lvert E_0\rvert\cos(\omega t\mp\beta z + \angle E_0),
\end{gathered}
$$

the cosine waves of Lectures 19 and 21, travelling toward $\pm z$ at $\omega/\beta = 1/\sqrt{\mu\epsilon}$.

**H from phasor Faraday.** $\nabla\times(\hat x\,\tilde E_x(z)) = \hat y\,d\tilde E_x/dz$, so Faraday's law reads $d\tilde E_x/dz = -j\omega\mu\tilde H_y$. With $d\tilde E_x/dz = \mp j\beta\tilde E_x$:

$$
\begin{gathered}
\tilde H_y = -\frac{1}{j\omega\mu}\frac{d\tilde E_x}{dz} = \pm\frac{\beta}{\omega\mu}\tilde E_x = \pm\frac{\tilde E_x}{\eta},\\[6pt]
\eta = \frac{\omega\mu}{\beta} = \sqrt{\frac\mu\epsilon} .
\end{gathered}
$$

This is [[3-maxwell-and-waves/18-the-wave-equation-and-plane-tem-waves#5-h-from-e-the-intrinsic-impedance|Lecture 18 §5]] again. The time integration there has become a division by $j\omega$, and the static constant it had to drop never appears: a field at frequency $\omega$ has no static part.

> [!example] Example — H from E by the phasor curl
> In a lossless, non-magnetic dielectric with $\epsilon_r = 4$, $\mathbf{E} = \hat y\,2\cos(2\pi\times10^8t + \tfrac{4\pi}{3}x)$ V/m. Find $\mathbf{H}$.
>
> The phasor is $\tilde{\mathbf{E}} = \hat y\,2e^{j\beta x}$ with $\beta = 4\pi/3$ rad/m. That agrees with $\omega\sqrt{\mu_0\epsilon} = 2\pi\times10^8\times2/(3\times10^8) = 4\pi/3$. The $+$ between $\omega t$ and $\beta x$ says the wave travels toward $-x$. The curl of $\hat y\,\tilde E_y(x)$ is $\hat z\,d\tilde E_y/dx = \hat z\,j\beta\tilde E_y$, and Faraday's law sets this equal to $-j\omega\mu_0\tilde{\mathbf{H}}$:
> $$
> \begin{gathered}
> \tilde{\mathbf{H}} = -\hat z\,\frac{\beta}{\omega\mu_0}\,2e^{j\beta x} = -\hat z\,\frac{2}{\eta}\,e^{j\beta x},\\[6pt]
> \eta = \frac{\eta_0}{2} = 60\pi\ \Omega,
> \end{gathered}
> $$
> so $\mathbf{H} = -\hat z\,10.6\cos(2\pi\times10^8t + \tfrac{4\pi}{3}x)$ mA/m. Two checks. The rotation rule gives $\hat u\times\tilde{\mathbf{E}}/\eta = (-\hat x)\times\hat y\,(2/\eta) = -\hat z\,(2/\eta)$ ✓. And $\tilde{\mathbf{E}}\times\tilde{\mathbf{H}}^*$ points along $\hat y\times(-\hat z) = -\hat x$, the direction of travel ✓.

The notes sum up: the phasor route has reproduced the plane TEM waves of Lectures 18–21 with algebra instead of calculus. The real payoff is the next step, a conducting medium, which the time domain handles badly.

## 3. Conducting media: one replacement

Now let the medium conduct, $\tilde{\mathbf{J}} = \sigma\tilde{\mathbf{E}}$, with $\tilde\rho = 0$ (automatic, §1). Only Ampère's law changes:

$$
\nabla\times\tilde{\mathbf{H}} = \sigma\tilde{\mathbf{E}} + j\omega\epsilon\tilde{\mathbf{E}} = (\sigma + j\omega\epsilon)\,\tilde{\mathbf{E}} .
$$

The set is identical to the lossless one except that $j\omega\epsilon$ has become $\sigma + j\omega\epsilon$. Every result of §2 therefore carries over with that one replacement. The notes apply it to $\gamma$ and $\eta$:

$$
\begin{gathered}
\gamma^2 = -\omega^2\mu\epsilon = (j\omega\mu)(j\omega\epsilon)\\[4pt]
\longrightarrow\quad \gamma^2 = (j\omega\mu)(\sigma + j\omega\epsilon),\\[8pt]
\eta = \sqrt{\frac{j\omega\mu}{j\omega\epsilon}}\quad\longrightarrow\quad \eta = \sqrt{\frac{j\omega\mu}{\sigma + j\omega\epsilon}} .
\end{gathered}
$$

> [!key] Propagation constant and intrinsic impedance of a conducting medium
> $$
> \begin{gathered}
> \gamma = \sqrt{j\omega\mu(\sigma + j\omega\epsilon)} = \alpha + j\beta\ \ [\text{m}^{-1}],\\[6pt]
> \eta = \sqrt{\frac{j\omega\mu}{\sigma + j\omega\epsilon}} = \lvert\eta\rvert e^{j\tau}\ \ [\Omega],\\[6pt]
> \gamma\eta = j\omega\mu,\qquad \frac{\gamma}{\eta} = \sigma + j\omega\epsilon .
> \end{gathered}
> $$
> Take both square roots with a positive real part (for $\sigma = 0$, the root $\gamma = +j\beta$). Then $\alpha\ge0$, $\beta>0$ and $0\le\tau<45^\circ$ (§5), with $\alpha = \tau = 0$ only when $\sigma = 0$. $\alpha$ is the **attenuation constant**, in nepers per metre (Np/m), and $\beta$ the **phase constant**, in radians per metre. Both are 1/m; the unit names only say which is which. With $\sigma = 0$ they reduce to the lossless $\gamma = j\omega\sqrt{\mu\epsilon}$ of §2 and Lecture 18's $\eta = \sqrt{\mu/\epsilon}$. See [[concepts/propagation-constant]] and [[concepts/intrinsic-impedance]].

The two product rules follow from multiplying and dividing the square roots, and exam stems print them. Read backwards, they give the medium from a measured $\gamma$ and $\eta$ (the notes' margin, for real $\sigma$ and $\epsilon$):

$$
\mu = \frac{\gamma\eta}{j\omega},\qquad \sigma = \text{Re}\Big\{\frac\gamma\eta\Big\},\qquad \epsilon = \frac1\omega\,\text{Im}\Big\{\frac\gamma\eta\Big\} .
$$

**Two ways to read σ + jωε.**

- *Complex conductivity.* $\sigma\tilde{\mathbf{E}}$ is the conduction current density and $j\omega\epsilon\tilde{\mathbf{E}}$ the displacement current density of [[3-maxwell-and-waves/16-charge-conservation-displacement-current-and-maxwells-equations#2-the-asymmetry-and-maxwells-displacement-current|Lecture 16]], 90° ahead of the field. Their sum is the total current density per unit field. The circuit version is the leaky capacitor of [[1-electrostatics/10-capacitance-and-conductance#6-conductance-the-leaky-capacitor|Lecture 10]]: $I = GV + C\,dV/dt$ becomes $\tilde I = (G + j\omega C)\tilde V$, and with one homogeneous filling $G/C = \sigma/\epsilon$. Its admittance is $\sigma + j\omega\epsilon$ times a geometric factor.
- *Complex permittivity.* Factor out $j\omega$: $\sigma + j\omega\epsilon = j\omega(\epsilon - j\sigma/\omega)$. The conductor then looks like a dielectric with a complex permittivity,
$$
\epsilon_c = \epsilon - j\frac{\sigma}{\omega},\qquad \gamma = j\omega\sqrt{\mu\epsilon_c},\qquad \eta = \sqrt{\frac{\mu}{\epsilon_c}},
$$
which are $j\beta = j\omega\sqrt{\mu\epsilon}$ and $\eta = \sqrt{\mu/\epsilon}$ with $\epsilon\to\epsilon_c$. The negative imaginary part of $\epsilon_c$ stands for the current in phase with $\tilde{\mathbf{E}}$, which is the one that dissipates energy.

The two views are the same number up to the factor $j\omega$. The notes use the first; many textbooks use the second.

**One ratio decides.** The conduction current is $\sigma/\omega\epsilon$ times the displacement current. Lecture 23 calls this ratio the **loss tangent** ([[concepts/loss-tangent]]) and works out its two limits. Earlier lectures already met it in other clothes:

- In Lecture 8's terms, $\sigma/\omega\epsilon = (1/\omega)/(\epsilon/\sigma)$ compares the time the field takes to turn through one radian with the relaxation time $\epsilon/\sigma$. If the free charges relax much faster than the field reverses ($\sigma\gg\omega\epsilon$), they follow it and carry a conduction current far larger than the displacement current: the medium acts as a conductor. If the field reverses much faster ($\sigma\ll\omega\epsilon$), the free charges hardly respond and the medium acts as a dielectric with a little loss.
- Lecture 10 said it about the leaky capacitor: resistor-like for $\omega\ll\sigma/\epsilon$, capacitor-like for $\omega\gg\sigma/\epsilon$.
- The crossover $\sigma = \omega\epsilon$ sits at $f = \sigma/2\pi\epsilon$. The lossy medium of Lecture 10's example ($\epsilon = 2\epsilon_0$, $\sigma = 2\ \mu$S/m, $\epsilon/\sigma = 8.85\ \mu$s) crosses over near 18 kHz. Sea water ($\epsilon\approx81\epsilon_0$, $\sigma\approx4$ S/m, $\epsilon/\sigma\approx0.18$ ns, which Lecture 8 rounds to 0.2 ns) crosses over near 0.9 GHz. "Conductor" describes a material *at a frequency*, not a material.

**The time-domain view, and why the notes switch to phasors.** Take the curl of Faraday's law with $\mathbf{J} = \sigma\mathbf{E}$ kept in Ampère's law. In a uniform medium, where $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{E} = 0$, the result is

$$
\nabla^2\mathbf{E} = \mu\sigma\frac{\partial\mathbf{E}}{\partial t} + \mu\epsilon\frac{\partial^2\mathbf{E}}{\partial t^2} .
$$

The first term on the right is the one [[3-maxwell-and-waves/18-the-wave-equation-and-plane-tem-waves#2-the-wave-equation|Lecture 18 §2]] said a conductor would add. It is a damping term, and $f(t\mp z/v)$ no longer solves the equation: waves decay and change shape as they go. When $\sigma\gg\omega\epsilon$ the second term is negligible and the equation becomes a diffusion equation, like the heat equation. Fields then soak into a good conductor rather than propagate. With $\partial/\partial t\to j\omega$ all of this becomes $\nabla^2\tilde{\mathbf{E}} = (j\omega\mu\sigma - \omega^2\mu\epsilon)\tilde{\mathbf{E}} = \gamma^2\tilde{\mathbf{E}}$: one complex number. This is the "new domain" the notes say phasors open up.

## 4. The damped plane wave

With $\gamma$ and $\eta$ in hand, the plane wave of §2 carries over in the same form (the notes' p. 5):

> [!key] An x-polarized plane wave in a conducting medium
> $$
> \begin{gathered}
> \tilde{\mathbf{E}} = \hat x\,E_0e^{\mp\gamma z},\qquad \tilde{\mathbf{H}} = \pm\hat y\,\frac{E_0}{\eta}\,e^{\mp\gamma z},\\[6pt]
> \mathbf{E} = \hat x\,\lvert E_0\rvert\,e^{\mp\alpha z}\cos(\omega t\mp\beta z + \angle E_0),\\[4pt]
> \mathbf{H} = \pm\hat y\,\frac{\lvert E_0\rvert}{\lvert\eta\rvert}\,e^{\mp\alpha z}\cos(\omega t\mp\beta z + \angle E_0 - \tau).
> \end{gathered}
> $$
> $E_0$ is any complex constant, the complex amplitude. Read the upper signs together (travel toward $+z$, and $e^{-\alpha z}$ shrinks as $z$ grows) and the lower signs together (travel toward $-z$, and $e^{+\alpha z}$ shrinks as $z$ decreases). Either way the wave **decays in the direction it travels**. The real fields are $\text{Re}\{\tilde{\mathbf{E}}e^{j\omega t}\}$ and $\text{Re}\{\tilde{\mathbf{H}}e^{j\omega t}\}$, with $E_0 = \lvert E_0\rvert e^{j\angle E_0}$ and $1/\eta = e^{-j\tau}/\lvert\eta\rvert$.

Each of the three new numbers has one job. The notes' margin lists the first two.

- **β sets the phase.** The wavelength is $\lambda = 2\pi/\beta$ and the phase velocity $v_p = \omega/\beta = \lambda f$, now with $\beta = \text{Im}\{\sqrt{j\omega\mu(\sigma + j\omega\epsilon)}\}$. In $v_p = \omega/\beta$ and in $\lambda = v_p/f$ the frequency now enters both the numerator and the denominator, so neither is simply proportional to $1/f$ or constant. $v_p$ is the speed of the **nodes**, the zero crossings, which the decaying factor cannot move; $\lambda$ is twice the node spacing. Because $v_p$ depends on frequency, the frequencies inside a pulse travel at different speeds and the pulse spreads (dispersion, [[concepts/dispersion-relation]]). The speed of a narrow-band packet, the group velocity $v_g$, is a different quantity. The notes leave it to ECE 450; Lecture 24 takes up signal transmission.
- **α sets the decay.** Along the travel the amplitude falls as $e^{-\alpha z}$. The **penetration depth**
$$
\delta\equiv\frac1\alpha = \frac{1}{\text{Re}\{\sqrt{j\omega\mu(\sigma + j\omega\epsilon)}\}}
$$
is the distance over which the amplitude falls by a factor $e$, to 37 %. When it is very small it is called the **skin depth** ([[concepts/skin-depth]], Lecture 23). Power goes as the square of the amplitude, so it falls as $e^{-2\alpha z}$, to 13.5 % at $z = \delta$; Lecture 23 does that bookkeeping. One neper is an amplitude ratio of $e$, or $20\log_{10}e = 8.686$ dB.
- **τ sets the timing between E and H.** At every point $\mathbf{H}$ reaches each maximum $\tau/\omega$ after $\mathbf{E}$, and the ratio of amplitudes is still $\lvert\eta\rvert$. In a snapshot the $\mathbf{H}$ pattern sits $\tau/\beta$ behind the $\mathbf{E}$ pattern, on the source side.

**E, H and the direction of travel.** The pairing rule of Lecture 18 is unchanged, with $\eta$ now complex:

$$
\tilde{\mathbf{H}} = \frac1\eta\,\hat u\times\tilde{\mathbf{E}},\qquad \tilde{\mathbf{E}} = \eta\,\tilde{\mathbf{H}}\times\hat u .
$$

Dividing by $\eta$ divides the amplitude by $\lvert\eta\rvert$ *and* subtracts $\tau$ from the phase. Lecture 21's time-average Poynting vector, $\langle\mathbf{S}\rangle = \tfrac12\text{Re}\{\tilde{\mathbf{E}}\times\tilde{\mathbf{H}}^*\}$, still points along $\hat u$. For the $+z$ wave it is $\hat z\,(\lvert E_0\rvert^2/2\lvert\eta\rvert)\cos\tau\,e^{-2\alpha z}$, positive because $\tau<90^\circ$. Lecture 23 follows that power as it turns into heat: the $\mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{E}>0$ term of Poynting's theorem ([[concepts/poynting-theorem]]), the Joule heating of Lecture 10 now spread through the medium.

> [!trap] Sign and phase traps with damped waves
> - **Which exponential.** $e^{-\gamma z} = e^{-\alpha z}e^{-j\beta z}$ goes with travel toward $+z$; $e^{+\gamma z}$ with travel toward $-z$. A field written as $e^{-\alpha z}\cos(\omega t + \beta z)$ would grow in the direction it travels. No passive medium does that.
> - **The phase of H.** $\tilde{\mathbf{H}} = \tilde{\mathbf{E}}/\eta$ subtracts $\tau$; $\tilde{\mathbf{E}} = \eta\tilde{\mathbf{H}}$ adds it. With real $\mu$, $\epsilon$ and $\sigma$, as in every conducting medium of this course, $\mathbf{H}$ lags $\mathbf{E}$, never the reverse.
> - **β is never smaller than α.** For a real $\sigma$ the angle of $\gamma$ lies between 45° and 90° (§5), so $\beta\ge\alpha$, with equality only in the good-conductor limit. The one exception on this page has an imaginary $\sigma$: the plasma of §8, which below its plasma frequency has $\beta = 0$.
> - **Nepers and radians.** $\alpha$ multiplies distance inside an exponential, $\beta$ inside a cosine. They share the unit 1/m but are not interchangeable.
> - **Degrees and radians in one argument.** Exams write phases such as $\cos(\omega t - 4x + 60^\circ)$: $\beta x$ is in radians and the constant in degrees. Convert before you add $\tau$.

## 5. The general formulas, and how to decode a damped wave

The notes leave $\alpha$ and $\beta$ as the real and imaginary parts of a square root, which is how a calculator finds them. Closed forms follow from squaring $\gamma = \alpha + j\beta$ (the route of Rao §5.3):

$$
\begin{gathered}
(\alpha + j\beta)^2 = \alpha^2 - \beta^2 + 2j\alpha\beta = -\omega^2\mu\epsilon + j\omega\mu\sigma\\[4pt]
\Longrightarrow\quad \beta^2 - \alpha^2 = \omega^2\mu\epsilon,\qquad 2\alpha\beta = \omega\mu\sigma .
\end{gathered}
$$

A third equation comes from the magnitude: $\alpha^2 + \beta^2 = \lvert\gamma\rvert^2 = \lvert j\omega\mu\rvert\,\lvert\sigma + j\omega\epsilon\rvert = \omega^2\mu\epsilon\sqrt{1 + (\sigma/\omega\epsilon)^2}$. Adding and subtracting:

> [!key] The general formulas (real, positive σ, ε, μ)
> $$
> \begin{gathered}
> \alpha = \omega\sqrt{\frac{\mu\epsilon}{2}}\left[\sqrt{1 + \Big(\frac{\sigma}{\omega\epsilon}\Big)^2} - 1\right]^{1/2},\\[8pt]
> \beta = \omega\sqrt{\frac{\mu\epsilon}{2}}\left[\sqrt{1 + \Big(\frac{\sigma}{\omega\epsilon}\Big)^2} + 1\right]^{1/2},\\[8pt]
> \lvert\eta\rvert = \frac{\sqrt{\mu/\epsilon}}{\big[1 + (\sigma/\omega\epsilon)^2\big]^{1/4}},\\[8pt]
> \tau = \frac12\tan^{-1}\frac{\sigma}{\omega\epsilon},\qquad \angle\gamma = 90^\circ - \tau,\\[8pt]
> \frac\alpha\beta = \tan\tau,\qquad \frac{\sigma}{\omega\epsilon} = \tan2\tau = \frac{2\alpha\beta}{\beta^2 - \alpha^2} .
> \end{gathered}
> $$
> Also $\lvert\gamma\rvert\lvert\eta\rvert = \omega\mu$, and $0\le\tau<45^\circ$. The notes write these as $\text{Re}$, $\text{Im}$, magnitude and angle of the two square roots; the numbers are the same.

**Where the angles come from.** $j\omega\mu$ points at 90°. $\sigma + j\omega\epsilon$ points at $90^\circ - \tan^{-1}(\sigma/\omega\epsilon)$: it leans from the imaginary axis toward the real axis as $\sigma$ grows. $\eta$ is the square root of their ratio, so its angle is half the difference, $\tau = \tfrac12\tan^{-1}(\sigma/\omega\epsilon)$. $\gamma$ is the square root of their product, so its angle is half the sum, $90^\circ - \tau$. The two angles add to 90° because $\gamma\eta = j\omega\mu$. Finally, $\alpha = \lvert\gamma\rvert\cos\angle\gamma$ and $\beta = \lvert\gamma\rvert\sin\angle\gamma$, so $\alpha/\beta = \cot(90^\circ - \tau) = \tan\tau$.

So apart from the scales $\omega\sqrt{\mu\epsilon}$ and $\sqrt{\mu/\epsilon}$, every property of the wave depends on the single number $\sigma/\omega\epsilon$:

> [!recipe] Decoding a damped wave (the exam skill)
> 1. **Read off.** The direction comes from the signs of the $t$ and position terms: opposite signs mean travel toward $+$ that axis, the same signs toward $-$. $\omega$ is the coefficient of $t$, $\beta$ of position in the cosine, and $\alpha$ of position in the exponential, which must decay along the travel. Then $\gamma = \alpha + j\beta$.
> 2. **η** $= j\omega\mu/\gamma$, with the medium's $\mu$. In polar form, $\lvert\eta\rvert = \omega\mu/\lvert\gamma\rvert$ and $\tau = 90^\circ - \angle\gamma = \tan^{-1}(\alpha/\beta)$.
> 3. **The other field.** $\tilde{\mathbf{H}} = \hat u\times\tilde{\mathbf{E}}/\eta$ (divide by $\lvert\eta\rvert$, subtract $\tau$) or $\tilde{\mathbf{E}} = \eta\,\tilde{\mathbf{H}}\times\hat u$ (multiply by $\lvert\eta\rvert$, add $\tau$). Do the cross product; do not guess the direction.
> 4. **The medium.** $\gamma/\eta = \gamma^2/j\omega\mu = \sigma + j\omega\epsilon$, so $\sigma = 2\alpha\beta/\omega\mu$ and $\epsilon = (\beta^2 - \alpha^2)/\omega^2\mu$.
> 5. **The regime.** $\sigma/\omega\epsilon = 2\alpha\beta/(\beta^2 - \alpha^2) = \tan2\tau$ (§6).
> 6. **Sanity checks.** $\beta\ge\alpha$; $0\le\tau<45^\circ$; $\lvert\gamma\rvert\lvert\eta\rvert = \omega\mu$; $\epsilon\ge\epsilon_0$ for ordinary matter.

> [!example] Example — decode $\mathbf{E} = \hat y\,6e^{-x}\cos(3\times10^8t - 3x)$ V/m in a non-magnetic medium
> Find $\gamma$, $\eta$, $\mathbf{H}$, the medium's $\sigma$ and $\epsilon$, and $\delta$, $\lambda$, $v_p$ ($x$ in m, $t$ in s; use $\mu_0 = 4\pi\times10^{-7}$ H/m and $\epsilon_0 = 10^{-9}/36\pi$ F/m).
> - **Read off.** The $t$ and $x$ terms have opposite signs, so the wave travels toward $+x$, and $e^{-x}$ decays that way ✓. $\omega = 3\times10^8$ rad/s ($f = 47.7$ MHz), $\alpha = 1$ Np/m, $\beta = 3$ rad/m, $\gamma = 1 + j3 = \sqrt{10}\,e^{j71.6^\circ}$ m⁻¹.
> - **η.** $\omega\mu_0 = 3\times10^8\times4\pi\times10^{-7} = 120\pi$ Ω/m, so
> $$
> \begin{gathered}
> \eta = \frac{j120\pi}{1 + j3} = \frac{120\pi\,j(1 - j3)}{10} = 12\pi(3 + j)\\[6pt]
> = 113.1 + j37.7 = 119.2\,e^{j18.4^\circ}\ \Omega .
> \end{gathered}
> $$
> Check: $\tan^{-1}(\alpha/\beta) = \tan^{-1}(1/3) = 18.4^\circ$ ✓.
> - **H.** $\hat u = \hat x$, so $\tilde{\mathbf{H}} = \hat x\times\hat y\,(6/\eta)\,e^{-\gamma x} = \hat z\,(6/119.2)\,e^{-j18.4^\circ}e^{-\gamma x}$ A/m, and $\mathbf{H} = \hat z\,50.3\,e^{-x}\cos(3\times10^8t - 3x - 18.4^\circ)$ mA/m. Check: $\hat y\times\hat z = +\hat x$ ✓.
> - **The medium.** $\gamma^2 = (1 + j3)^2 = -8 + j6$, so $\gamma/\eta = \gamma^2/j\omega\mu_0 = (-8 + j6)/(j120\pi) = (6 + j8)/120\pi$. Hence $\sigma = 6/120\pi = 1/20\pi = 15.9$ mS/m, and $\omega\epsilon = 8/120\pi$ gives $\epsilon = 7.07\times10^{-11}$ F/m $= 8\epsilon_0$. The ratio $\sigma/\omega\epsilon = 6/8 = 3/4$: conduction and displacement currents are comparable.
> - **Lengths and speed.** $\delta = 1/\alpha = 1$ m, $\lambda = 2\pi/\beta = 2.09$ m, $v_p = \omega/\beta = 1.0\times10^8$ m/s. A lossless medium with $\epsilon_r = 8$ would carry the wave at $c/\sqrt8 = 1.06\times10^8$ m/s; the conduction current slows it further.
> - **Cross-check with the general formulas.** $\omega\sqrt{\mu\epsilon} = \sqrt8 = 2\sqrt2$ rad/m and $\sqrt{1 + (3/4)^2} = 5/4$, so $\alpha = 2\sqrt{5/4 - 1} = 1$ ✓ and $\beta = 2\sqrt{5/4 + 1} = 3$ ✓.

> [!trap] What μ does, and does not, change
> $\mu$ enters $\eta = j\omega\mu/\gamma$, $\sigma = 2\alpha\beta/\omega\mu$ and $\epsilon = (\beta^2 - \alpha^2)/\omega^2\mu$. If the medium is magnetic and you use $\mu_0$, all three come out wrong. The ratio $\sigma/\omega\epsilon = 2\alpha\beta/(\beta^2 - \alpha^2)$ does not contain $\mu$, so the regime verdict survives the mistake while the numbers do not. The [[problems/decoding-a-damped-wave|worked problem]] uses $\mu = 3\mu_0$ and shows the factor of 3 that $\mu_0$ would cost.

## 6. Four regimes at a glance

The notes now take four special cases. Here they are together, as an overview. [[3-maxwell-and-waves/23-imperfect-dielectrics-and-good-conductors|Lecture 23]] has the notes' full table, the loss tangent, numbers for real materials and the power each wave carries.

> [!key] The four regimes (overview)
> - **Perfect dielectric**, $\sigma = 0$: $\gamma = j\omega\sqrt{\mu\epsilon}$ and $\eta = \sqrt{\mu/\epsilon}$. No decay; $\mathbf{E}$ and $\mathbf{H}$ in phase ($\alpha = \tau = 0$).
> - **Imperfect dielectric** (the notes also say "lousy" conductor), $\sigma\ll\omega\epsilon$: $\gamma\approx\tfrac\sigma2\sqrt{\mu/\epsilon} + j\omega\sqrt{\mu\epsilon}$ and $\eta\approx\sqrt{\mu/\epsilon}\,(1 + j\sigma/2\omega\epsilon)$. The wave travels many wavelengths, and $\tau\approx\sigma/2\omega\epsilon$ rad.
> - **Good conductor**, $\sigma\gg\omega\epsilon$: $\gamma\approx(1 + j)\sqrt{\pi f\mu\sigma}$ and $\eta\approx\sqrt{\omega\mu/\sigma}\,e^{j\pi/4}$. Since $\alpha\approx\beta$, the wave is gone within a wavelength, and $\tau\approx45^\circ$.
> - **Perfect conductor**, $\sigma\to\infty$: $\alpha\to\infty$ and $\eta\to0$. No field gets inside.

> [!derivation]- Where the two shortcuts come from (the notes' route)
> Write $\sigma + j\omega\epsilon = j\omega\epsilon\,(1 - j\sigma/\omega\epsilon)$. Then
> $$
> \begin{gathered}
> \gamma = j\omega\sqrt{\mu\epsilon}\,\Big(1 - j\frac{\sigma}{\omega\epsilon}\Big)^{1/2},\\[6pt]
> \eta = \sqrt{\frac\mu\epsilon}\,\Big(1 - j\frac{\sigma}{\omega\epsilon}\Big)^{-1/2} .
> \end{gathered}
> $$
> **Imperfect dielectric**, $\sigma/\omega\epsilon\ll1$. Use $(1 + a)^n\approx1 + na$ for $\lvert a\rvert\ll1$, with $n = \tfrac12$ for $\gamma$ and $n = -\tfrac12$ for $\eta$:
> $$
> \begin{gathered}
> \gamma\approx j\omega\sqrt{\mu\epsilon}\Big(1 - j\frac{\sigma}{2\omega\epsilon}\Big) = \frac\sigma2\sqrt{\frac\mu\epsilon} + j\omega\sqrt{\mu\epsilon},\\[6pt]
> \eta\approx\sqrt{\frac\mu\epsilon}\Big(1 + j\frac{\sigma}{2\omega\epsilon}\Big)\approx\sqrt{\frac\mu\epsilon}\,e^{j\sigma/2\omega\epsilon} .
> \end{gathered}
> $$
> So $\alpha\approx(\sigma/2)\sqrt{\mu/\epsilon}$, $\beta\approx\omega\sqrt{\mu\epsilon}$, $\lvert\eta\rvert\approx\sqrt{\mu/\epsilon}$ and $\tau\approx\sigma/2\omega\epsilon$ in radians, with errors of order $(\sigma/\omega\epsilon)^2$. The notes write the last factor as $e^{j\tan^{-1}(\sigma/2\omega\epsilon)}$, which agrees to this order; the exact angle is $\tfrac12\tan^{-1}(\sigma/\omega\epsilon)$.
>
> **Good conductor**, $\sigma/\omega\epsilon\gg1$. Drop the 1 in $1 - j\sigma/\omega\epsilon$. Then $\gamma^2\approx j\omega\mu\sigma$ and $\eta^2\approx j\omega\mu/\sigma$, and with $\sqrt j = e^{j\pi/4} = (1 + j)/\sqrt2$:
> $$
> \begin{gathered}
> \gamma\approx(1 + j)\sqrt{\frac{\omega\mu\sigma}{2}} = (1 + j)\sqrt{\pi f\mu\sigma},\\[6pt]
> \eta\approx\sqrt{\frac{j\omega\mu}{\sigma}} = \sqrt{\frac{\omega\mu}{\sigma}}\,e^{j\pi/4} .
> \end{gathered}
> $$
> So $\alpha\approx\beta\approx\sqrt{\pi f\mu\sigma}$, and $\mathbf{E}$ and $\mathbf{H}$ are 45° apart.
>
> **Perfect conductor**, $\sigma\to\infty$. Then $\alpha\to\infty$ ($\delta\to0$) and $\lvert\eta\rvert\to0$. No field gets in, and any finite $\mathbf{H}$ at the surface would come with $\mathbf{E} = \eta\mathbf{H}\to0$. The notes promise a proper proof that $\tilde E_x\to0$ later, when waves reflect from conductors.

**Penetration depth against frequency** (the notes' last page). Fix the material and vary $\omega$:

- *High frequency* ($\sigma\ll\omega\epsilon$): $\delta\to\dfrac2\sigma\sqrt{\dfrac\epsilon\mu}$, the same at every frequency. It is small if $\sigma$ is large, and such a wave is strongly attenuated even though the medium counts as a "dielectric" there.
- *Low frequency* ($\sigma\gg\omega\epsilon$): $\delta\approx\sqrt{\dfrac{2}{\omega\mu\sigma}} = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{\pi f\mu\sigma}}$, which grows as the frequency falls. Hence the notes' remark that low frequencies are the ones that get through a lossy medium with large $\sigma$, such as sea water. Lecture 23 puts numbers on it, and on the bandwidth this costs.

**Reading the regime from a wave.** Three quick tests, all from §5:

- **β against α.** For a real $\sigma$, $\beta\ge\alpha$, with equality only in the good-conductor limit. A claim that $\beta$ is much smaller than $\alpha$ cannot hold in a conducting medium. (Only the plasma of §8, whose conductivity is imaginary, allows $\beta<\alpha$.)
- **Decay per wavelength.** Over one wavelength the amplitude is multiplied by $e^{-\alpha\lambda} = e^{-2\pi\tan\tau}$. A good conductor gives $e^{-2\pi}\approx0.19\,\%$. An imperfect dielectric gives $\approx1 - \pi\sigma/\omega\epsilon$, close to 1.
- **δ against λ.** The ratio is $\lambda/\delta = 2\pi\alpha/\beta = 2\pi\tan\tau$, so for a real $\sigma$ the wavelength is always shorter than $2\pi\delta$. In a good conductor $\lambda\approx2\pi\delta$, and $\delta$ is shorter than a wavelength. In an imperfect dielectric $\delta\gg\lambda$. A problem that says "$\delta$ is much longer than $\lambda$", or "$\lambda\approx2\pi\delta$", is telling you the regime. "$\lambda$ is longer than $\delta$" is not enough: it only needs $\sigma/\omega\epsilon>0.33$, which includes the in-between range.

> [!example] Example — a medium at the crossover, where neither shortcut works
> Take $\mu = \mu_0$, $\epsilon = 4\epsilon_0$, $\sigma = 0.04$ S/m at $f = 180$ MHz. With $\epsilon_0 = 10^{-9}/36\pi$, $\omega\epsilon = 2\pi(1.8\times10^8)(4\times10^{-9}/36\pi) = 0.04$ S/m, so $\sigma/\omega\epsilon = 1$ (0.9986 with the exact $\epsilon_0$).
> - **Exact.** $\omega\sqrt{\mu\epsilon} = 2.4\pi = 7.540$ rad/m and $\sqrt{1 + 1} = \sqrt2$, so $\alpha = (7.540/\sqrt2)\sqrt{\sqrt2 - 1} = 3.43$ Np/m and $\beta = (7.540/\sqrt2)\sqrt{\sqrt2 + 1} = 8.28$ rad/m. Also $\lvert\eta\rvert = 60\pi/2^{1/4} = 158.5\ \Omega$ and $\tau = \tfrac12\tan^{-1}1 = 22.5^\circ$, giving $\delta = 0.291$ m, $\lambda = 0.758$ m and $v_p = 1.37\times10^8$ m/s.
> - **Imperfect-dielectric shortcut.** $\alpha\approx(\sigma/2)\sqrt{\mu/\epsilon} = 0.02\times60\pi = 3.77$ (10 % high), $\beta\approx7.54$ (9 % low), $\tau\approx\sigma/2\omega\epsilon = 0.5$ rad $= 28.6^\circ$ (instead of 22.5°).
> - **Good-conductor shortcut.** $\alpha\approx\beta\approx\sqrt{\omega\mu\sigma/2} = 5.33$: 55 % high for $\alpha$, 36 % low for $\beta$, and $\tau = 45^\circ$.
>
> Near $\sigma = \omega\epsilon$, use the exact formulas or the calculator's square root. The shortcuts are for ratios well below or well above 1.

## 7. Lecture 19's current sheet in a conducting medium

The current sheet of [[3-maxwell-and-waves/19-dalembert-solutions-and-radiation-from-current-sheets|Lecture 19]] shows complex $\eta$ at work. Take the notes' Lecture 21 sheet, $\mathbf{J}_s = \hat x\,J_0\cos\omega t$ on $z = 0$ with phasor $\tilde{\mathbf{J}}_s = \hat x\,J_0$, and surround it with a conducting medium. The boundary-condition argument of [[3-maxwell-and-waves/19-dalembert-solutions-and-radiation-from-current-sheets#4-the-same-result-from-boundary-conditions-slides|Lecture 19 §4]] goes through in phasor form with $j\beta\to\gamma$:

1. Outgoing waves on both sides, with the same $\tilde{\mathbf{E}} = \hat x\,Ae^{-\gamma\lvert z\rvert}$ on each face (tangential $\mathbf{E}$ is continuous), and $\tilde{\mathbf{H}} = \pm\hat y\,(A/\eta)\,e^{-\gamma\lvert z\rvert}$ for $z\gtrless0$.
2. The jump condition, with region 1 at $z>0$ and $\hat n = \hat z$, gives $\hat z\times(\tilde{\mathbf{H}}^+ - \tilde{\mathbf{H}}^-) = \hat z\times\hat y\,(2A/\eta) = -\hat x\,(2A/\eta) = \hat x\,J_0$, so $A = -\eta J_0/2$.

$$
\begin{gathered}
\tilde{\mathbf{E}} = -\hat x\,\frac{\eta J_0}{2}\,e^{-\gamma\lvert z\rvert},\\[6pt]
\tilde{\mathbf{H}} = \mp\hat y\,\frac{J_0}{2}\,e^{-\gamma\lvert z\rvert}\qquad(z\gtrless0).
\end{gathered}
$$

This is Lecture 19's result, $\tilde{\mathbf{H}} = \tfrac12\tilde{\mathbf{J}}_s\times\hat n$ and $\tilde{\mathbf{E}} = -\tfrac\eta2\tilde{\mathbf{J}}_s$, with the delay $e^{-j\beta\lvert z\rvert}$ replaced by $e^{-\gamma\lvert z\rvert}$ and $\eta$ now complex.

> [!example] Example — the sheet in the medium of the §5 example
> The medium has $\epsilon = 8\epsilon_0$, $\sigma = 1/20\pi$ S/m and $\mu_0$, at $\omega = 3\times10^8$ rad/s, so $\gamma = 1 + j3$ m⁻¹ and $\eta = 119.2\,e^{j18.4^\circ}\ \Omega$. With $J_0 = 2$ A/m, the amplitude of the notes' Lecture 21 example:
> $$
> \begin{gathered}
> \mathbf{H} = \mp\hat y\,e^{-\lvert z\rvert}\cos(\omega t - 3\lvert z\rvert)\ \ \text{A/m},\\[4pt]
> \mathbf{E} = -\hat x\,119.2\,e^{-\lvert z\rvert}\cos(\omega t - 3\lvert z\rvert + 18.4^\circ)\ \ \text{V/m}
> \end{gathered}
> $$
> ($z$ in m). Three things differ from the lossless sheet.
> - Both waves fade with distance, here by a factor $e$ per metre.
> - $\mathbf{H}$ is still fixed by the current alone, $\tfrac12J_0 = 1$ A/m at the sheet. The medium decides $\mathbf{E}$, including its phase. $\mathbf{E}$ leads $\mathbf{H}$ by $\tau = 18.4^\circ$ everywhere, so at the sheet $\mathbf{E}$ is no longer exactly opposite to $\mathbf{J}_s$.
> - The power the current gives up, $\langle-\mathbf{J}_s\cdot\mathbf{E}\rangle = \tfrac12\text{Re}\{-\tilde{\mathbf{J}}_s\cdot\tilde{\mathbf{E}}^*\} = \tfrac14J_0^2\,\text{Re}\{\eta\} = 36\pi\approx113$ W/m², carries the factor $\cos\tau$, since $\text{Re}\{\eta\} = \lvert\eta\rvert\cos\tau$. Half of it, 56.5 W/m², enters each side and is turned into heat within a few metres. Lecture 23 follows it.
>
> Direction check for $z>0$: $\tilde{\mathbf{E}}\times\tilde{\mathbf{H}}^*\propto(-\hat x)\times(-\hat y) = +\hat z$, away from the sheet ✓.

## 8. When the conductivity depends on frequency

The notes open with a caveat: $\epsilon$, $\mu$ and $\sigma$ can all depend on $\omega$. The case that matters most here is the Drude conductivity of free carriers ([[1-electrostatics/11-lorentz-drude-models-for-conductivity-and-susceptibility#3-ac-conductivity-when-the-carriers-cannot-keep-up|Lecture 11 §3]]):

$$
\sigma(\omega) = \frac{Nq^2}{m(\nu + j\omega)} = \frac{\sigma_{\text{DC}}}{1 + j\omega/\nu} .
$$

In the phasor domain nothing changes: $\sigma(\omega) + j\omega\epsilon$ is still one complex number at each frequency, and $\gamma$ and $\eta$ are still its square-root combinations. What changes is the meaning of the margin relations $\sigma = \text{Re}\{\gamma/\eta\}$ and $\epsilon = \text{Im}\{\gamma/\eta\}/\omega$. With a complex $\sigma$ they return *effective* values, because the imaginary part of $\sigma(\omega)$ looks exactly like a change in $\epsilon$. At one frequency only the sum $\sigma + j\omega\epsilon$ (equivalently $\epsilon_c$) can be measured. See [[concepts/conductivity-and-susceptibility-models]].

**Metals.** For copper $\nu\approx4\times10^{13}$ s⁻¹, so $\sigma(\omega)$ equals its DC value to within $1.6\times10^{-4}$ at 1 GHz (Lecture 11). Real $\sigma$ is a safe assumption for metals throughout this course, and the good-conductor results of Lecture 23 use it.

**A collisionless plasma** ($\nu\to0$, as in the ionosphere). Now $\sigma = Nq^2/(j\omega m)$ is purely imaginary. The current lags the field by 90° (the carriers' inertia) and partly cancels the displacement current:

$$
\sigma + j\omega\epsilon_0 = j\omega\epsilon_0\Big(1 - \frac{\omega_p^2}{\omega^2}\Big),\qquad \omega_p^2 = \frac{Nq^2}{m\epsilon_0},
$$

so $\gamma^2 = j\omega\mu_0(\sigma + j\omega\epsilon_0) = -(\omega^2 - \omega_p^2)/c^2$.

- **Above the plasma frequency,** $\gamma = j\sqrt{\omega^2 - \omega_p^2}/c$. There is no attenuation, and $\beta = \tfrac1c\sqrt{\omega^2 - \omega_p^2}$ is exactly the dispersion relation of TEM waves in a collisionless plasma in [[3-maxwell-and-waves/21-monochromatic-waves-and-phasor-notation|Lecture 21]] ([[concepts/dispersion-relation]]). Lecture 21 derives it from the electrons' equation of motion; here it drops out of $\gamma$ in one line. $\eta = \omega\mu_0/\beta$ is real and larger than $\eta_0$.
- **Below it,** $\gamma = \sqrt{\omega_p^2 - \omega^2}/c$ is real. The field decays as $e^{-\alpha z}$ without oscillating in space (an *evanescent* field). $\eta$ is purely imaginary, so $\mathbf{E}$ and $\mathbf{H}$ are 90° apart and carry no average power. This decay is not loss: $\text{Re}\{\sigma\} = 0$, so there is no Joule heating. The plasma does not absorb the wave; it sends it back, and reflection is Lecture 25's subject.

Numbers for $N = 10^{12}$ electrons per m³, a typical daytime peak of the ionosphere: $f_p = \omega_p/2\pi\approx9$ MHz (the rule of thumb is $f_p\approx9\sqrt N$ Hz). A 5 MHz wave dies off with $\delta = 6.4$ m, while a 20 MHz wave passes with $\beta = 0.375$ rad/m instead of the 0.419 rad/m of vacuum.

## 9. Connections

- **Lecture 8: relaxation time.** $\sigma/\omega\epsilon = (1/\omega)/(\epsilon/\sigma)$ sets the regime, and the phasor form of the relaxation equation gives $\tilde\rho = 0$ inside a uniform conductor (§1).
- **Lecture 10: the leaky capacitor and Joule heating.** $G + j\omega C$ is $\sigma + j\omega\epsilon$ times geometry, and "resistor-like for $\omega\ll\sigma/\epsilon$" is the good-conductor regime. The power a decaying wave loses is Lecture 10's Joule heating, now spread through the medium.
- **Lecture 11: the Drude σ(ω).** It explains why metals have real $\sigma$ in this course, and in the collisionless limit it yields the plasma dispersion relation (§8).
- **Lecture 16: displacement current.** The two terms of $\sigma + j\omega\epsilon$ are the conduction and displacement currents. Their ratio decides everything.
- **Lecture 18: the wave equation.** Here are the $\mu\sigma\,\partial\mathbf{E}/\partial t$ term it promised, the Helmholtz form, and the reason waves in conductors "change shape" (§3).
- **Lecture 19: current sheets.** The same sheet, with $e^{-\gamma\lvert z\rvert}$ and complex $\eta$ (§7).
- **Lectures 20–21: Poynting and phasors.** $\langle\mathbf{S}\rangle = \tfrac12\text{Re}\{\tilde{\mathbf{E}}\times\tilde{\mathbf{H}}^*\}$ with complex $\eta$ brings in $\cos\tau$ and $e^{-2\alpha z}$ (Lecture 23).
- **Unit 4: transmission lines.** This connection is the site's own. A coax filled with the lossy medium has $\mathcal{L} = (\mu/2\pi)\ln(b/a)$, $\mathcal{C} = 2\pi\epsilon/\ln(b/a)$ and $\mathcal{G} = 2\pi\sigma/\ln(b/a)$ per unit length. With $\mathcal{L}\mathcal{C} = \mu\epsilon$ ([[2-magnetostatics/15-inductance-magnetic-energy-and-the-potentials|Lecture 15]]) and $\mathcal{G}/\mathcal{C} = \sigma/\epsilon$ (Lecture 10), the line's $\sqrt{j\omega\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{G} + j\omega\mathcal{C})}$ is exactly this lecture's $\gamma$, and $\sqrt{j\omega\mathcal{L}/(\mathcal{G} + j\omega\mathcal{C})} = \eta\ln(b/a)/2\pi$. A plane wave in a lossy medium and a line with a leaky filling are the same problem.

## 10. Summary

- One frequency: $\mathbf{X} = \text{Re}\{\tilde{\mathbf{X}}e^{j\omega t}\}$ and $\partial/\partial t\to j\omega$. The laws become $\nabla\cdot\tilde{\mathbf{D}} = \tilde\rho$, $\nabla\cdot\tilde{\mathbf{B}} = 0$, $\nabla\times\tilde{\mathbf{E}} = -j\omega\tilde{\mathbf{B}}$ and $\nabla\times\tilde{\mathbf{H}} = \tilde{\mathbf{J}} + j\omega\tilde{\mathbf{D}}$, with $\tilde{\mathbf{D}} = \epsilon\tilde{\mathbf{E}}$, $\tilde{\mathbf{B}} = \mu\tilde{\mathbf{H}}$, $\tilde{\mathbf{J}} = \sigma\tilde{\mathbf{E}}$ at that frequency. For $\omega\neq0$ the divergence laws follow from the curl laws, and $\tilde\rho = 0$ inside a uniform conductor.
- Lossless: $\nabla^2\tilde{\mathbf{E}} + \omega^2\mu\epsilon\tilde{\mathbf{E}} = 0$ (Helmholtz); $e^{\mp j\beta z}$ with $\beta = \omega\sqrt{\mu\epsilon}$; $\tilde H_y = \pm\tilde E_x/\eta$ with $\eta = \sqrt{\mu/\epsilon}$.
- Conducting: $j\omega\epsilon\to\sigma + j\omega\epsilon = j\omega\epsilon_c$, with $\epsilon_c = \epsilon - j\sigma/\omega$. Then $\gamma = \sqrt{j\omega\mu(\sigma + j\omega\epsilon)} = \alpha + j\beta$, $\eta = \sqrt{j\omega\mu/(\sigma + j\omega\epsilon)} = \lvert\eta\rvert e^{j\tau}$, $\gamma\eta = j\omega\mu$, $\gamma/\eta = \sigma + j\omega\epsilon$.
- Damped wave: $\mathbf{E} = \hat x\lvert E_0\rvert e^{\mp\alpha z}\cos(\omega t\mp\beta z + \angle E_0)$ and $\mathbf{H} = \pm\hat y(\lvert E_0\rvert/\lvert\eta\rvert)e^{\mp\alpha z}\cos(\omega t\mp\beta z + \angle E_0 - \tau)$. $\lambda = 2\pi/\beta$; $v_p = \omega/\beta$ (frequency dependent; the speed of the nodes); $\delta = 1/\alpha$; $\mathbf{H}$ lags $\mathbf{E}$ by $\tau$.
- General formulas for $\alpha$, $\beta$, $\lvert\eta\rvert$; $\tau = \tfrac12\tan^{-1}(\sigma/\omega\epsilon)$ between 0 and 45°; $\angle\gamma = 90^\circ - \tau$; $\alpha/\beta = \tan\tau$; $\beta\ge\alpha$.
- Regimes: $\sigma = 0$, no loss. $\sigma\ll\omega\epsilon$: $\alpha\approx(\sigma/2)\sqrt{\mu/\epsilon}$, many wavelengths. $\sigma\gg\omega\epsilon$: $\alpha\approx\beta\approx\sqrt{\pi f\mu\sigma}$, $\eta\approx\sqrt{\omega\mu/\sigma}\,e^{j\pi/4}$, under a wavelength. $\sigma\to\infty$: no field. At fixed $\sigma$, $\delta\to(2/\sigma)\sqrt{\epsilon/\mu}$ at high frequency and $1/\sqrt{\pi f\mu\sigma}$ at low.
- $\sigma/\omega\epsilon$ is conduction over displacement current, $1/\omega$ over the relaxation time $\epsilon/\sigma$; the crossover is at $f = \sigma/2\pi\epsilon$.
