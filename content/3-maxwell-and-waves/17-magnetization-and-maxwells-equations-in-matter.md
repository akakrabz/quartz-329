> [!abstract] In one breath
> Lecture 16's Maxwell equations are exact, but their $\rho$ and $\mathbf{J}$ count *every* charge — including the roughly $10^{30}$ electrons per cubic metre bound inside the atoms of a metal. Nobody can track those, so we average them. On average, bound charges do three things: their dipoles leave uncancelled charge $-\nabla\cdot\mathbf{P}$ (Lecture 8), their sloshing carries the polarization current $\partial\mathbf{P}/\partial t$ (Lecture 11), and — the new item — their *circulation*, electrons orbiting nuclei and spinning, makes tiny current loops whose moments add up to a **magnetization** $\mathbf{M}$, the magnetic dipole moment per unit volume. Inside a uniformly magnetized body neighbouring loops cancel; where $\mathbf{M}$ changes or ends, a **magnetization current** survives: $\nabla\times\mathbf{M}$ in the volume, $\mathbf{M}\times\hat n$ on a surface. Moving it to the left side of Ampère's law defines $\mathbf{H} = \mathbf{B}/\mu_0 - \mathbf{M}$, exactly as moving the bound charge into Gauss's law defined $\mathbf{D} = \epsilon_0\mathbf{E} + \mathbf{P}$, and Maxwell's equations come back in their old form with free sources only. For most materials $\mathbf{M} = \chi_m\mathbf{H}$ — with no $\mu_0$ — so $\mathbf{B} = \mu\mathbf{H}$, $\mu = \mu_0(1+\chi_m)$. Diamagnets ($\chi_m<0$) and paramagnets ($\chi_m>0$), both with $\lvert\chi_m\rvert$ around $10^{-5}$, are vacuum for most purposes; ferromagnets (iron, nickel, cobalt) have $\chi_m$ in the hundreds to hundreds of thousands, non-linear and with a memory — which is what a permanent magnet is.

## 1. Why matter needs new bookkeeping

Here are Lecture 16's equations with $\mathbf{D} = \epsilon_0\mathbf{E}$ and $\mathbf{B} = \mu_0\mathbf{H}$ written out:

$$
\begin{gathered}
\nabla\cdot(\epsilon_0\mathbf{E}) = \rho,\qquad \nabla\cdot\mathbf{B} = 0,\\[4pt]
\nabla\times\mathbf{E} = -\frac{\partial\mathbf{B}}{\partial t},\qquad \nabla\times\frac{\mathbf{B}}{\mu_0} = \mathbf{J}+\frac{\partial(\epsilon_0\mathbf{E})}{\partial t}.
\end{gathered}
$$

These are the **microscopic** equations. They are exact, provided $\rho$ and $\mathbf{J}$ include every charge and every current, down to the electrons inside each atom. In a material that is hopeless: a cubic millimetre of copper holds about $2.5\times10^{21}$ electrons, nearly all bound to their atoms. The way out is the one [[1-electrostatics/08-conductors-dielectrics-and-polarization|Lecture 8]] used for dielectrics. Average over volumes that contain many atoms, and split every source into the part you control (**free**) and the part the material supplies (**bound**):

> [!key] Sources in matter, averaged
> $$
> \rho = \rho_f - \nabla\cdot\mathbf{P},\qquad \mathbf{J} = \mathbf{J}_f + \frac{\partial\mathbf{P}}{\partial t} + \nabla\times\mathbf{M}.
> $$
> | term | name | what it is physically | first met |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | $\rho_f$, $\mathbf{J}_f$ | free charge and current | charges you place; carriers that drift ($\mathbf{J}_f = \sigma\mathbf{E}$ in a conductor) | Lectures 2, 8, 12 |
> | $-\nabla\cdot\mathbf{P}$ | bound charge density | dipole ends that fail to cancel where $\mathbf{P}$ changes | [[1-electrostatics/08-conductors-dielectrics-and-polarization\|Lecture 8]] |
> | $\partial\mathbf{P}/\partial t$ | polarization current | bound charges moving as their dipoles stretch and shrink | [[1-electrostatics/11-lorentz-drude-models-for-conductivity-and-susceptibility#5-polarization-current-bound-charges-that-carry-an-ac-current\|Lecture 11]] |
> | $\nabla\times\mathbf{M}$ | magnetization current | atomic current loops that fail to cancel where $\mathbf{M}$ changes | this lecture |

Every bound term is a derivative of one of two smooth, averaged vector fields: $\mathbf{P}$, the electric dipole moment per unit volume, and $\mathbf{M}$, the magnetic dipole moment per unit volume. That is the whole trick — the microscopic swarm of charges is replaced by two fields. The split respects charge conservation piece by piece: the free sources satisfy the continuity equation on their own, and so do the bound ones, because $\partial_t(-\nabla\cdot\mathbf{P}) + \nabla\cdot(\partial_t\mathbf{P}) = 0$ and $\nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf{M}) = 0$ identically.

The course notes substitute these expressions into Gauss's and Ampère's laws straight away and justify the $\nabla\times\mathbf{M}$ term afterwards. This page goes the other way round: first what $\mathbf{M}$ is (§2), then why its curl is a current (§3), then the bookkeeping (§4), because once the physics is clear the bookkeeping is one line.

An electron circling a nucleus is a current loop. Charge $-e$ passes any point of the orbit once per period, so the loop carries a current $I = e/T$, flowing **opposite** to the electron's velocity (the charge is negative). [[2-magnetostatics/13-current-sheets-solenoids-and-the-vector-potential#6-the-current-loop-and-the-magnetic-dipole|Lecture 13]] showed that from far away any small current loop looks like a magnetic dipole; its strength is the dipole moment.

> [!key] Magnetic dipole moment, and the torque a field exerts on it
> $$
> \mathbf{m} = I\mathbf{A} = IA\,\hat n\quad[\text{A}\cdot\text{m}^2],\qquad\qquad \mathbf{T} = \mathbf{m}\times\mathbf{B}\quad[\text{N}\cdot\text{m}],
> $$
> with $\hat n$ normal to the loop by the right-hand rule (fingers along $I$, thumb along $\hat n$). For a circle of radius $a$, $m = I\pi a^2$; the slides write $\mathbf{m} = I(\pi a^2)\hat a_z$ for a loop in the $xy$-plane. In a uniform $\mathbf{B}$ the net force on the loop is zero ($\oint I\,d\mathbf{l}\times\mathbf{B} = I(\oint d\mathbf{l})\times\mathbf{B} = 0$), but the torque is not: it has magnitude $mB\sin\theta$ and turns $\mathbf{m}$ toward $\mathbf{B}$, as a compass needle turns north. The electric twin is the torque $\mathbf{p}\times\mathbf{E}$ on an electric dipole.

The slide draws the torque as the two forces $I\,d\mathbf{l}\times\mathbf{B}_0$ on opposite sides of a tilted orbit: equal, opposite, and acting along different lines — a couple.

> [!derivation]- The torque on a square loop, side by side
> Take a square loop of side $\ell$ carrying $I$, with moment $\mathbf{m} = I\ell^2(0,\sin\theta,\cos\theta)$ tilted by $\theta$ from a uniform $\mathbf{B} = B\hat z$ about the $x$-axis. Two sides run along $\pm\hat x$, at positions $\mp\tfrac\ell2\hat u$ with $\hat u = (0,\cos\theta,-\sin\theta)$ (so that $\hat x\times\hat u = \mathbf{m}/m$ and the current circulates the right way). Their forces are
> $$
> \mathbf{F}_{+x} = I\ell\,\hat x\times B\hat z = -I\ell B\,\hat y,\qquad \mathbf{F}_{-x} = +I\ell B\,\hat y .
> $$
> The other two sides run along $\pm\hat u$; their forces $\pm I\ell B\cos\theta\,\hat x$ lie on the same line through the centre and cancel without a torque. Net force: zero. Torque about the centre:
> $$
> \Big(-\tfrac\ell2\hat u\Big)\times(-I\ell B\hat y) + \Big(\tfrac\ell2\hat u\Big)\times(I\ell B\hat y) = I\ell^2B\,(\hat u\times\hat y) = I\ell^2B\sin\theta\,\hat x = \mathbf{m}\times\mathbf{B}.
> $$
> Any flat loop can be tiled with small squares (shared edges cancel, as in §3), so $\mathbf{T} = \mathbf{m}\times\mathbf{B}$ holds for every planar loop in a uniform field.

**Where atomic loops come from.** The notes list three sources of bound, circulating current. (1) Electrons **orbiting** nuclei — the orbital moment, the slides' **L**. (2) The intrinsic **spin** of the electron, the slides' **S**: every electron behaves as if it were a tiny current loop with a fixed, quantized moment, although no classical picture of a spinning point charge works (the notes say so explicitly). (3) Charge motion **inside protons and neutrons**, which is far weaker. In filled shells, and in most chemical bonds, electrons pair up with opposite moments that cancel; only atoms or ions with unpaired electrons keep a net permanent moment.

> [!example] Example (this site's numbers) — the size of an atomic moment
> In the Bohr model of hydrogen the electron circles at radius $a_0 = 5.29\times10^{-11}$ m with speed $v = 2.19\times10^{6}$ m/s. The loop current is $I = ev/(2\pi a_0) \approx 1.06$ mA — a respectable current, through an area of about $10^{-20}$ m². The moment is $m = I\pi a_0^2 = eva_0/2 \approx 9.28\times10^{-24}$ A·m², which is, to the rounding of $a_0$ and $v$, the **Bohr magneton** $\mu_B = 9.27\times10^{-24}$ A·m² — the natural unit of atomic moments, and also the size of the electron's spin moment. Direction: for the electron moving one way round, $I$ runs the other way, and $\mathbf{m}$ is antiparallel to the electron's angular momentum.

**From moments to magnetization.** With no field, the moments in a sample point every which way — thermal motion scatters them — and their sum is zero (slide 3 of the deck). A field torques them toward alignment (slide 5); the net moment per unit volume is the magnetization:

> [!key] Magnetization
> $$
> \mathbf{M} = N\mathbf{m}\qquad\Big[\frac{1}{\text{m}^3}\cdot\text{A}\cdot\text{m}^2 = \text{A/m}\Big],
> $$
> with $N$ the number of atoms (or molecules) per unit volume and $\mathbf{m}$ the *average* moment per atom. The unit is that of $\mathbf{H}$ — the ink on the slide reads "same H" — and also that of a surface current density $\mathbf{J}_s$, which is no coincidence (§3). Its electric twin $\mathbf{P} = N\mathbf{p}$ [C/m²] has the unit of a surface *charge* density, for the same reason.

For a feel of the largest possible value: iron has $N\approx8.5\times10^{28}$ atoms/m³ with about $2.2\,\mu_B$ each when all are aligned, so $M\approx1.7\times10^{6}$ A/m and $\mu_0M\approx2.2$ T. That is why iron cores saturate near 2 T: there is nothing left to align.

## 3. The magnetization current

**Why a curl? (the notes' argument).** Bound charges obey charge conservation just as free ones do: $\partial\rho_b/\partial t + \nabla\cdot\mathbf{J}_b = 0$. Insert $\rho_b = -\nabla\cdot\mathbf{P}$:

$$
\nabla\cdot\Big(\mathbf{J}_b - \frac{\partial\mathbf{P}}{\partial t}\Big) = 0 .
$$

So whatever bound current exists *beyond* the polarization current is divergence-free — and a divergence-free field can always be written as the curl of another field (the theorem behind $\mathbf{B} = \nabla\times\mathbf{A}$ in [[2-magnetostatics/13-current-sheets-solenoids-and-the-vector-potential#5-the-vector-potential|Lecture 13]]). Call that field $\mathbf{M}$:

> [!key] Bound current density
> $$
> \mathbf{J}_b = \frac{\partial\mathbf{P}}{\partial t} + \nabla\times\mathbf{M}.
> $$
> In the notes' words, $\partial\mathbf{P}/\partial t$ is *not sufficient*: bound charges can also carry closed-loop, divergence-free currents — orbits and spins — that never pile up charge anywhere, so the continuity equation cannot see them. $\mathbf{J}_M = \nabla\times\mathbf{M}$ (the slides' symbol) is their macroscopic average, the **magnetization current density** [A/m²].

As an argument this shows only that such a term is *allowed*; by itself it fixes $\mathbf{M}$ no better than Lecture 13 fixed $\mathbf{A}$ (any gradient could be added). What pins $\mathbf{M}$ down is the physics of §2, $\mathbf{M} = N\mathbf{m}$ — and the picture below shows that the curl of exactly this $\mathbf{M}$ is the leftover loop current.

**The picture: loops cancel inside, survive at the edges.** Model a uniformly magnetized slab as a lattice of cells of side $s$, each with a loop current $I$ around it. Each cell has $m = Is^2$, so $M = m/s^3 = I/s$. Every interior edge is shared by two loops whose currents run in opposite directions, and they cancel. Only the boundary edges have no partner: along the boundary there is one uncancelled current $I$ per length $s$, a surface current density $I/s = M$ in A/m, circulating in the sense of the loops. If $\mathbf{M}$ varies, neighbouring loops differ and the shared edges stop cancelling. For $\mathbf{M} = M_z(x)\hat z$ the leftover current density on the shared edges is $-\partial M_z/\partial x$ along $\hat y$ — exactly $(\nabla\times\mathbf{M})_y$. That is what $\nabla\times\mathbf{M}$ means.

> [!key] Magnetization current: volume and surface
> $$
> \mathbf{J}_M = \nabla\times\mathbf{M}\quad[\text{A/m}^2],\qquad\qquad \mathbf{J}_{sM} = \mathbf{M}\times\hat n\quad[\text{A/m}],
> $$
> with $\hat n$ the **outward** normal of the magnetized body. At an interface between two magnetized media, with $\hat n$ from medium 2 into medium 1, the two faces add up to $\mathbf{J}_{sM} = \hat n\times(\mathbf{M}_1-\mathbf{M}_2)$.
>
> **The surface term is this site's addition:** neither the notes nor the slides write it. It is the exact twin of the bound surface charge $\rho_{sb} = \mathbf{P}\cdot\hat n$ of [[1-electrostatics/08-conductors-dielectrics-and-polarization#3-dielectrics-bound-charge-and-polarization|Lecture 8]], and it follows from $\nabla\times\mathbf{M}$ applied across the thin surface layer where $\mathbf{M}$ falls to zero: replace $\nabla$ by $\hat n$ acting on the jump (outside minus inside), $\hat n\times(0-\mathbf{M}) = \mathbf{M}\times\hat n$, just as $-\hat n\cdot(0-\mathbf{P}) = \mathbf{P}\cdot\hat n$.

**A magnetized rod is a solenoid.** Take a long rod magnetized along its axis, $\mathbf{M} = M\hat z$ (figure, right). On its side $\hat n = \hat r$ and $\mathbf{M}\times\hat r = M\hat\phi$: a sheet of current circling the rod exactly like the winding of a solenoid, with $nI$ replaced by $M$. On the end faces $\hat n = \pm\hat z$ and $\mathbf{M}\times\hat n = 0$. So a long, uniformly magnetized rod with no free current has the field of a long solenoid, $\mathbf{B}\approx\mu_0\mathbf{M}$ inside — the result the notes reach by averaging stacks of atomic loops (§4). A permanent magnet with $\mu_0M\approx1.3$ T is, as far as its field is concerned, a coil of 1000 turns per metre carrying 1000 A.

## 4. H redefined: Maxwell's equations in matter

Now the bookkeeping. The microscopic Ampère–Maxwell law counts all current:

$$
\nabla\times\frac{\mathbf{B}}{\mu_0} = \mathbf{J}_f + \frac{\partial\mathbf{P}}{\partial t} + \nabla\times\mathbf{M} + \frac{\partial(\epsilon_0\mathbf{E})}{\partial t}.
$$

The magnetization current is a curl, so it moves to the left side and joins $\mathbf{B}/\mu_0$; the polarization current is a time derivative, so it joins the displacement current:

$$
\nabla\times\Big(\frac{\mathbf{B}}{\mu_0}-\mathbf{M}\Big) = \mathbf{J}_f + \frac{\partial}{\partial t}(\epsilon_0\mathbf{E}+\mathbf{P}).
$$

Gauss's law does the same with the bound charge, $\nabla\cdot(\epsilon_0\mathbf{E}+\mathbf{P}) = \rho_f$ (Lecture 8). Give the two combinations names and drop the subscript $f$:

> [!key] Maxwell's equations in matter ("the same form as before")
> $$
> \begin{gathered}
> \nabla\cdot\mathbf{D} = \rho,\qquad \nabla\cdot\mathbf{B} = 0,\\[4pt]
> \nabla\times\mathbf{E} = -\frac{\partial\mathbf{B}}{\partial t},\qquad \nabla\times\mathbf{H} = \mathbf{J}+\frac{\partial\mathbf{D}}{\partial t},
> \end{gathered}
> $$
> with the **auxiliary fields**
> $$
> \mathbf{D}\equiv\epsilon_0\mathbf{E}+\mathbf{P},\qquad\qquad \mathbf{H}\equiv\frac{\mathbf{B}}{\mu_0}-\mathbf{M},
> $$
> and $\rho$, $\mathbf{J}$ now meaning **free** charge and current only. For linear, isotropic media $\mathbf{D} = \epsilon\mathbf{E}$ and $\mathbf{B} = \mu\mathbf{H}$. In integral form the last law is $\oint_C\mathbf{H}\cdot d\mathbf{l} = I_{f,\text{enc}} + \dfrac{d}{dt}\displaystyle\int_S\mathbf{D}\cdot d\mathbf{S}$: Ampère's law counts free current only, whatever magnetic material the loop passes through.

Faraday's law and $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B} = 0$ needed no change: they have no sources, and they are written in $\mathbf{E}$ and $\mathbf{B}$ — the two fields in the Lorentz force $q(\mathbf{E}+\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B})$, the ones a charge actually feels. Only the two source laws were rewritten, and each now has an auxiliary field ($\mathbf{D}$, $\mathbf{H}$) whose sources are the free ones. The boundary conditions of [[3-maxwell-and-waves/16-charge-conservation-displacement-current-and-maxwells-equations#4-boundary-conditions--same-for-dynamics|Lecture 16 §4]] were already of this macroscopic kind: their $\rho_s$ and $\mathbf{J}_s$ are free surface sources (§6).

**What sets what.** The notes' footnote 6 puts it in one line: free currents produce the **magnetizing field** $\mathbf{H}$, which does not care what material is around; the material then decides how much flux density $\mathbf{B} = \mu\mathbf{H}$ that $\mathbf{H}$ produces — just as free charge produces $\mathbf{D}$ and the medium decides $\mathbf{E} = \mathbf{D}/\epsilon$. ("Does not care" needs symmetry to hold exactly; see the trap below.) Names vary: the notes call $\mathbf{H}$ the *magnetic field intensity* or *magnetizing field* and $\mathbf{B}$ the *magnetic flux density* (or "magnetic field B" for short); the slides call $\mathbf{H}$ the *magnetic field*. The notes also write $\mu_o$, $\epsilon_o$ and $N_a$ where this site writes $\mu_0$, $\epsilon_0$ and $N$.

### Linear media: χm and μ

Laboratory measurements (inductances of coils wound around magnetic materials, say the notes, with a footnote recalling Lecture 15's $L\propto\mu$) show that for most materials $\mathbf{M}$ is proportional to $\mathbf{H}$:

> [!key] Linear magnetic media
> $$
> \mathbf{M} = \chi_m\mathbf{H}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \mathbf{B} = \mu_0(\mathbf{H}+\mathbf{M}) = \mu_0(1+\chi_m)\mathbf{H} = \mu\mathbf{H},\qquad \mu = \mu_0(1+\chi_m) = \mu_r\mu_0,\quad \mu_r = 1+\chi_m .
> $$
> $\chi_m$ (**magnetic susceptibility**) and $\mu_r$ (**relative permeability** — the slides' symbol; the notes never use it) are dimensionless; the **permeability** $\mu$ is in H/m, with $\mu_0 = 4\pi\times10^{-7}$ H/m. The $\mathbf{H}$ in $\mathbf{M} = \chi_m\mathbf{H}$ is the total $\mathbf{H}$ at that point of the material. Compare the dielectric column of slide 8: $\mathbf{P} = \epsilon_0\chi_e\mathbf{E}$, $\mathbf{D} = \epsilon_0(1+\chi_e)\mathbf{E} = \epsilon\mathbf{E}$.

> [!trap] No μ₀ in M = χm H (the point highlighted on the slide)
> - $\mathbf{P} = \epsilon_0\chi_e\mathbf{E}$ needs $\epsilon_0$ because $\mathbf{P}$ [C/m²] and $\mathbf{E}$ [V/m] have different units. $\mathbf{M}$ and $\mathbf{H}$ are both in A/m, so the dimensionless $\chi_m$ alone converts one into the other. "$\mathbf{M} = \mu_0\chi_m\mathbf{H}$" comes out in tesla — wrong.
> - $\mathbf{M} = \mathbf{B}/\mu_0 - \mathbf{H}$, not $\mathbf{B}/\mu_0$, and not $\mathbf{B}/\mu - \mathbf{H}$, which is identically zero in a linear medium (the twin of "$\mathbf{P} = \mathbf{D}-\epsilon\mathbf{E}$").
> - $\mathbf{H} = \mathbf{B}/\mu_0 - \mathbf{M} = \mathbf{B}/\mu$. Never $\mathbf{B}/\mu - \mathbf{M}$, which subtracts the magnetization twice.
> - The concept map on the slides writes $\mathbf{M} = \mathbf{B}\,(\mu-\mu_0)/(\mu\mu_0)$; it is the same thing, $\chi_m\mathbf{H}$, expressed through $\mathbf{B}$ (the ink beside it says so).

### The solenoid with a core

The notes make all of this concrete with a long solenoid — $n$ turns per metre, current $I_0$ — and a long cylindrical core inside it, thinner than the coil. Without the core, [[2-magnetostatics/13-current-sheets-solenoids-and-the-vector-potential#3-the-solenoid|Lecture 13]] gives $\mathbf{H}_0 = nI_0\hat z$ and $\mathbf{B}_0 = \mu_0nI_0\hat z$ inside the winding. With the core, its atomic loops add their own field, $\mu_0\mathbf{M}$, inside the core and (being a long "solenoid" themselves) nothing outside it:

$$
\mathbf{B} = \mathbf{B}_0 + \mu_0\mathbf{M}\ \ \text{in the core},\qquad \mathbf{B} = \mathbf{B}_0\ \ \text{in the air around it}\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \mathbf{H} = \frac{\mathbf{B}}{\mu_0}-\mathbf{M} = nI_0\hat z\ \ \text{in both}.
$$

$\mathbf{B}$ differs between the core and its surroundings; $\mathbf{H}$ is the same in both, "analogous with $\mathbf{D}$ in dielectrics". Whether $\mathbf{B}$ in the core is larger or smaller than $\mathbf{B}_0$ depends on the sign of the atomic loop currents, that is, on the direction of $\mathbf{M}$: smaller in diamagnets, larger in paramagnets and ferromagnets.

> [!derivation]- Where μ₀M comes from: stacks of atomic loops (the notes' model)
> Put $N = 1/(\Delta x\,\Delta y\,\Delta z)$ atoms per cubic metre on a lattice, each a loop of area $A_l$ carrying $I_l$ about $\hat z$, so $\mathbf{m} = I_lA_l\hat z$. A column of loops spaced $\Delta z$ apart is a little solenoid with $1/\Delta z$ turns per metre: inside its cross-section $A_l$ the field is $\mu_0I_l/\Delta z$, outside it zero. There is one column per area $\Delta x\,\Delta y$, so the field averaged over the cross-section is
> $$
> \frac{A_l}{\Delta x\,\Delta y}\cdot\mu_0\frac{I_l}{\Delta z}\,\hat z = \mu_0NI_lA_l\,\hat z = \mu_0N\mathbf{m} = \mu_0\mathbf{M}.
> $$
> The calculation mirrors Lecture 8's average over a lattice of electric dipoles, which gave $-\mathbf{P}/\epsilon_0$. Note the sign difference: the averaged field of charge dipoles *opposes* $\mathbf{p}$ (between its two charges a dipole's field points from $+$ to $-$), while the averaged field of current loops runs *along* $\mathbf{m}$ (inside a loop its field points along its moment). That is the physical root of the $+\mathbf{P}$ in $\mathbf{D}$ and the $-\mathbf{M}$ in $\mathbf{H}$ (§7).

> [!example] Example (this site's numbers) — a coil with and without a core
> $n = 1000$ turns/m, $I_0 = 2$ A, and a long core with $\chi_m = 99$ ($\mu_r = 100$) along the axis.
> - $\mathbf{H}$: $nI_0 = 2000$ A/m everywhere inside the winding, in the core and in the air around it. (Ampère's law on a rectangle with one leg inside the core encloses the free current $n\ell I_0$, whatever the core.)
> - Air around the core: $B_0 = \mu_0H = 2.51$ mT.
> - Core: $M = \chi_mH = 1.98\times10^{5}$ A/m, $\mu_0M = 0.249$ T, $B = \mu_0(H+M) = 0.251$ T — 100 times $B_0$.
> - The same coil on an iron core with the table's $\mu_r = 5000$ would, by the linear formula, give 12.6 T. Impossible: iron saturates near 2.2 T (§2), and well before that $\mathbf{M}$ stops being proportional to $\mathbf{H}$ (§5).

> [!trap] "H is the same with or without the core" is a fact about this geometry, not a law
> It holds here because the core is long and lies along $\mathbf{H}$. $\mathbf{H}$ is tangential to the core's side wall, which carries no free current, so tangential $\mathbf{H}$ is continuous there, and the end faces are far away. Shorten the core, or turn it across the field, and its ends — where $\mathbf{M}$ stops and normal $\mathbf{B}$ must stay continuous — produce a *demagnetizing* field that makes $\mathbf{H}$ inside smaller than $nI_0$ (for $\chi_m>0$; in a diamagnet, very slightly larger). For a sphere in a uniform applied $\mathbf{H}_0$, $\mathbf{H}$ inside is $\mathbf{H}_0-\mathbf{M}/3 = 3\mathbf{H}_0/(\mu_r+2)$, so even an iron sphere ($\mu_r\to\infty$) multiplies $B$ by less than 3, where a long iron rod along the field would multiply it by $\mu_r$. This is the twin of Lecture 8's trap "$\mathbf{D}$ is the same inside and out is a slab result" (a dielectric sphere has $\mathbf{E}_p = -\mathbf{P}/3\epsilon_0$). The slides' $\mathbf{M} = \chi_m\mathbf{H}_{\text{ext}}$, written with the *applied* field, carries the same caveat. What is general: $\nabla\times\mathbf{H} = \mathbf{J}_{\text{free}}$ (plus $\partial\mathbf{D}/\partial t$ when the fields vary), tangential $\mathbf{H}$ continuous where there is no free surface current, normal $\mathbf{B}$ continuous everywhere.

**Inductance is proportional to μ.** [[2-magnetostatics/15-inductance-magnetic-energy-and-the-potentials|Lecture 15]] promised that inside a medium $\mu_0\to\mu$ in $L$, in the energy $\tfrac12LI^2$ and in the energy density $\tfrac12\mu H^2$. Now it is clear why: the free current fixes $\mathbf{H}$, and the flux $\Psi = \int\mu\mathbf{H}\cdot d\mathbf{S}$ grows with $\mu$. A long solenoid filled with a core has $L = n^2\mu A\ell$, $\mu_r$ times its air-core value: with $n = 1000$/m, $A = 1$ cm² and $\ell = 10$ cm, 12.6 μH becomes 1.26 mH for $\mu_r = 100$. The notes turn this around: measured inductances of coils wound on magnetic materials are the laboratory evidence that $\mathbf{M}\propto\mathbf{H}$ for most materials.

## 5. Dia-, para- and ferromagnetism

| | diamagnetic | paramagnetic | ferromagnetic |
|---|---|---|---|
| $\chi_m$ | negative, about $-10^{-5}$ | positive, about $10^{-5}$ to $10^{-3}$ | positive, $10^2$ to $10^6$, not a constant |
| $\mu_r$ | slightly below 1 | slightly above 1 | $\gg1$ |
| $\mathbf{M}$ relative to $\mathbf{H}$ | opposes it | weakly along it | strongly along it |
| mechanism | orbital moments *induced* by the field (Lenz) | *permanent* moments partly aligned by the field | permanent moments aligned *spontaneously*, in domains |
| keeps $\mathbf{M}$ when $\mathbf{H} = 0$? | no | no | yes — permanent magnets |
| examples (slides) | copper, bismuth, water, silver | aluminum, platinum, liquid oxygen, palladium | iron, nickel, cobalt and their alloys |

**Diamagnetism** is in every atom. Switch on a field and Faraday's law ([[2-magnetostatics/14-faradays-law-and-induced-emf|Lecture 14]]) induces an emf around each electron orbit; by Lenz's law the orbital motion changes so that the induced moment *opposes* the applied field. (The notes' "electron clouds spin up" refers to this change in the orbital motion, not to quantum spin.) Where no permanent atomic moments outweigh it — water, wood, glass, and metals such as copper, silver and bismuth — it is the response you measure. $\mathbf{B} = \mu\mathbf{H}$ ends up slightly smaller than $\mu_0\mathbf{H}$, and such materials are weakly pushed out of a strong field.

**Paramagnetism** needs atoms with a *permanent* moment: unpaired electron spins, which the notes trace to unfilled inner shells (in a filled shell the Pauli exclusion principle pairs spins oppositely and their moments cancel). The field's torque $\mathbf{m}\times\mathbf{B}$ pulls the moments toward $\mathbf{B}$ and thermal agitation scatters them again, and the contest is lopsided: at 1 T the aligning energy $\mu_BB\approx9\times10^{-24}$ J is about $1/450$ of $k_BT\approx4\times10^{-21}$ J at room temperature, so only a small excess of moments ends up along the field. The result is a small $\mathbf{M}$ along $\mathbf{H}$ that outweighs the diamagnetic response; paramagnets are weakly attracted by magnets. The instructor's mnemonic: **Para**magnetic ↔ **Pos**itive.

**Ferromagnetism** is a quantum effect. In iron, nickel and cobalt the spins of neighbouring atoms align *spontaneously*; the notes describe conduction electrons, constrained by the exclusion principle, passing a spin orientation from one atom to the next — the "exchange" interaction. The result is **domains**: regions in which essentially all moments point the same way, magnetized to saturation, with different domains pointing in different directions so that an unmagnetized sample has $\mathbf{M}\approx0$ overall. An applied $\mathbf{H}$ grows the domains that point along it and turns the others, so a modest $\mathbf{H}$ produces an enormous $\mathbf{M}$. But $\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{H})$ is neither linear nor single-valued: $\mathbf{B} = \mu_0\mathbf{H}+\mu_0\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{H})$ depends on what happened before.

That memory is **hysteresis**. Raise $H$ from zero and $B$ climbs along the virgin curve to saturation; bring $H$ back to zero and $B$ falls back only to the **remanence** $B_r$; a reverse field $-H_c$, the **coercivity**, is needed to bring $B$ to zero. Non-zero $\mathbf{B}$ with zero $\mathbf{H}$ is what a **permanent magnet** is (notes, p. 12). Materials with narrow loops (small $H_c$: transformer steel, ferrites) are called *soft* and are used where the field reverses many times per second, because the area of the loop is the energy lost as heat per cubic metre in every cycle. Materials with wide loops (large $B_r$ and $H_c$) are *hard* and make permanent magnets. For ferromagnets, then, "$\mu_r = 5000$" is a small-field slope, not a constant of the material.

## 6. Boundary conditions in magnetic media

The four boundary conditions of [[3-maxwell-and-waves/16-charge-conservation-displacement-current-and-maxwells-equations#4-boundary-conditions--same-for-dynamics|Lecture 16]] are already the macroscopic ones: their $\rho_s$ and $\mathbf{J}_s$ are *free* surface sources. The two magnetic conditions, combined with $\mathbf{B} = \mu\mathbf{H}$ on each side, give:

> [!key] Magnetic boundary conditions ($\hat n$ from medium 2 into medium 1)
> $$
> \hat n\cdot(\mathbf{B}_1-\mathbf{B}_2) = 0\ \Longrightarrow\ \mu_1H_{1n} = \mu_2H_{2n},\qquad\qquad \hat n\times(\mathbf{H}_1-\mathbf{H}_2) = \mathbf{J}_s\ \ (\text{free}).
> $$
> With no free surface current, $\mathbf{H}_t$ is continuous, hence $\mathbf{B}_t/\mu$ is continuous, and field lines **refract**:
> $$
> \frac{\tan\theta_1}{\tan\theta_2} = \frac{\mu_1}{\mu_2}\qquad(\text{angles from the normal}).
> $$
> $\mathbf{B}$ is parallel to $\mathbf{H}$ in each medium, so $B$-lines and $H$-lines bend alike. Tangential $\mathbf{B}$ jumps even with no free current; the jump is carried by the bound current on the interface: $\hat n\times(\mathbf{B}_1-\mathbf{B}_2)/\mu_0 = \mathbf{J}_s + \hat n\times(\mathbf{M}_1-\mathbf{M}_2)$. And normal $\mathbf{H}$ is *not* continuous: $H_{1n}-H_{2n} = -(M_{1n}-M_{2n})$. That is why "$\nabla\cdot\mathbf{H} = 0$" fails across a magnetic interface, while $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B} = 0$ never does.

This is [[1-electrostatics/09-static-fields-in-dielectric-media#2-refraction-of-field-lines-at-an-interface|Lecture 9's refraction law]] with $\epsilon$ replaced by $\mu$ — the slides' ink writes $\mu_1H_{n1} = \mu_2H_{n2}$ on the Lecture 16 dielectric slide and adds $B_{n1} = B_{n2}$ to this lecture's concept map. The worked problem [[problems/fields-across-a-magnetic-interface]] runs it with numbers, including the magnetization current on the interface. The extreme case is iron. With $\mu_r = 5000$, a line running at 85° to the normal inside the iron leaves into the air at 0.13°; even at 89.9° inside it leaves at 6.5°. Field lines leave an iron surface essentially perpendicularly, just as electric field lines leave a conductor, which is why iron pole pieces shape the field in the gap of a motor or a loudspeaker.

> [!recipe] Problems with magnetic media
> 1. **$\mathbf{H}$ from the free currents alone**: Ampère's law, the sheet field $\tfrac12\mathbf{J}_s\times\hat n$ and superposition, continuity of tangential $\mathbf{H}$ wherever there is no free surface current (and $\mu_1H_{1n} = \mu_2H_{2n}$ where $\mathbf{H}$ crosses a boundary).
> 2. **$\mathbf{B} = \mu\mathbf{H}$** region by region, each region with its own $\mu$.
> 3. Flux, inductance or force — whatever is asked — from $\mathbf{B}$.
> 4. **$\mathbf{M} = \mathbf{B}/\mu_0-\mathbf{H} = \chi_m\mathbf{H}$ last**, if asked. The magnetization currents $\nabla\times\mathbf{M}$ and $\mathbf{M}\times\hat n$ are a check: put into the *vacuum* formulas together with the free currents, they must give back $\mathbf{B}$.
>

> [!example] Example (slides, solved in class) — a magnetic slab between two current sheets
> An infinite slab with $\mu_r = 100$ lies between two infinite current sheets: $-0.1\hat y$ A/m on top (out of the page) and $+0.1\hat y$ A/m below (into the page), with $x$ to the right, $z$ up and $y$ into the page. Find $\mathbf{H}$, $\mathbf{B}$, $\mathbf{M}$ in the slab, and compare with a non-magnetic slab ($\mu_r = 1$).
> - *$\mathbf{H}$ from the free currents.* Each sheet gives $\tfrac12\mathbf{J}_s\times\hat n$ with $\hat n$ toward the field point. Between them: top sheet $\tfrac12(-0.1\hat y)\times(-\hat z) = +0.05\hat x$, bottom sheet $\tfrac12(0.1\hat y)\times\hat z = +0.05\hat x$, so $\mathbf{H} = 0.1\hat x$ A/m $= J_s\hat x$. Above and below the pair the two contributions cancel: $\mathbf{H} = 0$.
> - *Into the slab.* Its faces carry no free current ("no $J_s$ on slab", says the ink), so tangential $\mathbf{H}$ is continuous: $\mathbf{H}_{\text{slab}} = 0.1\hat x$ A/m — the same as with no slab at all.
> - *The medium sets the rest.* $\mathbf{B}_{\text{slab}} = \mu_0\mu_r\mathbf{H} = 100\mu_0(0.1)\hat x = 1.26\times10^{-5}\hat x$ T (12.6 μT); $\mathbf{M} = \chi_m\mathbf{H} = 99\times0.1\hat x = 9.9\hat x$ A/m; check $\mu_0(\mathbf{H}+\mathbf{M}) = \mu_0(10)\hat x$ ✓.
> - *Non-magnetic slab.* Same $\mathbf{H}$; $\mathbf{B} = \mu_0(0.1)\hat x = 1.26\times10^{-7}\hat x$ T, 100 times smaller; $\mathbf{M} = 0$.
>
> *The check with bound currents (this site's addition).* Inside the uniform slab $\nabla\times\mathbf{M} = 0$; all of the magnetization current sits on the faces. Top face ($\hat n = +\hat z$): $\mathbf{M}\times\hat n = 9.9\hat x\times\hat z = -9.9\hat y$ A/m. Bottom face: $+9.9\hat y$ A/m. Both have the same sense as the free sheet next to them. Free plus bound is $10$ A/m per face, and the *vacuum* sheet formula with these four sheets gives $\mathbf{B} = \mu_0(10\text{ A/m})\hat x = 100\mu_0\mathbf{H}$ inside the slab, $\mu_0(0.1)\hat x$ in any air gap between a sheet and the slab, and zero outside — the same field, now computed from all the currents with no $\mu$ at all.

## 7. The dictionary: polarization ↔ magnetization

The slides' first annotation reads "M → P", and the deck keeps returning to the pairing: a "Magnetic | dielectric" two-column slide, a paired "Recall: D and P" / "B and M" slide, a concept map and a three-materials comparison. Here is the whole translation, row by row. It extends the electrostatics ↔ magnetostatics [[2-magnetostatics/index#the-dictionary-electrostatics--magnetostatics|dictionary on the Unit 2 page]] into matter.

| | polarization (Lectures 8–9) | magnetization (Lecture 17) |
|---|---|---|
| microscopic dipole | $\mathbf{p} = q\mathbf{d}$ [C·m], from $-$ to $+$ | $\mathbf{m} = I\mathbf{A}$ [A·m²], right-hand rule |
| torque in a field | $\mathbf{p}\times\mathbf{E}$ | $\mathbf{m}\times\mathbf{B}$ |
| density | $\mathbf{P} = N\mathbf{p}$ [C/m²] | $\mathbf{M} = N\mathbf{m}$ [A/m] |
| bound volume source | $\rho_b = -\nabla\cdot\mathbf{P}$ (and $\partial\mathbf{P}/\partial t$, Lecture 11) | $\mathbf{J}_M = \nabla\times\mathbf{M}$ |
| bound surface source ($\hat n$ outward) | $\rho_{sb} = \mathbf{P}\cdot\hat n$ | $\mathbf{J}_{sM} = \mathbf{M}\times\hat n$ (this site's addition) |
| at an interface ($\hat n$ from 2 into 1) | $\rho_{sb} = -\hat n\cdot(\mathbf{P}_1-\mathbf{P}_2)$ | $\mathbf{J}_{sM} = \hat n\times(\mathbf{M}_1-\mathbf{M}_2)$ |
| averaged field of the bound sources, notes' lattice model | $-\mathbf{P}/\epsilon_0$, opposes $\mathbf{p}$ (slab polarized across its faces) | $+\mu_0\mathbf{M}$, along $\mathbf{m}$ (long rod magnetized along its axis) |
| auxiliary field | $\mathbf{D} = \epsilon_0\mathbf{E}+\mathbf{P}$ | $\mathbf{H} = \mathbf{B}/\mu_0-\mathbf{M}$ |
| its law | $\oint\mathbf{D}\cdot d\mathbf{S} = Q_f$, $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{D} = \rho_f$ | $\oint\mathbf{H}\cdot d\mathbf{l} = I_f + d\psi_E/dt$, $\nabla\times\mathbf{H} = \mathbf{J}_f+\partial\mathbf{D}/\partial t$ |
| linear medium | $\mathbf{P} = \epsilon_0\chi_e\mathbf{E}$ | $\mathbf{M} = \chi_m\mathbf{H}$ |
| material constant | $\epsilon = \epsilon_0(1+\chi_e) = \epsilon_r\epsilon_0$ | $\mu = \mu_0(1+\chi_m) = \mu_r\mu_0$ |
| constitutive relation | $\mathbf{D} = \epsilon\mathbf{E}$ | $\mathbf{B} = \mu\mathbf{H}$ |
| set by the free sources (with enough symmetry) | $\mathbf{D}$ | $\mathbf{H}$ |
| then set by the medium | $\mathbf{E} = \mathbf{D}/\epsilon$ | $\mathbf{B} = \mu\mathbf{H}$ |
| "unchanged by the material" caveat | slab normal to $\mathbf{E}$ only; sphere: $-\mathbf{P}/3\epsilon_0$ | long core along $\mathbf{H}$ only; sphere: $-\mathbf{M}/3$ |
| continuous without free surface sources | $D_n$ and $E_t$ (always) | $B_n$ (always) and $H_t$ |
| interface law | $\epsilon_1E_{1n} = \epsilon_2E_{2n}$, $\tan\theta_1/\tan\theta_2 = \epsilon_1/\epsilon_2$ | $\mu_1H_{1n} = \mu_2H_{2n}$, $\tan\theta_1/\tan\theta_2 = \mu_1/\mu_2$ |
| sign of the susceptibility | $\chi_e>0$ | $\chi_m<0$ (dia), $>0$ small (para), $\gg1$ (ferro) |
| huge-susceptibility limit | $\mathbf{E}\to0$ inside: like a conductor | $B$-lines leave the surface normally: iron pole faces |
| circuit element | $C\propto\epsilon$ | $L\propto\mu$ |
| model of the susceptibility | Lorentz oscillator ([[1-electrostatics/11-lorentz-drude-models-for-conductivity-and-susceptibility\|Lecture 11]]) | quantum mechanics (notes) |

> [!intuition] The four places where the analogy is *not* a straight translation
> 1. **$\epsilon_0$ appears in $\mathbf{P} = \epsilon_0\chi_e\mathbf{E}$, but no $\mu_0$ appears in $\mathbf{M} = \chi_m\mathbf{H}$.** Units: $\mathbf{P}$ and $\mathbf{E}$ differ, $\mathbf{M}$ and $\mathbf{H}$ agree (both A/m). This is the trap the instructor highlighted.
> 2. **$\rho_b = -\nabla\cdot\mathbf{P}$ has a minus sign; $\mathbf{J}_M = +\nabla\times\mathbf{M}$ does not.** Where $\mathbf{P}$ converges, the $+$ ends of the dipoles crowd together and leave positive charge, hence the minus; neighbouring loop currents simply add, with no sign flip.
> 3. **$\mathbf{D}$ adds $\mathbf{P}$ but $\mathbf{H}$ subtracts $\mathbf{M}$.** The fields a charge feels are $\mathbf{E}$ and $\mathbf{B}$ (Lorentz force), so $\mathbf{B}$, not $\mathbf{H}$, is the twin of $\mathbf{E}$, and the auxiliary fields are built from them: $\mathbf{D} = \epsilon_0\mathbf{E}+\mathbf{P}$ and $\mathbf{H} = \mathbf{B}/\mu_0-\mathbf{M}$. Physically, a charge dipole's averaged field *opposes* $\mathbf{p}$ while a current loop's averaged field runs *along* $\mathbf{m}$ — the dielectric weakens $\mathbf{E}$, a para- or ferromagnet strengthens $\mathbf{B}$.
> 4. **$\chi_e>0$ always, while $\chi_m$ can be negative.** Electric dipoles are induced *along* the field; induced orbital moments oppose it (Lenz), and only permanent moments make $\chi_m$ positive.

## 8. Summary

- Matter: average the bound sources. $\rho = \rho_f-\nabla\cdot\mathbf{P}$, $\mathbf{J} = \mathbf{J}_f+\partial\mathbf{P}/\partial t+\nabla\times\mathbf{M}$ (bound charge: Lecture 8; polarization current: Lecture 11; magnetization current: here).
- Atomic loops: $\mathbf{m} = I\mathbf{A}$ (orbits, spins), torque $\mathbf{m}\times\mathbf{B}$ toward alignment, $\mathbf{M} = N\mathbf{m}$ in A/m — the unit of $\mathbf{H}$.
- Continuity of bound charge forces the extra bound current to be a curl, $\nabla\times\mathbf{M}$; physically, loops cancel inside and survive where $\mathbf{M}$ changes or ends: $\mathbf{J}_M = \nabla\times\mathbf{M}$, $\mathbf{J}_{sM} = \mathbf{M}\times\hat n$ (site's addition, the twin of $\mathbf{P}\cdot\hat n$). A uniformly magnetized rod is a solenoid with $nI\to M$.
- $\mathbf{H}\equiv\mathbf{B}/\mu_0-\mathbf{M}$, $\mathbf{D}\equiv\epsilon_0\mathbf{E}+\mathbf{P}$: Maxwell's equations keep their form with free $\rho$, $\mathbf{J}$. Free currents set $\mathbf{H}$, the medium sets $\mathbf{B}$.
- Linear media: $\mathbf{M} = \chi_m\mathbf{H}$ (no $\mu_0$), $\mathbf{B} = \mu\mathbf{H}$, $\mu = \mu_0(1+\chi_m) = \mu_r\mu_0$; solenoid with a core: same $H$, $B\times\mu_r$ (long core only); $L\propto\mu$.
- Diamagnets $\chi_m\approx-10^{-5}$ (induced, Lenz), paramagnets $\chi_m\approx+10^{-5}$ (permanent moments, no domains), ferromagnets $\chi_m\gg1$ (domains, non-linear, hysteresis, $B_r$, $H_c$, permanent magnets).
- Boundaries: $B_n$ continuous, $\mu_1H_{1n} = \mu_2H_{2n}$; $H_t$ jumps by the free $J_s$; $\tan\theta_1/\tan\theta_2 = \mu_1/\mu_2$; lines leave iron normally.
