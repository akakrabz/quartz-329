*Lecture 18 · course notes §18 · slides "Uniform Plane Waves in Free Space" · prev: [[3-maxwell-and-waves/17-magnetization-and-maxwells-equations-in-matter|Lecture 17]] · next: Lecture 19, d'Alembert solutions and radiation from current sheets (coming) · worked problem: [[problems/a-pulse-on-the-move|A pulse on the move]] · practice: [[practice/18-wave-equation-and-plane-waves|12 problems]]*

> [!abstract] In one breath
> Take a region with no charges and no currents in it. A static field there can only be the field of sources outside it, and it disappears when they are switched off. Maxwell's two curl equations, however, still feed each other: a changing $\mathbf{H}$ makes $\mathbf{E}$ curl (Faraday), and a changing $\mathbf{E}$ makes $\mathbf{H}$ curl (the displacement current of [[3-maxwell-and-waves/16-charge-conservation-displacement-current-and-maxwells-equations|Lecture 16]]). Take the curl of one and substitute the other, and you get the **wave equation** $\nabla^2\mathbf{E} = \mu\epsilon\,\partial^2\mathbf{E}/\partial t^2$. Its simplest solutions are **plane waves**, $\mathbf{E} = \hat x\,f(t\mp z/v)$, with any waveform $f$. They slide along $\pm z$ without changing shape at $v = 1/\sqrt{\mu\epsilon}$, which is $c\approx3\times10^8$ m/s in vacuum. Each carries a magnetic field $\mathbf{H} = \pm\hat y\,f/\eta$: perpendicular to $\mathbf{E}$, of size $\lvert\mathbf{E}\rvert/\eta$ with the **intrinsic impedance** $\eta = \sqrt{\mu/\epsilon}$ ($\approx377\ \Omega$ in vacuum), and oriented so that $\mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{H}$ points the way the wave travels. Light, radio and X-rays are all this one solution with different waveforms.

## 1. Fields without sources?

Units 1 and 2 were about fields with sources: charge makes a static $\mathbf{E}$, steady current makes a static $\mathbf{H}$. With the equations completed in [[3-maxwell-and-waves/16-charge-conservation-displacement-current-and-maxwells-equations|Lecture 16]], ask the opposite question. Take a region that is **homogeneous** (the same $\mu$ and $\epsilon$ everywhere), **lossless** ($\sigma = 0$) and **source-free** (no free charge, no current). Can a field that varies in time exist there?

> [!key] Maxwell's equations in a source-free, homogeneous, lossless region
> $$
> \begin{gathered}
> \nabla\cdot\mathbf{E} = 0,\qquad \nabla\cdot\mathbf{H} = 0,\\[4pt]
> \nabla\times\mathbf{E} = -\mu\frac{\partial\mathbf{H}}{\partial t},\qquad \nabla\times\mathbf{H} = \epsilon\frac{\partial\mathbf{E}}{\partial t}.
> \end{gathered}
> $$
> Conditions: $\rho = 0$ and $\mathbf{J} = 0$ (no free charge and no conduction current, so in particular $\sigma = 0$), and $\mu$, $\epsilon$ constant in space and in time. That is a linear medium of [[3-maxwell-and-waves/17-magnetization-and-maxwells-equations-in-matter|Lecture 17]] with $\mathbf{B} = \mu\mathbf{H}$, $\mathbf{D} = \epsilon\mathbf{E}$, or vacuum with $\mu_0$, $\epsilon_0$.

A *static* field cannot exist there on its own. One whose divergence and curl vanish everywhere in space, and which dies off far away, is zero (the Helmholtz theorem of [[1-electrostatics/04-divergence-and-curl|Lecture 4]]). A charge-free region can still hold a static field, such as the gap of a capacitor, but only as the field of charges outside it, and it vanishes when they are removed. A *time-varying* field can, and the solutions are electromagnetic waves: radio, microwaves, infrared, light, ultraviolet, X-rays and gamma rays, the whole spectrum from low frequency to high. They cross empty space, from the Sun to the Earth for example, carrying energy and momentum. Their sources (time-varying $\rho$ and $\mathbf{J}$, linked by the [[concepts/continuity-equation|continuity equation]]) are somewhere else, such as an antenna or the Sun. Once launched, the fields carry on through the source-free region by themselves.

How they carry on is the slides' opening argument (slide 3, with the terms circled and joined by arrows in class):

1. a time-varying source current $\mathbf{J}(t)$ produces a time-varying $\mathbf{H}$ around it (Ampère–Maxwell);
2. a time-varying $\mathbf{B} = \mu\mathbf{H}$ produces an $\mathbf{E}$ that curls ([[concepts/faradays-law|Faraday]], Lecture 14);
3. a time-varying $\mathbf{D} = \epsilon\mathbf{E}$ produces an $\mathbf{H}$ that curls (the [[concepts/displacement-current|displacement current]], Lecture 16);
4. back to step 2.

Steps 2–4 involve no current at all: $\cdots\to\mathbf{H}\to\mathbf{E}\to\mathbf{H}\to\cdots$ sustains itself and moves away from the source. That recursion *is* the wave. Without the displacement current, step 3 is missing and the chain stops at $\mathbf{E}$. You would get induction but no propagation.

## 2. The wave equation

The notes turn the recursion into one equation for $\mathbf{E}$. Take the curl of Faraday's law and use Ampère–Maxwell to replace $\nabla\times\mathbf{H}$:

$$
\nabla\times(\nabla\times\mathbf{E}) = -\mu\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\big(\nabla\times\mathbf{H}\big) = -\mu\epsilon\frac{\partial^2\mathbf{E}}{\partial t^2}.
$$

The left side is the identity of [[1-electrostatics/04-divergence-and-curl#a-free-consequence-charge-is-conserved|Lecture 4]] (also in the [[0-toolkit/02-vector-calculus-cheatsheet#identities-valid-for-any-smooth-fields|toolkit]]), $\nabla\times(\nabla\times\mathbf{E}) = \nabla(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{E}) - \nabla^2\mathbf{E}$, and its first term is zero because $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{E} = 0$. Each step uses one of the assumptions:

- **$\mu$ constant.** It can then be pulled out of the curl, and $\partial\mathbf{B}/\partial t = \mu\,\partial\mathbf{H}/\partial t$. (Swapping $\nabla\times$ and $\partial/\partial t$ is always allowed for smooth fields.)
- **$\mathbf{J} = 0$ and $\epsilon$ constant.** Ampère–Maxwell then reads $\nabla\times\mathbf{H} = \epsilon\,\partial\mathbf{E}/\partial t$. With a conductor, $\mathbf{J} = \sigma\mathbf{E}$ would add a term $\mu\sigma\,\partial\mathbf{E}/\partial t$, which makes the wave decay (Lectures 22–23).
- **$\nabla\cdot\mathbf{E} = 0$** needs $\rho = 0$ *and* constant $\epsilon$. Gauss's law says $\nabla\cdot(\epsilon\mathbf{E}) = 0$, and $\nabla\cdot(\epsilon\mathbf{E}) = \epsilon\nabla\cdot\mathbf{E} + \mathbf{E}\cdot\nabla\epsilon$.
- **Cartesian components.** The vector Laplacian $\nabla^2\mathbf{E} = \hat x\nabla^2E_x + \hat y\nabla^2E_y + \hat z\nabla^2E_z$ splits into scalar Laplacians only in Cartesian components. In cylindrical or spherical components it does not.

> [!key] The 3D vector wave equation
> $$
> \nabla^2\mathbf{E} = \mu\epsilon\,\frac{\partial^2\mathbf{E}}{\partial t^2},\qquad \nabla^2\mathbf{H} = \mu\epsilon\,\frac{\partial^2\mathbf{H}}{\partial t^2},
> $$
> that is, $\dfrac{\partial^2E_x}{\partial x^2}+\dfrac{\partial^2E_x}{\partial y^2}+\dfrac{\partial^2E_x}{\partial z^2} = \mu\epsilon\dfrac{\partial^2E_x}{\partial t^2}$ and the same for every other Cartesian component of $\mathbf{E}$ and $\mathbf{H}$. The $\mathbf{H}$ equation follows the same way, starting from the curl of Ampère–Maxwell and using $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{H} = 0$ and Faraday's law. The notes derive only the $\mathbf{E}$ equation.

Two remarks keep this in perspective.

**The displacement current is the whole story.** Drop $\partial\mathbf{D}/\partial t$ and the right-hand side of the curl-curl step is zero, giving $\nabla^2\mathbf{E} = 0$. That is Laplace's equation, which has static-like solutions and no waves. The term Maxwell added for the sake of charge conservation is the term that produces light.

**The wave equation is necessary, not sufficient.** Every source-free solution of Maxwell's equations obeys it, but a solution of the wave equation must still pass two more tests: it must be divergence-free, and its $\mathbf{E}$ and $\mathbf{H}$ must satisfy the first-order curl equations *together*. §3 shows a field that solves the wave equation and still fails.

## 3. The planar problem: only $E_x(z,t)$ and $H_y(z,t)$

The notes and the slides reach the same reduced problem by different routes.

**The notes' route: try the simplest field.** The aim at this stage is one nonzero example, not the general solution. Try $\mathbf{E} = \hat x\,E_x(z,t)$, a field that points along $x$ ("$x$-**polarized**": the polarization is the direction of $\mathbf{E}$) and depends only on $z$ and $t$. Gauss's law holds automatically, since $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{E} = \partial E_x/\partial x = 0$. Faraday's law gives $\nabla\times\mathbf{E} = \hat y\,\partial E_x/\partial z$, so $\partial\mathbf{H}/\partial t$ has only a $y$-component and $\mathbf{H} = \hat y\,H_y(z,t)$, apart from a static field that we leave out. The two curl equations collapse to two scalar ones:

> [!key] The two component equations
> $$
> \frac{\partial E_x}{\partial z} = -\mu\,\frac{\partial H_y}{\partial t},\qquad -\frac{\partial H_y}{\partial z} = \epsilon\,\frac{\partial E_x}{\partial t}.
> $$
> The first is the $y$-component of Faraday's law, $(\nabla\times\mathbf{E})_y = \partial E_x/\partial z$. The second is the $x$-component of Ampère–Maxwell, $(\nabla\times\mathbf{H})_x = \partial H_z/\partial y - \partial H_y/\partial z = -\partial H_y/\partial z$, which is where the minus sign comes from. Each says that a field which varies along $z$ forces its partner to vary in time. That is the chain of §1 written as equations.

**The slides' route: a current sheet as the source.** The deck puts a source in the picture: an infinite sheet of surface current $\mathbf{J}_s = -J_s(t)\,\hat x$ [A/m] in the plane $z = 0$, with free space on both sides.

- *No $x$ or $y$ dependence.* The sheet looks the same from every $(x, y)$, so every field depends on $z$ and $t$ only, and $\partial/\partial x = \partial/\partial y = 0$ (inked as "→ 0" on slides 8–9).
- *$\mathbf{H}$ along $\pm\hat y$.* For a steady $J_s$ this is the sheet of [[2-magnetostatics/13-current-sheets-solenoids-and-the-vector-potential|Lecture 13]]: $\mathbf{H} = \tfrac12\mathbf{J}_s\times\hat n = \pm\tfrac12J_s\,\hat y$ for $z\gtrless0$, with $\hat n$ pointing toward the field point. So expect $\mathbf{H} = H_y(z,t)\,\hat y$.
- *$\mathbf{E}$ along $\hat x$ only.* The $y$-component of Faraday's law gives $\partial E_x/\partial z = -\mu_0\,\partial H_y/\partial t$, so $E_x\neq0$. The $x$-component gives $-\partial E_y/\partial z = -\mu_0\,\partial H_x/\partial t = 0$, so $E_y$ is uniform (and, by the $y$-component of Ampère–Maxwell, constant in time). A uniform $E_y$ would still be there with the source switched off, so it is not part of the sheet's field and is set to zero. Gauss's law off the sheet gives $\partial E_z/\partial z = 0$, so $E_z = 0$ for the same reason. The $z$-component of Faraday's law says nothing new. Result: $\mathbf{E} = E_x(z,t)\,\hat x$.

So the slides *derive* the field that the notes *assume*. Away from the sheet the current does not appear in the equations ("in $z = 0$ plane", says the ink), so the two component equations above hold for $z>0$ and for $z<0$, with $\mu_0$ and $\epsilon_0$. If $J_s$ is steady they give $\partial E_x/\partial z = 0$ and $\partial H_y/\partial z = 0$: uniform fields on each side, which is the static sheet (with $E = 0$ because nothing drives one). If $J_s$ varies in time, the two equations couple $E_x$ to $H_y$ and waves leave both faces. Lecture 19 works out their amplitude and the jump of $H_y$ by $J_s$ across the sheet, and names them **uniform plane TEM waves**: *transverse electromagnetic* because $\mathbf{E}$ and $\mathbf{H}$ are both perpendicular to the direction of travel, *uniform plane* because the fields are the same everywhere on each plane $z = $ const. The ink on slide 10 already states the sign: "$\mathbf{E}$ always opposite of $\mathbf{J}_s$".

**Why not $\mathbf{E}$ along $z$?** A field $\mathbf{E} = \hat z\,f(t\mp z/v)$ (pointing along the direction of travel) satisfies the 1D wave equation of §4. It still cannot exist: $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{E} = \partial E_z/\partial z = \mp f'/v\neq0$ unless $f$ is constant, which violates Gauss's law with $\rho = 0$. Its curl is also zero, so Faraday's law gives it no $\mathbf{H}$ partner, and Ampère–Maxwell then forces $\partial E_z/\partial t = 0$. This is the necessary-not-sufficient point of §2, and it is why a plane wave in a source-free region has no field component along its direction of travel.

## 4. The 1D wave equation and its solutions

Cross-differentiate the two component equations (slide 15): differentiate the first by $z$, swap the order of the derivatives, and substitute the second.

$$
\frac{\partial^2E_x}{\partial z^2} = -\mu\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\Big(\frac{\partial H_y}{\partial z}\Big) = -\mu\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\Big(-\epsilon\frac{\partial E_x}{\partial t}\Big).
$$

The 3D equation of §2 with $\mathbf{E} = \hat xE_x(z,t)$ gives the same result directly (the notes' route).

> [!key] The 1D scalar wave equation
> $$
> \frac{\partial^2E_x}{\partial z^2} = \mu\epsilon\,\frac{\partial^2E_x}{\partial t^2} = \frac{1}{v^2}\frac{\partial^2E_x}{\partial t^2},\qquad v\equiv\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu\epsilon}}\ [\text{m/s}],\qquad c = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu_0\epsilon_0}} = 2.998\times10^8\ \text{m/s}.
> $$
> $H_y$ obeys the same equation. In handy units, $c\approx30$ cm per nanosecond, 300 m per microsecond and 300 km per millisecond.

**Cosines, by substitution (notes).** Try $E_x = \cos\big(\omega(t - z/v)\big)$. Then $\partial^2E_x/\partial z^2 = -(\omega/v)^2E_x = -\omega^2\mu\epsilon\,E_x$ and $\partial^2E_x/\partial t^2 = -\omega^2E_x$, so the equation holds for every $\omega$. So does $\cos\big(\omega(t + z/v)\big)$. The sign inside will turn out to set the direction of travel.

**Any waveform, by factoring (slides).** Measure distance in units of travel time, $\tau\equiv z/v$; the slides write $\tau = z\sqrt{\mu_0\epsilon_0}$ in free space, so 1 m is $\tau = 3.34$ ns. The equation becomes $\partial^2E_x/\partial\tau^2 = \partial^2E_x/\partial t^2$, and the operator factors like $x^2 - y^2 = (x+y)(x-y)$:

$$
\Big(\frac{\partial}{\partial\tau}+\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\Big)\Big(\frac{\partial}{\partial\tau}-\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\Big)E_x = 0 .
$$

Each first-order factor is solved by any function of one combination. $\partial_\tau E_x = -\partial_tE_x$ is solved by $E_x = A\,f(t-\tau)$, because by the chain rule $\partial_\tau f(t-\tau) = -f'$ and $\partial_t f(t-\tau) = +f'$ (the check inked on slide 19). Likewise $\partial_\tau E_x = +\partial_tE_x$ is solved by $E_x = B\,g(t+\tau)$. Adding the two and putting $\tau = z/v$ back:

> [!key] d'Alembert's general solution
> $$
> E_x(z,t) = A\,f\Big(t-\frac zv\Big) + B\,g\Big(t+\frac zv\Big),
> $$
> where $f$ and $g$ are *any* waveforms and $A$, $B$ are constants. The first term travels toward $+z$ and the second toward $-z$ (the slide circles the "$-z$" with "+z direction" and the "$+z$" with "−z direction": the minus sign goes with travel toward $+z$). The notes call these the **d'Alembert wave solutions**.

> [!derivation]- Why every solution has this form, not just these two families
> Change variables to $\xi = t-\tau$ and $\zeta = t+\tau$. The chain rule gives $\partial_\tau = -\partial_\xi + \partial_\zeta$ and $\partial_t = \partial_\xi + \partial_\zeta$, so the two factors are $\partial_\tau + \partial_t = 2\partial_\zeta$ and $\partial_\tau - \partial_t = -2\partial_\xi$. The wave equation becomes
> $$
> \frac{\partial}{\partial\xi}\Big(\frac{\partial E_x}{\partial\zeta}\Big) = 0 .
> $$
> So $\partial E_x/\partial\zeta$ cannot depend on $\xi$: it is some function of $\zeta$ alone. Integrating in $\zeta$ gives $E_x = G(\zeta) + F(\xi)$, with $F$ an arbitrary function of $\xi$. This is d'Alembert's argument for the vibrating string, and the slide's "two first-order equations" is its operator shorthand.

**Why any waveform works (notes).** With constant $\mu$ and $\epsilon$ the equations are *linear and time-invariant*. A weighted sum of solutions is a solution, and so is a time-shifted one. A sum of cosines $\sum_nA_n\cos\big(\omega_n(t - z/v) + \theta_n\big)$ is therefore a solution, and it equals $f(t - z/v)$ with $f(t) = \sum_nA_n\cos(\omega_nt + \theta_n)$. By Fourier analysis (ECE 210), sums or integrals of this kind can build any practical signal, so $f$ can be any waveform. The key physical fact is that **every frequency travels at the same $v$**, because $v$ depends only on $\mu$ and $\epsilon$, taken here as constants that are the same at every frequency. So a pulse keeps its shape. A conducting medium breaks this (Lectures 22–23), and so does a dielectric whose $\epsilon$ changes with frequency, as in the Lorentz model of [[1-electrostatics/11-lorentz-drude-models-for-conductivity-and-susceptibility|Lecture 11]].

**What "travels" means.** $f(t - z/v)$ has the same value wherever $t - z/v$ has the same value. A feature seen at $z_0$ at time $t_0$ is found at $z_0 + v(t - t_0)$ at time $t$. The whole pattern slides toward $+z$ at speed $v$, rigidly. For $g(t + z/v)$ it is at $z_0 - v(t - t_0)$, sliding toward $-z$.

## 5. H from E: the intrinsic impedance

**From Faraday's law (notes).** For $E_x = f(t - z/v)$, $\partial E_x/\partial z = -f'/v$. Faraday's component equation then reads $-\mu\,\partial H_y/\partial t = -f'/v$. Integrating in time (and dropping the constant, a static field), $H_y = f/(\mu v)$. Since $\mu v = \mu/\sqrt{\mu\epsilon} = \sqrt{\mu/\epsilon}$, call this ratio $\eta$, so $H_y = f/\eta$. For $E_x = g(t + z/v)$ the $z$-derivative changes sign, and $H_y = -g/\eta$.

**From Ampère–Maxwell (slides).** $\partial H_y/\partial z = -\epsilon\,\partial E_x/\partial t = -\epsilon\,[A f' + B g']$. Integrate in $z$, using $\int f'(t - z/v)\,dz = -v f$ and $\int g'(t + z/v)\,dz = +v g$:

$$
H_y = \epsilon v\,\big[A f - B g\big] = \frac1\eta\big[A f - B g\big],\qquad\text{since }\ \epsilon v = \sqrt{\epsilon/\mu} = 1/\eta .
$$

The "constant" of a $z$-integration is a function of $t$ alone. Faraday's law then forces it to be constant in time as well, so it is a uniform static field and is set to zero. Slide 21 drops it without comment; the reason is the one slide 9 gives for $E_y$ ("we can turn off our source"). The two routes agree, and each satisfies *both* component equations (checked numerically with arbitrary $f$ and $g$; flipping the sign of $Bg$ breaks Ampère's law).

> [!key] The plane-wave pair and the intrinsic impedance
> $$
> \begin{gathered}
> E_x = A f\Big(t-\frac zv\Big) + B g\Big(t+\frac zv\Big),\qquad H_y = \frac1\eta\Big[A f\Big(t-\frac zv\Big) - B g\Big(t+\frac zv\Big)\Big],\\[6pt]
> \eta\equiv\sqrt{\frac\mu\epsilon} = \mu v = \frac{1}{\epsilon v}\ [\Omega],\qquad \eta_0 = \sqrt{\frac{\mu_0}{\epsilon_0}} = 376.7\ \Omega\approx120\pi\ \Omega .
> \end{gathered}
> $$
> For a single wave, $E_x/H_y = +\eta$ if it travels toward $+z$ and $-\eta$ if it travels toward $-z$. Units: (V/m)/(A/m) = Ω. With $\mathbf{B} = \mu\mathbf{H}$ the same statement reads $\lvert\mathbf{B}\rvert = \lvert\mathbf{E}\rvert/v$, a quick check in any problem.

The notes state the general rule in words. **$\mathbf{H}$ is $\mathbf{E}$ divided by $\eta$ and rotated by 90°, in the sense that makes $\mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{H}$ point the way the wave travels.** Conversely, $\mathbf{E}$ is $\eta\mathbf{H}$ rotated back. With $\hat u$ the direction of travel:

$$
\mathbf{H} = \frac1\eta\,\hat u\times\mathbf{E},\qquad \mathbf{E} = \eta\,\mathbf{H}\times\hat u .
$$

$\mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{H}$ is the **Poynting vector**. On this page it serves only as the direction rule (the slides' ink: "$\mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{H}\to$ propagation direction"). Its meaning as power per unit area, and the energy it carries, are the subject of Lecture 20 (Poynting's theorem).

> [!example] Example — a 1 V/m plane wave in vacuum
> A wave with $E = 1$ V/m travelling in free space has $H = E/\eta_0 = 2.65$ mA/m and $B = \mu_0H = E/c = 3.34$ nT. A field of a few nanotesla is tiny next to the Earth's roughly 50 μT, but that does not make the magnetic half of the wave negligible: $E$ and $B$ are measured in different units, so "small" has no meaning between them, and in a wave their ratio is fixed at $E/B = c$ (equivalently $E/H = \eta_0$). If $\mathbf{E} = \hat y$ V/m travels toward $-z$, then $\mathbf{H} = \hat u\times\mathbf{E}/\eta_0 = (-\hat z)\times\hat y/\eta_0 = +\hat x\,2.65$ mA/m. Check: $\hat y\times\hat x = -\hat z$ ✓.

## 6. Reading a wave

**Direction and speed from the argument.** A field that depends on position and time only through $t - \hat u\cdot\mathbf{r}/v$ travels along $+\hat u$ at speed $v$. Two ways to read an argument:

- Factor out the coefficient of $t$. For $f(at - bz)$ with $a, b>0$, $at - bz = a\,(t - z/(a/b))$, so the wave travels toward $+z$ at $a/b$. For $f(at + bz)$ it travels toward $-z$ at $a/b$.
- Look at the relative sign of the $t$ term and the position term. Opposite signs mean travel toward $+$ that axis; the same sign means travel toward $-$ it. Which term is written first does not matter, and neither does an overall sign. A function of $z - vt = -v(t - z/v)$ is also a function of $t - z/v$, and travels toward $+z$.

> [!example] Example (slides, challenge) — the velocities of three waves
> Find the velocities of $f = (0.05y - t)^2$, $g = u(t + 0.02x)$ and $h = \cos(2\pi10^8t - 2\pi z)$, all in metres and seconds. The options are
> (a) $0.05\hat y,\ -0.02\hat x,\ 10^8\hat z$; (b) $0.05\hat y,\ -0.02\hat x,\ -10^8\hat z$; (c) $20\hat y,\ -50\hat x,\ 10^8\hat z$; (d) $20\hat y,\ 50\hat x,\ 10^8\hat z$; (e) $20\hat y,\ -50\hat x,\ -10^8\hat z$.
> - $f = (0.05y - t)^2 = (t - y/20)^2$, a function of $t - y/20$: toward $+y$ at **20 m/s**. Its zero sits at $y = 20t$.
> - $g = u(t + x/50)$: same signs on $t$ and $x$, so toward $-x$ at **50 m/s**.
> - $h = \cos\big(2\pi10^8(t - z/10^8)\big)$: toward $+z$ at **$10^8$ m/s**.
>
> **Answer (c).** Each distractor encodes a trap. (a) and (b) quote $0.05$ and $0.02$, which are the *slownesses* $1/v$ in s/m, not speeds. (d) loses the sign of $g$. (b) and (e) flip $h$. And $f$ is written with the position term first: $(0.05y - t) = -(t - y/20)$ looks like "$-t$", but the overall sign does not matter, only the relative sign. Note also that $h$ travels at $10^8$ m/s, three times slower than light, so it is not in vacuum (§7).

**Fixed position or fixed time.** A problem may give the wave as a *record* at one place, $E(0,t)$, or as a *snapshot* at one instant, $E(z,0)$. Slide 25 converts between them:

> [!key] Shift identities
> $$
> \begin{gathered}
> \text{toward}\ {+z}:\quad E(z,t) = E\Big(0,\ t-\frac zv\Big) = E(z - vt,\ 0),\\[4pt]
> \text{toward}\ {-z}:\quad E(z,t) = E\Big(0,\ t+\frac zv\Big) = E(z + vt,\ 0).
> \end{gathered}
> $$
> In words: a probe at $z$ sees what the probe at $0$ saw $z/v$ earlier (for $+z$ travel) or will see $z/v$ later (for $-z$ travel). The snapshot at time $t$ is the $t = 0$ snapshot slid by $vt$ in the direction of travel.

**The mirror.** Put $t = 0$ in the identities. A $+z$ wave has snapshot $E(z,0) = E(0, -z/v)$: the record reversed left-to-right. The front of the pulse reaches a probe first, so it sits at early $t$ (the left) in the record, but for a $+z$ wave it leads on the right in space. A $-z$ wave has $E(z,0) = E(0, z/v)$: same left-to-right order, not mirrored. A symmetric pulse such as the notes' triangle hides this. An asymmetric one exposes it, as in rows 2 and 3 of the figure in §4.

> [!example] Example (slides) — a moving waveform
> A wave travelling toward $-z$ at 100 m/s is recorded at $z = 0$. The record $E(0,t)$ rises linearly from 0 to 1 over $0<t<1$ s, stays at 1 until 2 s, and falls linearly back to 0 at 4 s. Find $E$ at (a) $z = 200$ m, $t = 0.2$ s; (b) $z = -300$ m, $t = 3.4$ s; (c) $z = 100$ m, $t = 0.6$ s.
>
> For travel toward $-z$, $E(z,t) = E(0,\ t + z/v)$ with $z/v = z/(100\ \text{m/s})$.
> - (a) $t + z/v = 0.2 + 2 = 2.2$ s, on the falling ramp: $E = 1 - (2.2-2)/2 = $ **0.9**.
> - (b) $3.4 - 3 = 0.4$ s, on the rising ramp: **0.4**.
> - (c) $0.6 + 1 = 1.6$ s, on the plateau: **1.0**.
>
> With the wrong sign, $t - z/v$ gives $-1.8$, $6.4$ and $-0.4$ s. All three miss the pulse and the answers come out $0, 0, 0$. When every answer is zero, suspect the sign. In space the snapshot at $t = 0$ is $E(0, z/100)$: zero for $z\le0$, rising to 1 at $z = 100$ m, flat to 200 m, back to zero at 400 m. At $t = 1$ s the same shape sits 100 m further toward $-z$. Part (b) fits this picture: the point of the record at 0.4 s reaches $z = -300$ m after another 3 s.

## 7. In matter, and on a cable

Nothing in §§2–6 required vacuum. In a linear medium ([[3-maxwell-and-waves/17-magnetization-and-maxwells-equations-in-matter|Lecture 17]] for $\mu = \mu_r\mu_0$; [[1-electrostatics/09-static-fields-in-dielectric-media|Lecture 9]] for $\epsilon = \epsilon_r\epsilon_0$), the bound charges and currents are already inside $\mu$ and $\epsilon$, and only those two numbers enter:

> [!key] Speed and impedance in a medium
> $$
> v = \frac{c}{\sqrt{\mu_r\epsilon_r}},\qquad \eta = \eta_0\sqrt{\frac{\mu_r}{\epsilon_r}} .
> $$
> Both $\mu_r$ and $\epsilon_r$ slow the wave. $\epsilon_r$ lowers $\eta$ and $\mu_r$ raises it. The ratio $c/v = \sqrt{\mu_r\epsilon_r}$ is what optics calls the refractive index.

- $\epsilon_r = 4$, $\mu_r = 1$: $v = c/2 = 1.50\times10^8$ m/s and $\eta = \eta_0/2 = 188\ \Omega$ ($\approx60\pi$).
- The challenge wave $h$ above travels at $10^8$ m/s. In a non-magnetic medium that needs $\epsilon_r = (c/v)^2 = 8.99\approx9$, and then $\eta = \mu_0v = 40\pi\approx126\ \Omega$.
- Polyethylene, $\epsilon_r = 2.25$: $v = c/1.5 = 2.00\times10^8$ m/s and $\eta = 251\ \Omega$.

**The same numbers on a cable.** [[2-magnetostatics/15-inductance-magnetic-energy-and-the-potentials|Lecture 15]] found $\mathcal{L}\mathcal{C} = \mu\epsilon$ for any two-conductor line filled with one medium. So $1/\sqrt{\mathcal{L}\mathcal{C}} = 1/\sqrt{\mu\epsilon} = v$: a signal on a cable travels at exactly the plane-wave speed of its filling. The other combination, $\sqrt{\mathcal{L}/\mathcal{C}}$, plays the role on a line that $\eta$ plays in open space. It is $\eta$ times a geometric factor: $\sqrt{\mathcal{L}/\mathcal{C}} = (\eta/2\pi)\ln(b/a)$ for a coax and $\eta\,d/W$ for parallel plates. For the polyethylene coax of the [[problems/coax-inductance-and-the-lc-product|coax worked problem]], $\eta = 251\ \Omega$ times the factor $\ln(3.5)/2\pi = 0.199$ gives the 50 Ω found there. This is no coincidence: the field inside a coax is a TEM wave guided by the conductors. Unit 4 takes that up, with $\sqrt{\mathcal{L}/\mathcal{C}}$ as the characteristic impedance.

With $\sigma\neq0$ the current $\mathbf{J} = \sigma\mathbf{E}$ adds a term $\mu\sigma\,\partial\mathbf{E}/\partial t$ to the wave equation, and waves decay and change shape as they travel. That is Lectures 22–23.

## 8. Summary

- Source-free, homogeneous, lossless region: $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{E} = \nabla\cdot\mathbf{H} = 0$, $\nabla\times\mathbf{E} = -\mu\,\partial_t\mathbf{H}$, $\nabla\times\mathbf{H} = \epsilon\,\partial_t\mathbf{E}$. With no sources anywhere, static fields vanish; time-varying ones need not. The recursion $\cdots\to\mathbf{H}\to\mathbf{E}\to\mathbf{H}\to\cdots$ is the wave.
- Curl of Faraday + identity + $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{E} = 0$ + Ampère–Maxwell: $\nabla^2\mathbf{E} = \mu\epsilon\,\partial_t^2\mathbf{E}$, and the same for $\mathbf{H}$. This needs constant $\mu,\epsilon$, $\rho = \mathbf{J} = 0$, and Cartesian components. Without $\partial\mathbf{D}/\partial t$ it would be $\nabla^2\mathbf{E} = 0$, with no waves.
- Planar case: $\mathbf{E} = \hat xE_x(z,t)$, $\mathbf{H} = \hat yH_y(z,t)$, with $\partial_zE_x = -\mu\,\partial_tH_y$ and $-\partial_zH_y = \epsilon\,\partial_tE_x$. The notes assume this field; the slides derive it from a current sheet by symmetry. A $z$-polarized wave along $z$ is impossible ($\nabla\cdot\mathbf{E}\neq0$).
- $\partial_z^2E_x = \mu\epsilon\,\partial_t^2E_x$; general solution $Af(t - z/v) + Bg(t + z/v)$, any waveforms, $v = 1/\sqrt{\mu\epsilon}$, $c = 2.998\times10^8$ m/s. Minus sign ↔ travel toward $+z$.
- $H_y = (Af - Bg)/\eta$, $\eta = \sqrt{\mu/\epsilon} = \mu v$, $\eta_0\approx120\pi\approx377\ \Omega$. $\mathbf{H} = \hat u\times\mathbf{E}/\eta$: divide by $\eta$, rotate so that $\mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{H}$ points along the travel; $\lvert\mathbf{B}\rvert = \lvert\mathbf{E}\rvert/v$.
- Reading waves: $t - \hat u\cdot\mathbf{r}/v$; $E(z,t) = E(0, t\mp z/v) = E(z\mp vt, 0)$; a $+z$ snapshot is the record mirrored, a $-z$ snapshot is not.
- In matter: $v = c/\sqrt{\mu_r\epsilon_r}$, $\eta = \eta_0\sqrt{\mu_r/\epsilon_r}$. On a cable: $1/\sqrt{\mathcal{L}\mathcal{C}} = v$, and $\sqrt{\mathcal{L}/\mathcal{C}}$ is $\eta$ times geometry.

> [!exam] On exams
> Seven things come up.
> 1. **Read direction and speed** off an argument such as $t + 0.02x$ or $(0.05y - t)$, as in slide 24. Watch for the slowness-versus-speed and term-order traps.
> 2. **$\mathbf{H}$ (or $\mathbf{B}$) from $\mathbf{E}$, or the reverse**, with $\eta$ and the $\mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{H}$ rule, including $y$-polarized waves and waves travelling toward $-z$. Write the cross product out; do not trust a remembered picture.
> 3. **Moving pulses**: a pulse given at one instant (or at one place) and a velocity; find it at another time, or what a probe records. SP18 Exam 2 #4 is exactly this, and the worked problem [[problems/a-pulse-on-the-move|A pulse on the move]] is a re-parameterized version.
> 4. **The derivation**, by either route, with each assumption named. Know where the displacement current enters.
> 5. **Why a $z$-polarized wave cannot travel along $z$** ($\nabla\cdot\mathbf{E}\neq0$).
> 6. **The constants**: $c\approx3\times10^8$ m/s $= 300$ m/µs, $\eta_0\approx120\pi\approx377\ \Omega$.
> 7. **$v$ and $\eta$ in a dielectric**, or $\epsilon_r$ from a measured speed. A wave slower than $c$ is in matter. That does not make it lossy: a lossless dielectric slows a wave without shrinking it.
>
> The current-sheet problems (SP18 Exam 2 #5: the coefficient and direction of $\mathbf{H}$ on both sides of a sheet) need Lecture 19's amplitude result.
