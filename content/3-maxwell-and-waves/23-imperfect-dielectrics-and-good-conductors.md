> [!abstract] In one breath
> [[3-maxwell-and-waves/22-phasor-maxwell-equations-and-damped-waves|Lecture 22]] showed that a medium with conductivity $\sigma$ turns every plane wave into a damped one, $e^{-\alpha z}\cos(\omega t - \beta z)$, with $\gamma = \alpha + j\beta = \sqrt{j\omega\mu(\sigma + j\omega\epsilon)}$ and a complex $\eta$. This lecture makes those formulas usable. Ampère's law carries two currents driven by the same field, the conduction current $\sigma\tilde{\mathbf{E}}$ and the displacement current $j\omega\epsilon\tilde{\mathbf{E}}$. Their ratio $\sigma/\omega\epsilon$, the **loss tangent**, decides everything. When it is small the medium is an **imperfect dielectric**: the wave travels almost as if $\sigma$ were zero and loses power slowly, at $\alpha\approx(\sigma/2)\sqrt{\mu/\epsilon}$. That rate is set by energy conservation, because the Joule heat $\tfrac12\sigma\lvert\tilde{\mathbf{E}}\rvert^2$ is drawn from the Poynting flux. When it is large the medium is a **good conductor**: $\alpha = \beta = 1/\delta$ with the **skin depth** $\delta = 1/\sqrt{\pi f\mu\sigma}$, and $\eta = (1+j)/(\sigma\delta)$ is tiny and has a 45° phase. The field is all but gone within one wavelength, $2\pi\delta$. The power always falls twice as fast as the field, as $e^{-2\alpha z}$. Whether a medium is a conductor depends on the frequency. Seawater is a good conductor below about 90 MHz and a dielectric above about 9 GHz, with $\delta = 7.96$ m at 1 kHz and 1.3 cm at 1 GHz. That is why submarines are reached at very low frequencies, at the price of very slow data and enormous antennas. Copper has $\delta = 8.5$ mm at 60 Hz and 2.1 µm at 1 GHz.

## 1. When does a medium count as a conductor?

In electrostatics the question had a simple answer: a material with free charge is a conductor, and in equilibrium it has no field inside ([[1-electrostatics/08-conductors-dielectrics-and-polarization|Lecture 8]]). For a wave the answer depends on the frequency. Write Ampère's law in phasor form for a medium with conductivity $\sigma$, as [[3-maxwell-and-waves/22-phasor-maxwell-equations-and-damped-waves|Lecture 22]] does:

$$
\nabla\times\tilde{\mathbf{H}} = \underbrace{\sigma\tilde{\mathbf{E}}}_{\text{conduction}} + \underbrace{j\omega\epsilon\tilde{\mathbf{E}}}_{\text{displacement}} = (\sigma + j\omega\epsilon)\,\tilde{\mathbf{E}} .
$$

The same field drives both currents. The conduction current is in phase with $\tilde{\mathbf{E}}$, so it dissipates power: this is the Joule heating $\mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{E}$ of [[1-electrostatics/10-capacitance-and-conductance|Lecture 10]]. The displacement current leads $\tilde{\mathbf{E}}$ by 90°, so on average it dissipates nothing; it stores energy and gives it back each cycle. Which current wins is a question of size.

> [!key] The loss tangent, and the four regimes
> $$
> \frac{\lvert\sigma\tilde{\mathbf{E}}\rvert}{\lvert j\omega\epsilon\tilde{\mathbf{E}}\rvert} = \frac{\sigma}{\omega\epsilon}
> $$
> is dimensionless, with $\omega = 2\pi f$ and $\epsilon$ the medium's own permittivity.
> - $\sigma = 0$: **perfect dielectric**, the lossless medium of Lectures 18–21.
> - $\sigma/\omega\epsilon\ll1$, in practice below 0.1: **imperfect dielectric** (the notes of Lecture 22 also say "lousy conductor"). The displacement current dominates.
> - $\sigma/\omega\epsilon\gg1$, in practice above 10: **good conductor**. The conduction current dominates.
> - $\sigma\to\infty$: **perfect conductor**, with no field inside ([[3-maxwell-and-waves/16-charge-conservation-displacement-current-and-maxwells-equations|Lecture 16]]).
>
> Between 0.1 and 10 the approximations lose accuracy (the good-conductor ones fastest, §2); use the exact formulas of Lecture 22. The ratio is called the **loss tangent** because it is the tangent of the angle between the displacement current and the total current (the [[concepts/loss-tangent|loss-tangent page]] describes the phasor diagram). The notes use the ratio without naming it; Rao and the exams use the name.

**The crossover frequency.** The two currents are equal when $\omega\epsilon = \sigma$, at

$$
f_c = \frac{\sigma}{2\pi\epsilon}.
$$

More than a decade below $f_c$ the medium is a good conductor; more than a decade above it, an imperfect dielectric. Two earlier results say the same thing in other words.

- **The relaxation time of [[1-electrostatics/08-conductors-dielectrics-and-polarization|Lecture 8]].** Charge placed inside a conductor decays as $e^{-t/\tau_r}$ with $\tau_r = \epsilon/\sigma$ (Lecture 8 writes it $\tau$; on this page $\tau$ is the phase of $\eta$). So $\sigma/\omega\epsilon = 1/(\omega\tau_r)$, and $f_c = 1/(2\pi\tau_r)$. A medium is a good conductor when its free charges can rearrange, and cancel the field, many times within one period of the wave. Seawater has $\tau_r = 0.18$ ns: at 1 kHz the charges have about a millisecond each cycle, plenty of time; at 10 GHz a period is 0.1 ns, too short.
- **The leaky capacitor of [[1-electrostatics/10-capacitance-and-conductance#6-conductance-the-leaky-capacitor|Lecture 10]].** A capacitor filled with the medium is a capacitance $C$ in parallel with a conductance $G = (\sigma/\epsilon)C$. Its current ratio is $G/\omega C = \sigma/\omega\epsilon$, the same number. Lecture 10 already said that such an element is "resistor-like for $\omega\ll\sigma/\epsilon$ and capacitor-like for $\omega\gg\sigma/\epsilon$". A wave sees the medium the same way.

Here are typical handbook values and the crossover frequencies they give (constant $\sigma$ and $\epsilon$, as in the notes):

| the notes' Example 1 medium | $10^{-3}$ | 4 | 4.5 MHz | 450 kHz | 45 MHz |

> [!example] Example — seawater at four frequencies
> With $\sigma = 4$ S/m and $\epsilon = 81\epsilon_0$, $\sigma/\omega\epsilon = 8.9\times10^{5}$ at 1 kHz, 888 at 1 MHz, 0.89 at 1 GHz and 0.089 at 10 GHz. So seawater is a good conductor at 1 kHz and 1 MHz, in between at 1 GHz, and an imperfect dielectric at 10 GHz. The quick way without a calculator uses $\epsilon_0\approx10^{-9}/36\pi$ F/m:
> $$
> \begin{gathered}
> \frac{\sigma}{\omega\epsilon} = \frac{\sigma\cdot36\pi\times10^{9}}{2\pi f\,\epsilon_r} = \frac{18\times10^{9}\,\sigma}{f\,\epsilon_r},\\[4pt]
> \text{at 1 kHz:}\quad \frac{18\times10^{9}\times4}{10^{3}\times81} = 8.9\times10^{5}.
> \end{gathered}
> $$

> [!trap] Classifying a medium
> - Use the medium's own $\epsilon$. With $\epsilon_0$ in place of $81\epsilon_0$, seawater's ratio comes out 81 times too large. That makes no difference at 1 kHz but decides the answer near the crossover.
> - $\omega = 2\pi f$, not $f$. Dropping the $2\pi$ moves the ratio by a factor of 6.3.
> - Never classify by name. Seawater is a good conductor at 1 kHz and a dielectric at 10 GHz; the dry earth of the table is a good conductor at 100 Hz.
> - Real $\sigma$ and $\epsilon$ change with frequency ([[1-electrostatics/11-lorentz-drude-models-for-conductivity-and-susceptibility|Lecture 11]]), so $f_c$ is a guide, not a sharp boundary. Water's own permittivity falls in the microwave range (ITU-R P.527 models it as frequency dependent), and copper's $\sigma$ is no longer constant above about $10^{13}$ Hz.

## 2. The notes' table, and the one number behind it

The notes open Lecture 23 with a table that collects the regimes of Lecture 22. Here it is in this site's notation, with $\eta = \lvert\eta\rvert e^{j\tau}$, $\lambda = 2\pi/\beta$ and $\delta = 1/\alpha$:

| regime | condition | $\alpha$ [Np/m] | $\beta$ [rad/m] | $\lvert\eta\rvert$ [Ω] | $\tau = \angle\eta$ | $\lambda$ | $\delta = 1/\alpha$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| perfect dielectric | $\sigma = 0$ | $0$ | $\omega\sqrt{\mu\epsilon}$ | $\sqrt{\mu/\epsilon}$ | $0$ | $2\pi/\omega\sqrt{\mu\epsilon}$ | $\infty$ |
| imperfect dielectric | $\sigma/\omega\epsilon\ll1$ | ${\approx\dfrac\sigma2\sqrt{\mu/\epsilon}}$ | ${\approx\omega\sqrt{\mu\epsilon}}$ | ${\approx\sqrt{\mu/\epsilon}}$ | ${\approx\dfrac{\sigma}{2\omega\epsilon}}$ | ${\approx2\pi/\omega\sqrt{\mu\epsilon}}$ | ${\approx\dfrac2\sigma\sqrt{\epsilon/\mu}}$ |
| good conductor | $\sigma/\omega\epsilon\gg1$ | ${\approx\sqrt{\pi f\mu\sigma}}$ | ${\approx\sqrt{\pi f\mu\sigma}}$ | ${\approx\sqrt{\omega\mu/\sigma}}$ | ${\approx45^\circ}$ | ${\approx2\pi\delta}$ | ${\approx1/\sqrt{\pi f\mu\sigma}}$ |
| perfect conductor | $\sigma\to\infty$ | $\infty$ | $\infty$ | $0$ | — | $0$ | $0$ |

The notes write the imperfect-dielectric $\alpha$ as $\beta\cdot\tfrac12\sigma/\omega\epsilon$, which is the same thing, and they write $\epsilon_o$, $\mu_o$, $\eta_o$ for this site's $\epsilon_0$, $\mu_0$, $\eta_0$. Around the table the notes recall how a wave in any of these media is written. An $x$-polarized wave has $\tilde{\mathbf{E}} = \hat x E_0e^{\mp\alpha z}e^{\mp j\beta z}$ and $\tilde{\mathbf{H}} = \pm\hat y\,(E_0/\eta)\,e^{\mp\alpha z}e^{\mp j\beta z}$, and its phase velocity is $v_p = \omega/\beta$, which can now depend on frequency. It travels along $\tilde{\mathbf{E}}\times\tilde{\mathbf{H}}^*$, and the time-average power per unit area is $\langle\mathbf{S}\rangle = \tfrac12\text{Re}\{\tilde{\mathbf{E}}\times\tilde{\mathbf{H}}^*\}$ ([[3-maxwell-and-waves/21-monochromatic-waves-and-phasor-notation|Lecture 21]]).

**Where the entries come from.** Lecture 22 derives the two limits by binomial expansion, in its overview of the four regimes. A polar-form route gives both limits and the exact values at once. Factor $j\omega\epsilon$ out of $\sigma + j\omega\epsilon$:

$$
\begin{gathered}
\gamma = j\omega\sqrt{\mu\epsilon}\,\big(1 - jp\big)^{1/2},\\[4pt]
\eta = \sqrt{\frac\mu\epsilon}\,\big(1 - jp\big)^{-1/2},\qquad p\equiv\frac{\sigma}{\omega\epsilon}.
\end{gathered}
$$

Apart from the lossless scales $\omega\sqrt{\mu\epsilon}$ and $\sqrt{\mu/\epsilon}$, everything depends on the single number $p$. In polar form $1 - jp = \sqrt{1+p^2}\,e^{-j\theta}$, where $\theta = \tan^{-1}p$ is the **loss angle**, between 0 and 90°.

> [!key] Exact forms for any medium (this site's packaging of Lecture 22)
> $$
> \begin{gathered}
> \alpha = \omega\sqrt{\mu\epsilon}\,(1+p^2)^{1/4}\sin\frac\theta2,\\[4pt]
> \beta = \omega\sqrt{\mu\epsilon}\,(1+p^2)^{1/4}\cos\frac\theta2,\\[4pt]
> \lvert\eta\rvert = \sqrt{\frac\mu\epsilon}\,(1+p^2)^{-1/4},\\[4pt]
> \tau = \angle\eta = \frac\theta2 = \frac12\tan^{-1}\frac{\sigma}{\omega\epsilon},\qquad \frac\alpha\beta = \tan\tau .
> \end{gathered}
> $$
> Three facts to remember. The phase of $\eta$ is exactly half the loss angle, so it lies between 0 and 45°. Since $\alpha/\beta = \tan\tau\le1$, $\alpha$ can never exceed $\beta$. And $\lvert\gamma\rvert\lvert\eta\rvert = \omega\mu$, from $\gamma\eta = j\omega\mu$ (Lecture 22).

> [!derivation]- The polar-form derivation
> $(1 - jp)^{1/2} = (1+p^2)^{1/4}e^{-j\theta/2}$, taking the root with positive real part. Then
> $$
> \begin{gathered}
> \gamma = j\omega\sqrt{\mu\epsilon}\,(1+p^2)^{1/4}e^{-j\theta/2}\\[4pt]
> = \omega\sqrt{\mu\epsilon}\,(1+p^2)^{1/4}\,e^{j(\pi/2 - \theta/2)},
> \end{gathered}
> $$
> whose real part is $\omega\sqrt{\mu\epsilon}(1+p^2)^{1/4}\cos(\pi/2 - \theta/2) = \ldots\sin(\theta/2)$, which is $\alpha$, and whose imaginary part is $\ldots\cos(\theta/2)$, which is $\beta$. Since $0\le\theta<\pi/2$, $\beta>0$ and $\alpha\ge0$ ($\alpha = 0$ only when $\sigma = 0$), so $e^{-\gamma z}$ travels toward $+z$ and decays. For $\eta$, $(1 - jp)^{-1/2} = (1+p^2)^{-1/4}e^{+j\theta/2}$, so $\lvert\eta\rvert = \sqrt{\mu/\epsilon}\,(1+p^2)^{-1/4}$ and $\tau = \theta/2$. Dividing, $\alpha/\beta = \tan(\theta/2) = \tan\tau$. (The notes of Lecture 22 write the small-$p$ phase as $\tan^{-1}(\sigma/2\omega\epsilon)$; it agrees with $\tfrac12\tan^{-1}(\sigma/\omega\epsilon)$ to first order in $p$.)

**The two limits fall out.**

- **$p\ll1$.** Then $\theta\approx p$ and $(1+p^2)^{1/4}\approx1$. So $\alpha\approx\omega\sqrt{\mu\epsilon}\cdot p/2 = (\sigma/2)\sqrt{\mu/\epsilon}$, $\beta\approx\omega\sqrt{\mu\epsilon}$, $\lvert\eta\rvert\approx\sqrt{\mu/\epsilon}$ and $\tau\approx p/2 = \sigma/2\omega\epsilon$. This is the table's second row. Nothing changes noticeably except that the wave now decays.
- **$p\gg1$.** Then $\theta\to90^\circ$ and $(1+p^2)^{1/4}\approx\sqrt p$. So $\alpha\approx\beta\approx\omega\sqrt{\mu\epsilon}\,\sqrt p\,/\sqrt2 = \sqrt{\omega\mu\sigma/2} = \sqrt{\pi f\mu\sigma}$, $\lvert\eta\rvert\approx\sqrt{\mu/\epsilon}/\sqrt p = \sqrt{\omega\mu/\sigma}$, and $\tau\to45^\circ$. This is the third row. Note that $\epsilon$ has dropped out.

**How small is "small"?** At $p = 0.1$ the imperfect-dielectric formulas are within about 0.3 % ($\alpha$ and $\beta$ to 0.12 %, $\lvert\eta\rvert$ to 0.25 %, $\tau$ to 0.33 %), and they degrade slowly: at $p = 0.3$, $\alpha$ is still within 1.1 %. At $p = 10$ the good-conductor $\alpha$ is 5 % too high, $\beta$ 5 % too low, and the true $\tau$ is 42.1° rather than 45°; at $p = 3$ that $\alpha$ is already 18 % high. At $p = 1$ both sets fail: the imperfect-dielectric $\alpha$ is 10 % high and the good-conductor $\alpha$ 55 % high. That is why the in-practice thresholds are 0.1 and 10.

**The attenuation saturates.** In the imperfect-dielectric regime $\alpha = (\sigma/2)\sqrt{\mu/\epsilon}$ does not depend on frequency, so above $10f_c$ raising the frequency no longer increases the loss per metre (per wavelength the loss even falls, because $\lambda$ shrinks while $\alpha$ stays put). In the good-conductor regime $\alpha = \sqrt{\pi f\mu\sigma}$ grows as $\sqrt f$, so a lower frequency always penetrates further. For seawater the plateau is 84 Np/m, a penetration depth of 1.2 cm. (Rao Problem 5.26 puts the comparison as a ratio: the good-conductor $\alpha$ is the plateau value times $\sqrt{2\omega\epsilon/\sigma}$.)

> [!example] Example — classifying a medium from a measured wavelength and penetration depth (this site's numbers)
> A 200 MHz plane wave in a non-magnetic medium has wavelength $\lambda = 0.5$ m and penetration depth $\delta = 20$ m. Is the medium an imperfect dielectric or a good conductor? Find $v_p$, $\epsilon_r$, $\lvert\eta\rvert$ and $\sigma$.
> - $\beta = 2\pi/\lambda = 4\pi = 12.6$ rad/m and $\alpha = 1/\delta = 0.05$ Np/m. Since $\alpha/\beta = \tan\tau = 0.004$, we have $\tau = 0.23^\circ$ and $p = \tan2\tau = 0.008\ll1$: an **imperfect dielectric**. A good conductor would have $\alpha = \beta$, and its field would be gone within about one wavelength; here it takes 40 wavelengths to fall to $1/e$.
> - $v_p = \lambda f = 0.5\times2\times10^{8} = 10^{8}$ m/s, so $\epsilon_r = (c/v_p)^2 = 9$, using $\beta\approx\omega\sqrt{\mu_0\epsilon}$.
> - $\lvert\eta\rvert\approx\eta_0/3 = 40\pi\approx126\ \Omega$.
> - From $\alpha = (\sigma/2)\lvert\eta\rvert$: $\sigma = 2\alpha/\lvert\eta\rvert = 0.1/(40\pi) = 8.0\times10^{-4}$ S/m. Check: $\sigma/\omega\epsilon = 8.0\times10^{-4}/(2\pi\cdot2\times10^{8}\cdot9\epsilon_0) = 0.008$, consistent.
>
> The same reasoning answers the Summer 2017 HE3 question that gives $\lambda$ and $\delta$ (with other numbers).

## 3. Imperfect dielectrics: slow decay, and where the power goes

The notes' first example is a wave in a slightly lossy dielectric. It is worth doing in full, because every damped-wave problem has the same steps.

> [!example] Example 1 (notes) — a 1 GHz wave in an imperfect dielectric
> $\tilde{\mathbf{E}} = \hat y\,2e^{-\alpha z}e^{-j\beta z}$ V/m travels toward $+z$ in a medium with $\epsilon = 4\epsilon_0$, $\mu = \mu_0$, $\sigma = 10^{-3}$ S/m, at $\omega = 2\pi\times10^{9}$ rad/s. Find $\tilde{\mathbf{H}}$ and $\langle\mathbf{S}\rangle$, and evaluate $\langle\mathbf{S}\rangle$ at $z = 0$ and $z = 10$ m.
>
> **Classify.** $\sigma/\omega\epsilon = 10^{-3}\cdot36\pi\times10^{9}/(2\pi\times10^{9}\cdot4) = 4.5\times10^{-3}\ll1$: an imperfect dielectric. So $\tau\approx\sigma/2\omega\epsilon = 2.25\times10^{-3}$ rad $= 0.13^\circ$, $\lvert\eta\rvert\approx\sqrt{\mu_0/4\epsilon_0} = \eta_0/2\approx60\pi = 188\ \Omega$, $\alpha\approx(\sigma/2)\lvert\eta\rvert = 30\pi\times10^{-3} = 0.094$ Np/m and $\beta\approx\omega\sqrt{\mu_0\cdot4\epsilon_0} = 41.9$ rad/m, so $\lambda = 15$ cm.
>
> **Magnetic field.** Divide by $\eta$ and rotate so that $\mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{H}$ points along $+\hat z$: $\hat z\times\hat y = -\hat x$, so
> $$
> \begin{gathered}
> \tilde{\mathbf{H}} = -\hat x\,\frac{2}{\eta}\,e^{-\alpha z}e^{-j\beta z}\\[4pt]
> = -\hat x\,\frac{2}{\lvert\eta\rvert}\,e^{-\alpha z}e^{-j\beta z}e^{-j\tau}\ \ \text{A/m}.
> \end{gathered}
> $$
> Check: $\hat y\times(-\hat x) = +\hat z$ ✓. In the time domain (real cosines, $E_0$ real),
> $$
> \begin{gathered}
> \mathbf{E} = \hat y\,2e^{-\alpha z}\cos(\omega t - \beta z)\ \text{V/m},\\[4pt]
> \mathbf{H} = -\hat x\,\frac{2}{\lvert\eta\rvert}e^{-\alpha z}\cos(\omega t - \beta z - \tau)\ \text{A/m},
> \end{gathered}
> $$
> with $2/\lvert\eta\rvert = 10.6$ mA/m. $\mathbf{H}$ lags $\mathbf{E}$ by only 0.13°.
>
> **Power.**
> $$
> \begin{gathered}
> \langle\mathbf{S}\rangle = \tfrac12\text{Re}\{\tilde{\mathbf{E}}\times\tilde{\mathbf{H}}^*\}\\[4pt]
> = \tfrac12\text{Re}\Big\{\hat y\times(-\hat x)\,\frac{4}{\lvert\eta\rvert}e^{-2\alpha z}e^{+j\tau}\Big\}\\[4pt]
> = \hat z\,\frac{2}{\lvert\eta\rvert}\cos\tau\,e^{-2\alpha z}\ \ \text{W/m}^2 .
> \end{gathered}
> $$
> At $z = 0$: $2/(60\pi) = 1/(30\pi) = 10.6$ mW/m². At $z = 10$ m: the factor is $e^{-2\times0.094\times10} = e^{-0.6\pi} = 0.15$, so $\langle\mathbf{S}\rangle = 0.15/(30\pi)\approx1.6$ mW/m². Only 15 % of the power is left after 10 m. In decibels the loss is $8.686\,\alpha = 0.82$ dB per metre, 8.2 dB over the 10 m, and the field penetration depth is $\delta = 1/\alpha = 10.6$ m.

The notes' general result is worth stating once for all damped plane waves:

> [!key] Time-average power in a lossy medium
> For $\tilde{\mathbf{E}} = \hat xE_0e^{-\alpha z}e^{-j\beta z}$ and $\tilde{\mathbf{H}} = \hat y\,(E_0/\eta)\,e^{-\alpha z}e^{-j\beta z}$, with $E_0$ possibly complex,
> $$
> \begin{gathered}
> \langle\mathbf{S}\rangle = \tfrac12\text{Re}\{\tilde{\mathbf{E}}\times\tilde{\mathbf{H}}^*\}\\[4pt]
> = \hat z\,\frac{\lvert E_0\rvert^2}{2\lvert\eta\rvert}\,\cos\tau\;e^{-2\alpha z}\quad[\text{W/m}^2].
> \end{gathered}
> $$
> - The **power** falls as $e^{-2\alpha z}$: to $1/e$ at $z = \delta/2$, while the field falls to $1/e$ at $z = \delta$.
> - In **decibels** the field and the power fall at the same rate, $10\log_{10}e^{2\alpha z} = 20\log_{10}e^{\alpha z} = 8.686\,\alpha z$ dB. One neper is 8.686 dB.
> - $\cos\tau$ works like a power factor: it is 1 in a lossless medium and $1/\sqrt2$ in a good conductor.

**Where does the lost power go?** Into the medium, as heat. Poynting's theorem ([[3-maxwell-and-waves/20-poynting-theorem-and-monochromatic-waves|Lecture 20]], [[concepts/poynting-theorem]]) says that, averaged over a cycle, the net power flowing into any small volume equals the power the field delivers to the charges inside it: $\nabla\cdot\langle\mathbf{S}\rangle = -\langle\mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{E}\rangle$. The stored energy has no net rate of change over a full period, so it drops out. Here $\langle\mathbf{S}\rangle$ depends on $z$ only, and $\mathbf{J} = \sigma\mathbf{E}$, so

$$
\begin{gathered}
-\frac{d\langle S_z\rangle}{dz} = 2\alpha\,\frac{\lvert E_0\rvert^2\cos\tau}{2\lvert\eta\rvert}\,e^{-2\alpha z}\\[4pt]
= \langle\mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{E}\rangle = \tfrac12\sigma\lvert E_0\rvert^2e^{-2\alpha z}\\[4pt]
\Longrightarrow\quad \alpha = \frac{\sigma\lvert\eta\rvert}{2\cos\tau}.
\end{gathered}
$$

This is exact for any medium; it is $\text{Re}\{\gamma/\eta\} = \sigma$ of Lecture 22 in disguise. For an imperfect dielectric, $\cos\tau\approx1$ and $\lvert\eta\rvert\approx\sqrt{\mu/\epsilon}$, which gives the table's $\alpha = (\sigma/2)\sqrt{\mu/\epsilon}$. So that entry is energy conservation: the power flux decreases at the fractional rate $2\alpha\approx\sigma\lvert\eta\rvert$ per metre, and the difference becomes Joule heat. In Example 1, at $z = 0$, the heating is $\tfrac12\sigma\lvert E_0\rvert^2 = \tfrac12\times10^{-3}\times4 = 2.0$ mW/m³, and $2\alpha\langle S_z\rangle = 2\times0.094\times10.6$ mW/m² $= 2.0$ mW/m³ ✓. This is the time-averaged twin of the Joule heating $\mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{E}$ of a resistor ([[1-electrostatics/10-capacitance-and-conductance|Lecture 10]]).

The notes add two remarks. First, heating by an absorbed wave is exactly what a microwave oven is designed to do. (In food, much of the loss comes from water molecules whose polarization lags the field, rather than from $\sigma$. The loss tangent lumps both mechanisms together; see [[concepts/loss-tangent]].) Second, for communication the same loss is waste: the signal heats the medium instead of reaching the receiver. That leads to the notes' next two examples.

## 4. Good conductors: skin depth, $\eta = (1+j)/(\sigma\delta)$ and the 45° lag

When $\sigma\gg\omega\epsilon$, drop $j\omega\epsilon$ next to $\sigma$. Then $\gamma\approx\sqrt{j\omega\mu\sigma}$ and $\eta\approx\sqrt{j\omega\mu/\sigma}$. With $\sqrt j = e^{j\pi/4} = (1+j)/\sqrt2$, everything can be written with one length.

> [!key] A plane wave in a good conductor
> $$
> \begin{gathered}
> \delta = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi f\mu\sigma}} = \sqrt{\frac{2}{\omega\mu\sigma}},\\[4pt]
> \gamma = \frac{1+j}{\delta}\quad(\alpha = \beta = 1/\delta),\\[4pt]
> \eta = \frac{1+j}{\sigma\delta} = \sqrt{\frac{\omega\mu}{\sigma}}\;e^{j\pi/4},\\[4pt]
> \lambda = 2\pi\delta,\qquad v_p = \frac\omega\beta = \omega\delta .
> \end{gathered}
> $$
> Valid for $\sigma/\omega\epsilon\gg1$. $\delta$ is the **skin depth**, the distance in which the field falls to $1/e$ ([[concepts/skin-depth]]). For $\tilde{\mathbf{E}} = \hat xE_0e^{-z/\delta}e^{-jz/\delta}$ with $E_0$ real, the time-domain fields are
> $$
> \begin{gathered}
> \mathbf{E} = \hat x\,E_0e^{-z/\delta}\cos\Big(\omega t - \frac z\delta\Big),\\[4pt]
> \mathbf{H} = \hat y\,\frac{\sigma\delta E_0}{\sqrt2}\,e^{-z/\delta}\cos\Big(\omega t - \frac z\delta - \frac\pi4\Big),
> \end{gathered}
> $$
> and $\langle\mathbf{S}\rangle = \hat z\,\dfrac{\sigma\delta E_0^2}{4}\,e^{-2z/\delta}$.

To check $\eta$, note that $\sqrt{\omega\mu/2\sigma} = \tfrac1\sigma\sqrt{\omega\mu\sigma/2} = 1/(\sigma\delta)$. The power line is the general $\lvert E_0\rvert^2\cos\tau/(2\lvert\eta\rvert)$ with $\cos\tau = 1/\sqrt2$ and $\lvert\eta\rvert = \sqrt2/(\sigma\delta)$.

What the formulas say:

1. **The wave dies within a wavelength.** With $\alpha = \beta$, the field falls by $e^{-2\pi}\approx1/535$, that is 54.6 dB, over each wavelength. A wave in a good conductor never travels "several wavelengths".
2. **It is slow, and its speed depends on frequency.** $v_p = \omega\delta = \sqrt{2\omega/\mu\sigma}$ grows as $\sqrt f$. Seawater at 1 kHz has $\lambda = 2\pi\delta = 50$ m and $v_p = 5\times10^{4}$ m/s. A lossless medium with the same $\epsilon_r = 81$ would give $c/9 = 3.3\times10^{7}$ m/s. Copper at 1 MHz has $\lambda = 0.42$ mm and $v_p = 415$ m/s.
3. **H lags E by 45°.** That is one-eighth of a period in time. The tiny $\lvert\eta\rvert$ makes $\mathbf{H}$ large: $\lvert\mathbf{H}\rvert/\lvert\mathbf{E}\rvert = 1/\lvert\eta\rvert$ is 22.5 (A/m) per (V/m) in seawater at 1 kHz, against $1/377$ in air. So the stored energy in a good conductor is almost all magnetic. The time-average electric and magnetic energy densities are in the ratio $\epsilon\lvert\eta\rvert^2/\mu = 1/\sqrt{1+p^2}\approx\omega\epsilon/\sigma$, which is $1.1\times10^{-6}$ for seawater at 1 kHz. In a lossless medium they are equal.
4. **The perfect conductor is the limit.** As $\sigma\to\infty$, $\delta\to0$ and $\eta\to0$: no field gets in at all. What remains is a surface current that carries the jump in $\mathbf{H}$ ([[3-maxwell-and-waves/16-charge-conservation-displacement-current-and-maxwells-equations|Lecture 16]]).

> [!intuition] Why the good-conductor η looks like a resistance
> The real part $1/(\sigma\delta)$ is the DC resistance of a square slab of the metal, one skin depth thick, measured between two opposite edges: $R = l/(\sigma A)$ from [[1-electrostatics/08-conductors-dielectrics-and-polarization|Lecture 8]] with length $l$ and cross-section $A = l\delta$. The induced current flows in a surface layer about $\delta$ thick, and the wave sees it as a resistive sheet. The power absorbed per unit area is $\tfrac12\text{Re}\{\eta\}\lvert\tilde H_0\rvert^2 = \lvert\tilde H_0\rvert^2/(2\sigma\delta)$: the $\tfrac12RI^2$ of a sheet current. Why the current hugs the surface is [[2-magnetostatics/14-faradays-law-and-induced-emf|Lecture 14]] at work: the changing $\mathbf{B}$ induces eddy currents whose own field opposes the change (Lenz), so each layer shields the layers below it.

> [!example] Example 2 (notes) — the same wave at 1 kHz in seawater
> Repeat Example 1 with $\omega = 2\pi\times10^{3}$ rad/s and $\sigma = 4$ S/m. The notes keep $\epsilon = 4\epsilon_0$ from Example 1 and get $\sigma/\omega\epsilon = 1.8\times10^{7}$. With seawater's own $\epsilon = 81\epsilon_0$ the ratio is $8.9\times10^{5}$. Either way it is a **good conductor**, and $\epsilon$ drops out of every result:
> $$
> \begin{gathered}
> \tau = \frac\pi4,\\[4pt]
> \lvert\eta\rvert = \sqrt{\frac{\omega\mu_0}{\sigma}} = \sqrt{\frac{2\pi\times10^{3}\cdot4\pi\times10^{-7}}{4}}\\[4pt]
> = \frac{\pi\sqrt2}{100} = 0.0444\ \Omega,\\[4pt]
> \alpha = \sqrt{\pi f\mu_0\sigma} = \frac{\pi}{25} = 0.126\ \text{Np/m}.
> \end{gathered}
> $$
> $\tilde{\mathbf{H}} = -\hat x\,(2/\lvert\eta\rvert)\,e^{-j\pi/4}e^{-\alpha z}e^{-j\beta z}$ has an amplitude of 45 A/m: the same 2 V/m now comes with a magnetic field 4,240 times stronger than in Example 1. Hence
> $$
> \begin{gathered}
> \langle\mathbf{S}\rangle = \hat z\,\frac{2}{\lvert\eta\rvert}\cos\frac\pi4\,e^{-2\alpha z}\\[4pt]
> = \hat z\,\frac{100}{\pi}\,e^{-2\alpha z}\ \text{W/m}^2,
> \end{gathered}
> $$
> which is 31.8 W/m² at $z = 0$ (3,000 times Example 1's 10.6 mW/m², for the same $\lvert\mathbf{E}\rvert$) and $31.8\times e^{-0.8\pi} = 31.8\times0.081 = 2.6$ W/m² at $z = 10$ m. The power is down to 8 % after 10 m: 1.09 dB per metre.

> [!example] Example 3 (notes) — the penetration depth of seawater at 1 kHz
> $\sigma = 4$ S/m, $\epsilon = 81\epsilon_0$, $\mu = \mu_0$, $f = 1$ kHz. First check the regime: $\sigma/\omega\epsilon = 72\times10^{9}/(81\times10^{3}) = 8.9\times10^{5}\gg1$. Then
> $$
> \begin{gathered}
> \delta = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi\cdot10^{3}\cdot4\pi\times10^{-7}\cdot4}}\\[4pt]
> = \frac{1}{\sqrt{16\pi^2\times10^{-4}}} = \frac{100}{4\pi} = \frac{25}{\pi} = 7.96\ \text{m}.
> \end{gathered}
> $$
> The wavelength in the water is $2\pi\delta = 50$ m, and $v_p = \lambda f = 5\times10^{4}$ m/s.

## 5. Why submarines are called at very low frequencies

The notes use Examples 1 and 2 to argue that underwater communication is better done at low frequencies. As printed, though, the two examples change the medium as well as the frequency, so they do not isolate the effect. In fact the 1 kHz seawater wave loses more over 10 m (92 %) than the 1 GHz Example 1 wave (85 %). The fair comparison keeps the medium fixed. At 1 GHz seawater is in between regimes ($\sigma/\omega\epsilon = 0.89$), and the exact formula of Lecture 22 gives $\alpha = 77$ Np/m, so $\delta = 1.3$ cm and the loss is 673 dB per metre. Ten metres of seawater cost 6,700 dB at 1 GHz, against 11 dB at 1 kHz. Since $\alpha = \sqrt{\pi f\mu\sigma}$ in a good conductor, every factor of 100 lower in frequency cuts the attenuation in dB per metre by a factor of 10.

Here is seawater ($\sigma = 4$ S/m, $\epsilon_r = 81$) at several frequencies, with the exact formulas. As an illustration, the last column but one assumes the receiver can tolerate a 100 dB loss in the water; the depth is then $100/(8.686\,\alpha) = 11.5\,\delta$.

| $f$ | $\sigma/\omega\epsilon$ | $\delta$ | loss [dB/m] | depth for 100 dB | free-space $\lambda_0 = c/f$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 76 Hz (ELF) | $1.2\times10^{7}$ | 28.9 m | 0.30 | 332 m | 3,900 km |
| 1 kHz (notes) | $8.9\times10^{5}$ | 7.96 m | 1.09 | 92 m | 300 km |
| 24 kHz (VLF) | $3.7\times10^{4}$ | 1.62 m | 5.3 | 19 m | 12.5 km |
| 1 MHz | 888 | 25 cm | 34.5 | 2.9 m | 300 m |
| 100 MHz | 8.9 | 2.7 cm | 326 | 31 cm | 3 m |
| 1 GHz | 0.89 | 1.3 cm | 673 | 15 cm | 30 cm |

At 100 MHz the good-conductor formula would give $\delta = 2.5$ cm, about 5 % short of the exact 2.7 cm, because $\sigma/\omega\epsilon$ is below 10 there. At 1 GHz it would be badly wrong.

The notes name the price of going low: **bandwidth**. A carrier at frequency $f$ can only carry a signal whose bandwidth is a small fraction of $f$, so a link to a deep submarine is necessarily slow. The real systems show the trade-off. Very-low-frequency (VLF, 3–30 kHz) stations reach a submarine a few tens of metres down at roughly 300 bit/s. The extremely-low-frequency (ELF) systems, the US Navy's at 76 Hz (shut down in 2004) and the Russian one at 82 Hz, reach hundreds of metres but send only a few characters per minute. (These figures are from the Wikipedia article "Communication with submarines". They are consistent with the table: about 19 m at 24 kHz and 330 m at 76 Hz for the assumed 100 dB budget.)

A second price is the **antenna**. In air the wavelength at 76 Hz is 3,900 km and at 24 kHz it is 12.5 km. Any antenna that can be built is a tiny fraction of a wavelength, so it radiates poorly. According to the same Wikipedia article, the transmitters use tens of kilometres of wire and very large power, and the link is one-way: a submarine cannot carry such an antenna to reply. (Rao makes a related point: inside the water the wavelength is only $2\pi\delta$, 50 m at 1 kHz instead of 300 km, so antennas immersed in seawater can be much shorter.)

## 6. Skin depth in metals

For metals the good-conductor formulas hold at every frequency the course uses (§1). With $\mu = \mu_0$, $\delta = 1/\sqrt{\pi f\mu_0\sigma}$, which is $0.0661/\sqrt f$ m for copper, the figure Rao quotes, and $0.0851/\sqrt f$ m for aluminium. Panel 1 of the figure in §5 plots both.

| $f$ | copper, $\sigma = 5.8\times10^{7}$ S/m | aluminium, $\sigma = 3.5\times10^{7}$ S/m | copper $\lvert\eta\rvert$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| 60 Hz | 8.53 mm | 11.0 mm | 2.86 µΩ |
| 1 kHz | 2.09 mm | 2.69 mm | 11.7 µΩ |
| 1 MHz | 66.1 µm | 85.1 µm | 0.369 mΩ |
| 100 MHz | 6.61 µm | 8.51 µm | 3.69 mΩ |
| 1 GHz | 2.09 µm | 2.69 µm | 11.7 mΩ |
| 10 GHz | 0.661 µm | 0.851 µm | 36.9 mΩ |

The conductivities are Rao Table 5.1 values; 5.8×10⁷ S/m is the international annealed-copper standard. The impedance follows $\lvert\eta\rvert = \sqrt{\omega\mu_0/\sigma} = 3.69\times10^{-7}\sqrt f\ \Omega$, Rao's number for copper. Three consequences:

- **Skin effect in wires.** An AC current crowds into a layer about $\delta$ thick under the surface of a conductor. A round wire of radius $a\gg\delta$ then behaves like a tube of wall $\delta$, and its resistance rises by about $a/2\delta$: a factor of 7.6 for a 1 mm radius copper wire at 1 MHz. At 60 Hz the house-wiring skin depth, 8.5 mm, is larger than the wire, so the whole cross-section carries current.
- **Shielding.** A metal sheet several skin depths thick stops a wave. Absorption alone reduces the field by $e^{-d/\delta}$, which is $8.686\,d/\delta$ dB, and reflection at the surface adds more (Lecture 25). A 1 mm aluminium sheet is $0.09\,\delta$ thick at 60 Hz, nearly transparent to the absorption mechanism. At 1 MHz it is $11.8\,\delta$ thick, worth 102 dB. That is why shielding power-frequency magnetic fields is hard, and why a thin metal wall suffices at radio frequencies. At the 2.45 GHz of a microwave oven, $\delta$ is only 1.7 µm even in aluminium, so a metal wall a fraction of a millimetre thick is hundreds of skin depths.
- **Tiny $\lvert\eta\rvert$.** Copper's 11.7 mΩ at 1 GHz is $3\times10^{-5}$ of $\eta_0$. A wave arriving from air therefore sees an almost perfect short circuit and is almost entirely reflected (Lecture 25).

> [!trap] "Copper is a good conductor at all frequencies"
> With a constant $\sigma$, copper's $\sigma/\omega\epsilon_0$ falls to 1 only at $f_c = 1.0\times10^{18}$ Hz, in the X-ray range. But no conductor is a good conductor at *all* frequencies: since $\sigma/\omega\epsilon\to0$ as $\omega\to\infty$, every medium eventually behaves as a dielectric. For metals the constant-$\sigma$ model fails long before $10^{18}$ Hz. The Drude conductivity of [[1-electrostatics/11-lorentz-drude-models-for-conductivity-and-susceptibility|Lecture 11]], $\sigma_{\text{DC}}/(1 + j\omega/\nu)$ with $\nu\approx4\times10^{13}$ s⁻¹ for copper, starts to change in the infrared. And X-rays do pass through thin metal foil. The safe statement is that copper is a good conductor at every radio and microwave frequency.

## 7. Reading a lossy-medium problem

> [!recipe] Damped plane waves, start to finish
> 1. **Classify** with the medium's own $\epsilon$ at the stated $\omega$: $p = \sigma/\omega\epsilon$. Below 0.1 use the imperfect-dielectric row; above 10 the good-conductor row; in between the exact Lecture 22 formulas, or the polar forms of §2.
> 2. **Constants**: $\alpha$, $\beta$, $\lvert\eta\rvert$, $\tau$, then $\delta = 1/\alpha$, $\lambda = 2\pi/\beta$, $v_p = \omega/\beta$. In a good conductor everything follows from $\delta$: $\alpha = \beta = 1/\delta$, $\lambda = 2\pi\delta$, $v_p = \omega\delta$, $\eta = (1+j)/(\sigma\delta)$.
> 3. **Fields**: $\tilde{\mathbf{H}} = \hat u\times\tilde{\mathbf{E}}/\eta$ for travel along $\hat u$, so $\mathbf{H}$ picks up the phase $-\tau$. Check that $\mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{H}$ points along $\hat u$ with an explicit cross product. Write the time domain with real cosines: $\lvert E_0\rvert e^{-\alpha d}\cos(\omega t - \beta d + \angle E_0)$ for $\mathbf{E}$, with the extra $-\tau$ inside the cosine for $\mathbf{H}$, where $d$ is the distance travelled.
> 4. **Power**: $\langle\mathbf{S}\rangle = \hat u\,\lvert E_0\rvert^2\cos\tau\,e^{-2\alpha d}/(2\lvert\eta\rvert)$. Ratios between two depths need only $e^{-2\alpha\,\Delta d}$, and the loss in dB is $8.686\,\alpha\,\Delta d$.
> 5. **Inverse problems** (given $\lambda$ and $\delta$, or a field expression): $\beta = 2\pi/\lambda$, $\alpha = 1/\delta$, then $\alpha/\beta = \tan\tau$ tells you the regime, $\tan2\tau = \sigma/\omega\epsilon$ gives the loss tangent, and $\gamma\eta = j\omega\mu$, $\gamma/\eta = \sigma + j\omega\epsilon$ (Lecture 22) give the material.

> [!trap] Where damped-wave problems lose points
> - Power decays as $e^{-2\alpha d}$, not $e^{-\alpha d}$. The field is at 37 % after one $\delta$; the power is at 14 %.
> - Distance travelled, not coordinate. A wave going toward $-z$ that is evaluated at $z = -10$ m has gone 10 m into the medium, so its factor is $e^{-2\alpha\cdot10}$.
> - The $\cos\tau$ in $\langle\mathbf{S}\rangle$ matters in a good conductor, where it is $1/\sqrt2$. It is not 1.
> - $\lvert\eta\rvert = \sqrt{\omega\mu/\sigma}$ in a good conductor, not $\sqrt{\mu/\epsilon}$; for metals it is milliohms or less.
> - Nepers and decibels: $\alpha$ in Np/m times 8.686 is dB/m. $\beta$ is in rad/m.
> - $\delta = 1/\sqrt{\pi f\mu\sigma}$ uses $f$; $\sqrt{2/\omega\mu\sigma}$ uses $\omega$. Mixing them gives an error of $\sqrt{2\pi}$.
> - The penetration depth of an imperfect dielectric, $(2/\sigma)\sqrt{\epsilon/\mu}$, does not depend on frequency. Do not use the skin-depth formula there.

## 8. Connections

- **Lecture 8 → here.** The relaxation time $\epsilon/\sigma$ that empties a conductor of interior charge is the time scale of the loss tangent: $\sigma/\omega\epsilon = 1/(\omega\tau_r)$. A medium is a conductor for a wave whose period is long compared with $\tau_r$.
- **Lecture 10.** The leaky capacitor ($G = (\sigma/\epsilon)C$) has the current ratio $G/\omega C = \sigma/\omega\epsilon$. The $\mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{E}$ heating of a resistor is, averaged over a cycle, exactly the power the wave loses: $\alpha = \sigma\lvert\eta\rvert/(2\cos\tau)$.
- **Lecture 11.** The Drude $\sigma(\omega)$ and the Lorentz $\epsilon(\omega)$ are why real media have frequency-dependent loss tangents. The constant values of this lecture are their low-frequency limits.
- **Lecture 14.** Skin depth is induction: eddy currents oppose the changing flux (Lenz), so the field dies within a few $\delta$.
- **Lecture 16.** The perfect conductor ($\delta = 0$, $\eta = 0$) is the limit of the good conductor. Its surface current is what remains of a layer of current $\delta$ thick.
- **Lectures 20–22.** Poynting's theorem accounts for the lost power; phasors give $\langle\mathbf{S}\rangle = \tfrac12\text{Re}\{\tilde{\mathbf{E}}\times\tilde{\mathbf{H}}^*\}$; Lecture 22 supplies $\gamma$ and $\eta$, which this lecture specializes.
- **Lecture 25 (ahead).** The tiny $\eta$ of a good conductor makes it an almost perfect reflector. SP17 Exam 3 #2(iv) asks for the reflection coefficient from a medium with $\sigma = 400\,\omega\epsilon_0$ and $\epsilon = 4\epsilon_0$; first decide its regime with this page.

## 9. Summary

- The loss tangent $\sigma/\omega\epsilon$ is the ratio of conduction to displacement current, with the medium's own $\epsilon$. Below 0.1: imperfect dielectric. Above 10: good conductor. In between: exact formulas. The crossover frequency is $f_c = \sigma/2\pi\epsilon = 1/(2\pi\tau_r)$. Seawater: 0.89 GHz; moist soil: 18 MHz; fresh water: 225 kHz; dry earth: 36 kHz; copper: $10^{18}$ Hz, a formal figure only.
- With $p = \sigma/\omega\epsilon$ and $\theta = \tan^{-1}p$: $\tau = \theta/2$ exactly, $\alpha/\beta = \tan\tau\le1$, $\lvert\eta\rvert = \sqrt{\mu/\epsilon}(1+p^2)^{-1/4}$. The notes' table is the two limits.
- Imperfect dielectric: $\alpha\approx(\sigma/2)\sqrt{\mu/\epsilon}$, independent of $f$; $\beta$, $\lvert\eta\rvert$ as if lossless; $\tau\approx\sigma/2\omega\epsilon$. The lost power is the Joule heat: $\alpha = \sigma\lvert\eta\rvert/(2\cos\tau)$ exactly.
- Good conductor: $\delta = 1/\sqrt{\pi f\mu\sigma}$, $\alpha = \beta = 1/\delta$, $\lambda = 2\pi\delta$, $v_p = \omega\delta$, $\eta = (1+j)/(\sigma\delta)$, so $\mathbf{H}$ lags $\mathbf{E}$ by 45°. The field falls by 54.6 dB per wavelength.
- $\langle\mathbf{S}\rangle = \hat u\,\lvert E_0\rvert^2\cos\tau\,e^{-2\alpha d}/(2\lvert\eta\rvert)$: power falls as $e^{-2\alpha d}$, and the loss is $8.686\,\alpha$ dB/m.
- Notes' examples: Example 1, 1 GHz with $\sigma = 10^{-3}$ S/m and $\epsilon = 4\epsilon_0$, keeps 15 % of its power after 10 m. Example 2, seawater at 1 kHz, keeps 8 %, with $\lvert\eta\rvert = 0.0444\ \Omega$. Example 3, seawater at 1 kHz, has $\delta = 25/\pi = 7.96$ m.
- Seawater $\delta$: 29 m at 76 Hz, 7.96 m at 1 kHz, 1.6 m at 24 kHz, 25 cm at 1 MHz, 1.3 cm at 1 GHz. Hence ELF/VLF for submarines, paid for in bandwidth (a few characters per minute to about 300 bit/s) and in antenna size.
- Copper $\delta$: 8.5 mm at 60 Hz, 66 µm at 1 MHz, 2.1 µm at 1 GHz. Aluminium is $\sqrt{5.8/3.5} = 1.29$ times larger.

Use $\alpha/\beta = \tan\tau\le1$ (§2), and check the regime with this medium's own $f_c$ (§1). Its "dry earth" is not the dry earth of the §1 table.
> 4. **Concept multiple choice and true/false on good conductors** (SP17 #1a and #2(i), SP18 #1(i)): $\alpha$ against $\beta$, the 45° phase of $\eta$, decay within one wavelength, frequency-dependent $v_p$, constant or decaying power flow, "good conductor at all frequencies". Sections 4 and 6 cover each statement. Related: the meaning of the loss tangent (FA23 #1(b), [[concepts/loss-tangent]]) and where a medium sits on a five-way scale (SP19 #1e).
> 5. **Power against depth in water, in dB/m** (FA23 #6): a given damped phasor such as $e^{-0.2z}e^{-j0.2z}$ (which has $\alpha = \beta$: a good conductor); $\langle S\rangle$ as a function of depth, and the attenuation of the power per metre and in dB per metre ($2\alpha$ per metre; $8.686\,\alpha$ dB/m).
>
> Typical numbers: $\epsilon_0\approx10^{-9}/36\pi$ F/m and $\mu_0 = 4\pi\times10^{-7}$ H/m make $\sigma/\omega\epsilon = 18\times10^{9}\,\sigma/(f\epsilon_r)$ and $\pi f\mu_0\sigma = 4\pi^2\times10^{-7}f\sigma$. So a calculator-free $\delta$ needs $f\sigma\times10^{-7}$ to be a perfect square, as with $f\sigma = 4\times10^{3}$ in the seawater questions.
