*Lecture 19 · course notes §19 · slides "Uniform Plane Waves in Free Space" (Lecture 19) · prev: [[3-maxwell-and-waves/18-the-wave-equation-and-plane-tem-waves|Lecture 18]] · next: Lecture 20, Poynting theorem and monochromatic waves (coming) · worked problem: [[problems/a-current-sheet-launches-two-waves|A current sheet launches two waves]] · practice: [[practice/19-radiation-from-current-sheets|12 problems]]*

> [!abstract] In one breath
> [[3-maxwell-and-waves/18-the-wave-equation-and-plane-tem-waves|Lecture 18]] found waves $f(t\mp z/v)$ in a region with no sources. This lecture names them and then asks where they come from. They are **uniform plane TEM waves**: $\mathbf{E}$ and $\mathbf{H}$ are transverse to the direction of travel, which is the direction of the **Poynting vector** $\mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{H}$, and the field is the same everywhere on each plane of constant phase. The simplest source is an infinite sheet of current $\mathbf{J}_s(t)$. Its static field is the $\tfrac12\mathbf{J}_s\times\hat n$ of [[2-magnetostatics/13-current-sheets-solenoids-and-the-vector-potential|Lecture 13]]. When the current varies, that field is delayed by the travel time $\lvert z\rvert/v$ and gains an electric partner: $\mathbf{E} = -\tfrac{\eta}{2}\mathbf{J}_s(t - \lvert z\rvert/v)$ on both sides. So $\mathbf{E}$ opposes the current, has the same value on both faces, and is continuous; $\mathbf{H}$ reverses across the sheet and jumps by $J_s$, exactly the boundary conditions of [[3-maxwell-and-waves/16-charge-conservation-displacement-current-and-maxwells-equations|Lecture 16]]. The notes reach this by delaying the static solution and invoking causality and uniqueness; the slides solve for it with boundary conditions. A cosine current gives sinusoidal waves with phase constant $\beta = \omega/v$ and wavelength $\lambda = 2\pi/\beta$, and $\mathbf{S} = \mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{H}$, in W/m², points away from the sheet on both sides.

## 1. What kind of wave is $f(t\mp z/v)$?

Lecture 18 ended with the d'Alembert solutions for a homogeneous, lossless, source-free region,

$$
\mathbf{E} = \hat x\,f\Big(t\mp\frac zv\Big),\qquad \mathbf{H} = \pm\hat y\,\frac{f(t\mp z/v)}{\eta},\qquad v = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu\epsilon}},\quad \eta = \sqrt{\frac\mu\epsilon}.
$$

The notes open Lecture 19 by giving this family its name. Each word in **uniform plane TEM wave** describes one property.

- **TEM** (transverse electromagnetic): $\mathbf{E}$ and $\mathbf{H}$ are both perpendicular to the direction of travel. That is forced by $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{E} = \nabla\cdot\mathbf{H} = 0$ for a field that depends on $z$ alone. A component along $z$ would have $\partial E_z/\partial z\neq0$ (the impossible $z$-polarized wave of [[3-maxwell-and-waves/18-the-wave-equation-and-plane-tem-waves#3-the-planar-problem-only-exzt-and-hyzt|Lecture 18 §3]]).
- **Direction of travel = direction of $\mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{H}$.** The notes now make this the definition and name the vector the **Poynting vector**, $\mathbf{S}\equiv\mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{H}$. For the upper signs, $\hat x\times\hat y = +\hat z$; for the lower signs, $\hat x\times(-\hat y) = -\hat z$.
- **Uniform plane:** the field vector is the same at every point of a **plane of constant phase**, a plane on which $t - z/v$ is constant. These planes are perpendicular to $\mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{H}$.

A plane of constant phase is a set of points that all carry the same value of the waveform. For $f(t - z/v)$, the plane where $t - z/v = t_0$ sits at $z = v(t - t_0)$, so it moves toward $+z$ at speed $v$. The slides call this speed the **phase velocity** $v_p$ (§7). In free space it equals $c$.

Not every TEM wave is a plane wave. A compact antenna radiates waves whose surfaces of constant phase are spheres; they are TEM but not uniform, and they are ECE 450 material. To radiate a *plane* wave you need an infinite plane of current, which is §3.

> [!key] Uniform plane TEM wave, and the rule that pairs E with H
> A field that depends on position only through $t - \hat u\cdot\mathbf{r}/v$, with $\mathbf{E}\perp\hat u$ and
> $$
> \mathbf{H} = \frac1\eta\,\hat u\times\mathbf{E},\qquad \mathbf{E} = \eta\,\mathbf{H}\times\hat u,\qquad \mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{H}\parallel\hat u .
> $$
> In the notes' words: divide $\mathbf{E}$ by $\eta$ and rotate it by 90° so that $\mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{H}$ points along the travel. This is the rule of [[3-maxwell-and-waves/18-the-wave-equation-and-plane-tem-waves#5-h-from-e-the-intrinsic-impedance|Lecture 18 §5]]; Lecture 19 uses it on every example.

## 2. Three warm-up examples (notes)

> [!example] Example 1 — a triangular pulse travelling along $y$, and its B two ways
> In free space $\mathbf{E} = \hat x\,\triangle\big((t - y/c)/\tau\big)$, where $\triangle(u)$ is the unit triangle of ECE 210 (peak 1 at $u = 0$, zero for $\lvert u\rvert\ge\tfrac12$). Find $\mathbf{B}$.
>
> **Route 1, the rotation rule.** The argument $t - y/c$ says the wave travels toward $+y$, so $\hat u = \hat y$. Then $\mathbf{H} = \hat y\times\mathbf{E}/\eta_0 = (\hat y\times\hat x)\,\triangle/\eta_0 = -\hat z\,\triangle/\eta_0$. Check: $\mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{H}\parallel\hat x\times(-\hat z) = +\hat y$ ✓. With $\mu_0/\eta_0 = \sqrt{\mu_0\epsilon_0} = 1/c$,
> $$
> \mathbf{B} = \mu_0\mathbf{H} = -\hat z\,\frac1c\,\triangle\Big(\frac{t - y/c}{\tau}\Big).
> $$
> **Route 2, Faraday's law.** $\nabla\times(\hat xE_x(y,t)) = -\hat z\,\partial E_x/\partial y$, so $\partial\mathbf{B}/\partial t = -\nabla\times\mathbf{E} = +\hat z\,\partial E_x/\partial y$. The chain rule gives $\partial E_x/\partial y = \triangle'\cdot\partial_y\big((t - y/c)/\tau\big) = -\triangle'/(c\tau)$, where $\triangle'$ is the derivative with respect to the argument. Integrating in time, and dropping the static constant as in Lecture 18, returns the same $\mathbf{B}$. (Numerically, at $y = 40$ m, $t = 0.05$ µs, $\tau = 1$ µs, both sides of Faraday's law equal $-6.67\times10^{-3}$ T/s.)

> [!example] Example 2 — is the magnetic force of a wave negligible?
> A charge $q$ moving with velocity $\mathbf{v}$ through the Example 1 wave feels $\mathbf{F} = q(\mathbf{E} + \mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B})$ ([[concepts/lorentz-force]]). With $\mathbf{B} = -\hat z\,\triangle/c$,
> $$
> \mathbf{F} = q\,\triangle\Big(\frac{t - y/c}{\tau}\Big)\Big(\hat x - \frac{\mathbf{v}\times\hat z}{c}\Big).
> $$
> The magnetic term is smaller than the electric one by $\lvert\mathbf{v}\times\hat z\rvert/c = v_\perp/c$, where $v_\perp$ is the part of the velocity perpendicular to $\mathbf{B}$. At $10^6$ m/s, a fast electron in a vacuum tube, the ratio is $3.3\times10^{-3}$. It becomes comparable to 1 only for relativistic charges ($v = 2\times10^8$ m/s gives 0.67). The notes state the conclusion; the ratio $v_\perp/c$ is this site's way of putting it. This is why the electric field does the work in antennas and in light absorbed by matter, even though $E$ and $H$ carry equal shares of the wave (Lecture 20).

> [!example] Example 3 — one pulse, plotted against t and against z
> In free space $\mathbf{E} = \hat x\,f(t - z/c)$ with $f(t) = At\,\mathrm{rect}(t/\tau)$, $A = 2$ V/m per µs and $\tau = 1$ µs: a ramp from $-1$ to $+1$ V/m over $\lvert t\rvert<0.5$ µs. Use $c = 300$ m/µs.
> - **Against t.** At $z = 0$ the probe records the ramp itself. At $z = 600$ m the delay is $z/c = 2$ µs, so $E_x(600\ \text{m}, t) = 2(t-2)\,\mathrm{rect}(t-2)$ V/m ($t$ in µs): the same rising ramp, from $-1$ at 1.5 µs to $+1$ at 2.5 µs.
> - **Against z.** $E_x(z,0) = 2(-z/c)\,\mathrm{rect}(-z/c)$: the $z$ enters with a minus sign, so the snapshot is a *descending* ramp, from $+1$ at $z = -150$ m to $-1$ at $+150$ m. At $t = 2$ µs the same descending ramp sits between 450 m ($+1$) and 750 m ($-1$), crossing zero at 600 m.
> - **The magnetic field**, which the notes ask for but do not write out: $\mathbf{H} = \hat z\times\mathbf{E}/\eta_0 = \hat y\,f(t - z/c)/\eta_0$, peaking at $1/\eta_0 = 2.65$ mA/m.
>
> The snapshot of a $+z$ wave is the time record mirrored, as in [[3-maxwell-and-waves/18-the-wave-equation-and-plane-tem-waves#6-reading-a-wave|Lecture 18 §6]]. The slides train the same skill in §6 below.

## 3. What launches a plane wave? The notes' route

Lecture 18's waves live where $\rho = 0$ and $\mathbf{J} = 0$. So what produces them? The notes' answer: varying charges and currents somewhere else, linked by the [[concepts/continuity-equation|continuity equation]], which the source-free equations leave out. To produce a *plane* wave the source must look the same from every point of a plane, so take an infinite sheet of surface current $\mathbf{J}_s = \hat x\,J_x$ [A/m] on $z = 0$, in a homogeneous lossless medium ($\mu$, $\epsilon$) on both sides. The notes build the answer in three steps, from what we already know.

**Step 1: static current (Lecture 13).** A constant $J_x$ gives

$$
\mathbf{H} = \tfrac12\mathbf{J}_s\times\hat n = \mp\hat y\,\frac{J_x}{2}\quad(z\gtrless0),
$$

with $\hat n$ pointing from the sheet toward the field point: $\tfrac12J_x\,\hat x\times\hat z = -\tfrac12J_x\hat y$ above, $+\tfrac12J_x\hat y$ below. The field reverses across the sheet, so tangential $\mathbf{H}$ jumps by $J_x$. A static current makes no $\mathbf{E}$.

**Step 2: slowly varying current (quasi-static).** If $J_x = J_x(t)$, then close to the sheet $\mathbf{H}\approx\mp\hat y\,J_x(t)/2$. This cannot hold everywhere. If it did, a change of current would change $\mathbf{H}$ at the same instant a kilometre away, or a light-year away: an infinite propagation speed (the notes' footnote 1). It is a good approximation only where the delay $\lvert z\rvert/v$ is negligible.

**Step 3: delay it (exact).** Above and below the sheet the medium is source-free, so the fields there must be d'Alembert waves. The fix is to replace $J_x(t)$ by $J_x(t\mp z/v)$ and "≈" by "=":

$$
\mathbf{H}(z,t) = \mp\hat y\,\frac12J_x\Big(t\mp\frac zv\Big),\qquad \mathbf{E}(z,t) = -\hat x\,\frac\eta2\,J_x\Big(t\mp\frac zv\Big)\qquad(z\gtrless0).
$$

$\mathbf{E}$ comes from the pairing rule of §1: multiply $\mathbf{H}$ by $\eta$ and rotate so that $\mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{H}$ points away from the sheet. Above: $(-\hat x)\times(-\hat y) = +\hat z$ ✓. Below: $(-\hat x)\times(+\hat y) = -\hat z$ ✓.

Two principles turn this guess into the answer.

- **Causality picks the signs** (the notes' footnote 2). Above the sheet, both $J_x(t - z/v)$ and $J_x(t + z/v)$ solve the wave equation. But the sheet is the only source, so waves can only travel *away* from it, like ripples spreading from a pebble dropped in a pond. Above the sheet that means $t - z/v$; below it, $t + z/v$. In both regions the argument is $t - \lvert z\rvert/v$.
- **Uniqueness confirms the answer.** Maxwell's equations plus the boundary conditions at the sheet have only one solution. The fields above satisfy Maxwell's equations in each source-free region (they are d'Alembert waves) and, as §5 checks, the jump conditions at $z = 0$. So they are *the* solution.

> [!key] Fields radiated by a current sheet (notes' form)
> A surface current $\mathbf{J}_s = \hat x\,f(t)$ on the plane $z = 0$, in a homogeneous lossless medium, radiates
> $$
> \mathbf{E}^\pm = -\hat x\,\frac{\eta}{2}\,f\Big(t\mp\frac zv\Big),\qquad \mathbf{H}^\pm = \mp\hat y\,\frac12\,f\Big(t\mp\frac zv\Big)\qquad\text{in } z\gtrless0 .
> $$
> Read the upper signs together ($z>0$, a wave travelling toward $+z$) and the lower signs together ($z<0$, toward $-z$). The $\mp$ inside the argument and the $\mp$ in front of $\hat y$ are separate signs that happen to agree. There is **no** $\mp$ in front of $\mathbf{E}$.
> Amplitudes: $\lvert\mathbf{E}\rvert = \tfrac\eta2\lvert J_s\rvert$ and $\lvert\mathbf{H}\rvert = \tfrac12\lvert J_s\rvert$, so $\lvert\mathbf{E}\rvert/\lvert\mathbf{H}\rvert = \eta$, as for any single plane wave. In vacuum, 1 A/m of sheet current makes $188$ V/m ($60\pi$) and 0.5 A/m on each side.

**Where the displacement current enters.** Without $\partial\mathbf{D}/\partial t$ (in magnetostatics), the quasi-static field of Step 2 would be exact everywhere, with no $\mathbf{E}$ partner and infinite speed. The displacement current of [[3-maxwell-and-waves/16-charge-conservation-displacement-current-and-maxwells-equations|Lecture 16]] couples $\mathbf{H}$ to $\mathbf{E}$, which delays the response by $\lvert z\rvert/v$ and turns the field into two outgoing waves.

## 4. The same result from boundary conditions (slides)

The slides reach the same fields by a different route, which is the one to reproduce on an exam if asked to "derive". They orient the sheet the other way, $\mathbf{J}_s = -J_s(t)\,\hat x$ (so their scalar $J_s$ is the current along $-\hat x$), work in free space ($\eta_0$, and $v_p = c$ for this site's $v$), and write unit vectors as $\hat a_x$. Here is the argument in this site's notation.

1. **General solution** (Lecture 18): on each side, $E_x = A f(t - z/v) + B g(t + z/v)$ and $H_y = \frac{1}{\eta}\big[Af(t - z/v) - Bg(t + z/v)\big]$.
2. **Radiation condition:** waves only leave the sheet, so $B = 0$ for $z>0$ and $A = 0$ for $z<0$. That leaves $E_x = Af(t - z/v)$, $H_y = Af/\eta$ above and $E_x = Bg(t + z/v)$, $H_y = -Bg/\eta$ below.
3. **Tangential $\mathbf{E}$ is continuous** at $z = 0$: $Af(t) = Bg(t)$ for all $t$. So the two waves carry the same waveform with the same sign, and $g$ can be eliminated.
4. **Tangential $\mathbf{H}$ jumps by the surface current**, $\hat n\times(\mathbf{H}_1 - \mathbf{H}_2) = \mathbf{J}_s$, with region 1 = $z>0$, region 2 = $z<0$ and $\hat n = \hat z$ from 2 into 1. Since $\hat z\times\hat y = -\hat x$, this reads $-\hat x\,\big[H_y(0^+) - H_y(0^-)\big] = -J_s\hat x$:
$$
H_y(0^+) - H_y(0^-) = \frac{Af(t)}{\eta} - \Big(-\frac{Af(t)}{\eta}\Big) = \frac{2Af(t)}{\eta} = J_s(t)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad Af(t) = \frac\eta2\,J_s(t).
$$
5. **Delay it:** because this holds for every $t$, $Af(t - z/v) = \tfrac\eta2J_s(t - z/v)$, and similarly below.

The blue ink on slides 5 and 7 stresses two points: both expressions are evaluated at $z = 0$ (step 3), and the minus sign of $H_y$ below the sheet turns into a plus in the jump (step 4). These are the two places where sign slips happen.

> [!key] Fields radiated by a current sheet (slides' form, free space)
> For $\mathbf{J}_s = -J_s(t)\,\hat x$ on $z = 0$:
> $$
> \mathbf{E}(z,t) = \hat x\,\frac{\eta_0}{2}\,J_s\Big(t\mp\frac zc\Big),\qquad \mathbf{H}(z,t) = \pm\hat y\,\frac12\,J_s\Big(t\mp\frac zc\Big),\qquad z\gtrless0 .
> $$
> This is the notes' result with $f\to-J_s$. The signs look opposite because the sheets point in opposite directions; the physics is identical, and in both $\mathbf{E}$ points against the current.

Both forms are special cases of one rule that works for a sheet in any plane, with the current along any direction in that plane.

> [!recipe] Fields of a current sheet in any orientation
> Let $\hat n$ be the unit normal pointing from the sheet toward the field point (so it flips from one side to the other), and let $d$ be the distance from the sheet. Then
> $$
> \mathbf{E} = -\frac\eta2\,\mathbf{J}_s\Big(t - \frac dv\Big),\qquad \mathbf{H} = \frac12\,\mathbf{J}_s\Big(t - \frac dv\Big)\times\hat n,\qquad \mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{H}\parallel+\hat n .
> $$
> 1. Write $\mathbf{J}_s(t)$ as a vector, sign included.
> 2. $\mathbf{H}$: the Lecture 13 rule $\tfrac12\mathbf{J}_s\times\hat n$, evaluated separately on each side.
> 3. $\mathbf{E}$: $-\tfrac\eta2\mathbf{J}_s$, the same on both sides.
> 4. Replace $t$ by $t - d/v$: $t - y/v$ for $y>0$ and $t + y/v$ for $y<0$ if the sheet is on $y = 0$, and so on.
> 5. Check that $\mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{H}$ points away from the sheet on both sides.

## 5. Reading the result

**E is even, H is odd.** Across the sheet $\mathbf{E}$ keeps its sign and $\mathbf{H}$ reverses (the slides circle and highlight exactly this: the coefficient $\eta_0/2$ is the same on both sides; $\pm\tfrac12$ flips). Both facts are the boundary conditions of [[3-maxwell-and-waves/16-charge-conservation-displacement-current-and-maxwells-equations|Lecture 16]] ([[concepts/boundary-conditions]]):

- tangential $\mathbf{E}$ is continuous: $E_x(0^+,t) = E_x(0^-,t)$;
- tangential $\mathbf{H}$ jumps by the surface current: $\hat z\times(\mathbf{H}^+ - \mathbf{H}^-) = \hat z\times(-\hat y\,f) = +\hat x\,f = \mathbf{J}_s$ in the notes' orientation.

In the notes these are checks done after the fact. In the slides they are the equations that produce the amplitude. Either way, the $\tfrac12$ is the static sheet's $\tfrac12$ from [[2-magnetostatics/13-current-sheets-solenoids-and-the-vector-potential|Lecture 13]]: half of the jump on each side. The new feature is the $\mathbf{E}$, which the static sheet does not have.

**E opposes the current.** At the sheet $\mathbf{J}_s\cdot\mathbf{E} = -\tfrac\eta2\lvert\mathbf{J}_s\rvert^2<0$. In a resistor $\mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{E}>0$, which means the field does work on the charges. Here the sign is reversed, so the charges do work on the field. The notes read it exactly that way: the sheet is a *source* of the energy carried off by the two waves. Lecture 20 makes this quantitative (the $\mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{E}$ term of Poynting's theorem). The in-class ink of Lecture 18, "$\mathbf{E}$ always opposite of $\mathbf{J}_s$", is this statement.

> [!trap] Sign traps at a sheet
> - Putting a $\mp$ in front of $\mathbf{E}$ by analogy with $\mathbf{H}$. $\mathbf{E}$ has the same sign on both sides; otherwise it would jump, and tangential $\mathbf{E}$ cannot jump.
> - Using $t + z/v$ above the sheet. The argument is always $t - (\text{distance})/v$: outgoing.
> - Using one $\hat n$ for both sides. In $\tfrac12\mathbf{J}_s\times\hat n$, the normal points toward the field point, so it is $+\hat z$ above and $-\hat z$ below.
> - Dropping the $\tfrac12$, or giving $\mathbf{E}$ the amplitude $\eta J_s$. Each side gets half: $\eta J_s/2$ and $J_s/2$.
> - Comparing the slides' and the notes' formulas term by term. Their sheets point in opposite directions; compare $\mathbf{E}$ with $\mathbf{J}_s$ instead.

> [!example] Example 4 (notes) — a ramp of sheet current, seen at $t = 2$ µs
> In free space $\mathbf{J}_s = \hat x\,f(t)$ on $z = 0$, with $f(t) = At\,\mathrm{rect}(t/\tau)$, $A = 2$ A/m per µs and $\tau = 1$ µs: a ramp from $-1$ to $+1$ A/m over $\lvert t\rvert<0.5$ µs. Plot $H_y$ and $E_x$ against $z$ at $t = 2$ µs ($c = 300$ m/µs, $\eta_0\approx120\pi\ \Omega$).
>
> In 2 µs the waves have gone 600 m, so the fields occupy $450<\lvert z\rvert<750$ m. Halve the current for $\mathbf{H}$:
> $$
> H_y(z, 2\ \mu\text{s}) = \mp\frac12\,f\Big(2\mp\frac{z}{300}\Big),\qquad E_x(z, 2\ \mu\text{s}) = -60\pi\,f\Big(2\mp\frac z{300}\Big)\quad(z\gtrless0;\ z\ \text{in m},\ f\ \text{in A/m}).

> - $z<0$: $H_y$ rises from $-0.5$ at $-750$ m to $+0.5$ at $-450$ m; $E_x$ falls from $+60\pi$ to $-60\pi$.

> [!example] Example (slide 17) — given $\mathbf{J}_s$ and $\mathbf{H}$ of a sheet on $y = 0$, find $\mathbf{E}$
> A sheet in the $xz$-plane carries current along $-\hat z$: $\mathbf{J}_s = -J_s(t)\,\hat z$. Its magnetic field is sinusoidal with amplitude $H_0$. Find the direction and magnitude of $\mathbf{E}$. (The slide's hint: $\mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{H}$ points along the propagation direction, and $\eta_0 = \lvert\mathbf{E}\rvert/\lvert\mathbf{H}\rvert$. The deck leaves it unsolved; the solution is this site's.)
> - Waves travel toward $\pm\hat y$ for $y\gtrless0$, so $\hat n = \pm\hat y$.
> - $\mathbf{H} = \tfrac12(-J_s\hat z)\times(\pm\hat y) = \pm\tfrac12J_s\,\hat x$, so $H_0 = J_{s0}/2$.
> - $\mathbf{E} = -\tfrac{\eta_0}{2}\mathbf{J}_s = +\hat z\,\tfrac{\eta_0}{2}J_s$ on both sides: along $+\hat z$, opposite to the current, with amplitude $\eta_0H_0\approx377H_0$ (or $120\pi H_0$).
> - Check with the hint: $\hat z\times\hat x = +\hat y$ for $y>0$ and $\hat z\times(-\hat x) = -\hat y$ for $y<0$ ✓.
>
> With a cosine current, $\mathbf{E} = \hat z\,\eta_0H_0\cos(\omega t\mp\beta y)$ and $\mathbf{H} = \pm\hat x\,H_0\cos(\omega t\mp\beta y)$ for $y\gtrless0$ (§7 explains $\beta$).

## 6. Transforming time and space (slide 11)

Sheet problems keep asking you to switch between a snapshot (field against position at one instant) and a record (field against time at one place). Slide 11 drills this, and was annotated twice in class.

> [!example] Example (slide 11) — a wave given at $t = 0$
> A wave travels toward $+z$ at 100 m/s. At $t = 0$ its profile is piecewise linear through the corners A $(-100\ \text{m}, 0)$, B $(0, 1)$, C $(100\ \text{m}, -1)$, D $(300\ \text{m}, 0)$, E $(400\ \text{m}, 0)$, and zero outside. >
> Write $E(z,t) = F(z - 100t)$, where $F$ is the given profile. In the $z$–$t$ plane each corner keeps its value along a line $z = z_0 + 100t$. These **world lines** have positive slope for a $+z$ wave (the ink: "+ slope"). Follow them from the given line $t = 0$ to the target.

## 7. A sinusoidal sheet current: β, λ and the Poynting vector (slides)

The notes stop at arbitrary waveforms; the slides now specialise to the most important one. Let $\mathbf{J}_s = -J_{s0}\cos(\omega t)\,\hat x$ on $z = 0$ in free space. Substituting $J_s(t\mp z/c) = J_{s0}\cos(\omega t\mp\beta z)$ into the result of §4:

> [!key] The sheet's sinusoidal waves (free space)
> $$
> \mathbf{E}(z,t) = \hat x\,\frac{\eta_0J_{s0}}{2}\cos(\omega t\mp\beta z),\qquad \mathbf{H}(z,t) = \pm\hat y\,\frac{J_{s0}}{2}\cos(\omega t\mp\beta z),\qquad z\gtrless0,\qquad \beta = \frac{\omega}{v_p}.
> $$
> These are real cosines of time; no phasors are involved (phasors arrive in Lecture 21).

The slides' table of wave parameters reads every quantity off the **phase** $\phi = \omega t\mp\beta z$:

| quantity | definition | unit |
|---|---|---|
| phase | $\phi = \omega t\mp\beta z$ | rad |
| angular frequency | $\omega = \partial\phi/\partial t$ | rad/s |
| frequency | $f = \omega/2\pi$ | Hz = 1/s |
| phase constant | $\beta = \lvert\partial\phi/\partial z\rvert$ | rad/m |
| wavelength | $\lambda = 2\pi/\beta$ | m |
| phase velocity | $v_p = \omega/\beta = \lambda f$ ($= c$ in free space) | m/s |
| intrinsic impedance | $\eta_0 = \lvert\mathbf{E}\rvert/\lvert\mathbf{H}\rvert$ | Ω |

The slides use $f$ both for the waveform (§4) and for the frequency here; context tells them apart. $v_p$ is the speed of the planes of constant phase of §1: setting $\phi$ = const gives $z = (\omega t - \text{const})/\beta$, which moves at $\omega/\beta$. The wavelength is the distance over which the phase changes by $2\pi$ at a fixed time, so $\beta$ counts radians of phase per metre. Example: at $f = 100$ MHz in free space, $\beta = 2\pi f/c = 2.10$ rad/m and $\lambda = 3.00$ m.

**The Poynting vector.** The slides now give $\mathbf{S} = \mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{H}$ its physical meaning: it points in the direction of energy flow, and its size is the energy flux density, power per unit area, in W/m² (V/m × A/m = W/m²). For the sheet's waves,

$$
\mathbf{S} = \mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{H} = \pm\hat z\,\frac{\eta_0J_{s0}^2}{4}\cos^2(\omega t\mp\beta z)\ \ \text{W/m}^2,\qquad z\gtrless0 .
$$

Check the direction: $\hat x\times\hat y = +\hat z$ above, $\hat x\times(-\hat y) = -\hat z$ below; $\mathbf{S}$ points away from the sheet on both sides. $\mathbf{S}$ is never negative along the direction of travel ($\cos^2\ge0$): energy only flows outward, in pulses at twice the source frequency. Its size can be written $\lvert\mathbf{E}\rvert^2/\eta_0 = \eta_0\lvert\mathbf{H}\rvert^2$. For $J_{s0} = 1$ A/m the peak is $\eta_0/4 = 94.2$ W/m² on each side. The two sides together carry $\eta_0J_s^2/2$, which is exactly $-\mathbf{J}_s\cdot\mathbf{E}$ at the sheet, the power per unit area the current gives up (§5).

That is as far as Lecture 19 goes. *Why* $\mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{H}$ is a power density, how it balances the stored energy and $\mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{E}$, and its time average, are Poynting's theorem in Lecture 20. See [[concepts/poynting-vector]].

> [!example] Example — frequency and wavelength from a phase change (the skill tested by SP18 Exam 2 #1(vii))
> At a fixed point in free space the phase of a plane wave advances by $4\pi$ rad in 5 µs. Then $\omega = \partial\phi/\partial t = 4\pi/(5\times10^{-6}\ \text{s}) = 8\pi\times10^5\approx2.51\times10^6$ rad/s, $f = \omega/2\pi = 400$ kHz and $\lambda = c/f = (3\times10^8)/(4\times10^5) = 750$ m. The exam item asks the same question with its own numbers; work it the same way.

## 8. An inverse problem: slides 18–21

The last four slides share one stem and ask the questions backwards: given a radiated field, reconstruct the rest, including the current. The deck leaves all four parts unsolved; the solutions below are this site's.

**Stem.** A time-varying sheet current flows in the plane $x = 0$. For $x>0$ its wave has $\mathbf{H}(x, t = 1\ \mu\text{s}) = 2x\,[u(x) - u(x - 100)]\,\hat y$ A/m ($x$ in m): a ramp from 0 at $x = 0$ to its peak at $x = 100$ m, then a vertical drop to zero. The stem does not say "free space"; take $c = 300$ m/µs.

> [!trap] The stem and its plot disagree by a factor of 100
> As printed, $2x$ at $x = 100$ m is **200 A/m**, but the plot beside it marks the peak "2". One of them is wrong (perhaps the coefficient should be 0.02 A/m²). The answers below use the **plot's scale** (peak 2 A/m, i.e. $H_y = 0.02x$). If you take the formula literally, multiply every field and current below by 100. The shapes and times do not change.

- **(a) $H_y(400\ \text{m}, t)$ for $0\le t\le10$ µs.** The wave moves toward $+x$, so $H_y(x,t) = F\big(x - c(t - 1)\big)$ with $F$ the given profile. The vertical edge at $x = 100$ m is the front; it reaches 400 m after another $300/300 = 1$ µs, at $t = 2$ µs. The record jumps to 2 A/m at 2 µs, falls linearly to 0 at $t = 7/3\approx2.33$ µs, and stays zero. The sharp edge comes first in time.
- **(b) $H_y(x, 4\ \mu\text{s})$ for $\lvert x\rvert\le1500$ m.** In 3 µs the pattern moves 900 m. For $x>0$: a ramp from 0 at 900 m to 2 A/m at 1000 m, then zero. For $x<0$ the mirror wave has $\mathbf{H}$ reversed (H is odd): a step to $-2$ A/m at $x = -1000$ m, ramping back to 0 at $-900$ m.
- **(c) $\mathbf{E}(x, 2\ \mu\text{s})$ for $\lvert x\rvert\le500$ m.** For $x>0$ the wave travels toward $+\hat x$ with $\mathbf{H}\parallel\hat y$, so $\mathbf{E} = \eta_0\mathbf{H}\times\hat x$ points along $\hat y\times\hat x = -\hat z$. $\mathbf{E}$ is even in $x$. With the front now at $\lvert x\rvert = 400$ m: $E_z = -2\eta_0(\lvert x\rvert - 300)/100$ for $300<\lvert x\rvert<400$ m, from 0 to $-2\eta_0\approx-754$ V/m at $\lvert x\rvert\to400$ m, and zero elsewhere.
- **(d) The current.** Apply the jump condition with $\hat n = \hat x$: $\mathbf{J}_s = \hat x\times\big(\mathbf{H}(0^+) - \mathbf{H}(0^-)\big) = \hat z\,2H_y(0^+, t)$. At $x = 0^+$, $H_y = F\big(c(1 - t)\big)$, nonzero for $2/3<t<1$ µs:
$$
\mathbf{J}_s(t) = \hat z\,12\Big(1 - \frac{t}{1\ \mu\text{s}}\Big)\ \text{A/m}\quad\text{for } \tfrac23\ \mu\text{s}<t<1\ \mu\text{s},\ \ \text{zero otherwise}.
$$
It jumps to 4 A/m at $t = 2/3$ µs and ramps to zero at 1 µs. (Formula scale: $1200(1 - t)$ A/m, peak 400 A/m.) Checks: $\mathbf{E}$ along $-\hat z$ opposes $\mathbf{J}_s$ along $+\hat z$, and $E_z(0,t) = -\tfrac{\eta_0}{2}J_{s,z}(t)$ ✓.

This is the notes' Example 4 run backwards: there a current gave the fields, here the fields give the current.

## 9. Connections

- **Lecture 18.** Every field on this page is a d'Alembert solution, paired by $\mathbf{H} = \hat u\times\mathbf{E}/\eta$. Lecture 19 adds the source, the outgoing (causal) choice of sign, and the amplitude $\eta J_s/2$ that Lecture 18 left open.
- **Lecture 13.** The static sheet field $\tfrac12\mathbf{J}_s\times\hat n$ is Step 1 of the notes' route. A time-varying sheet keeps the same $\mathbf{H}$, delayed, and adds an $\mathbf{E}$.
- **Lecture 16.** The two tangential boundary conditions do the work in the slides' route and serve as the checks in the notes' route. The displacement current is what delays the static field into a wave.
- **Lecture 15.** A line filled with one medium has $1/\sqrt{\mathcal{L}\mathcal{C}} = v$, the plane-wave speed. Here is a further link, this site's own: a wide parallel-plate line of width $W$ and spacing $d$ has $\sqrt{\mathcal{L}/\mathcal{C}} = \eta\,d/W$. Feed a current $I = J_sW$ into the middle of an infinitely long one, and it sees two such lines in parallel, $\eta d/(2W)$. The voltage is $V = I\eta d/(2W)$, so $E = V/d = \eta J_s/2$: the sheet's result. Seen from the source, a current sheet is a current source driving two lines, each of impedance $\eta$ per square. Unit 4 develops the line picture.

## 10. Summary

- $f(t\mp z/v)$ waves are **uniform plane TEM waves**: $\mathbf{E},\mathbf{H}\perp$ the travel, the travel along $\mathbf{S} = \mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{H}$, and the same field over each plane of constant phase. Those planes move at the phase velocity $v$.
- Notes' Examples 1–3: $\mathbf{B} = -\hat z\,\triangle/c$ for $\hat x\triangle(t - y/c)$, found by rotation or by Faraday; the magnetic force is $v_\perp/c$ of the electric one; a $+z$ snapshot is the record mirrored.
- Sheet $\mathbf{J}_s(t)$ in a homogeneous lossless medium: $\mathbf{E} = -\tfrac\eta2\mathbf{J}_s(t - d/v)$, $\mathbf{H} = \tfrac12\mathbf{J}_s(t - d/v)\times\hat n$, with $\hat n$ toward the field point and $d$ the distance. $\mathbf{E}$ is even and continuous; $\mathbf{H}$ is odd and jumps by $J_s$; $\mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{H}$ points away from the sheet.
- Notes' route: static → quasi-static → delayed, with causality for the signs and uniqueness for the proof. Slides' route: $Af + Bg$, radiation condition, $E_t$ continuous, $H_t$ jump $\Rightarrow Af = \tfrac\eta2J_s$.
- $\mathbf{J}_s\cdot\mathbf{E}<0$: the sheet supplies energy to the waves (Lecture 20).
- Cosine current: $\cos(\omega t\mp\beta z)$, $\beta = \omega/v_p$, $\lambda = 2\pi/\beta$, $v_p = \lambda f$; $\mathbf{S} = \pm\hat z\,\tfrac{\eta_0J_{s0}^2}{4}\cos^2(\omega t\mp\beta z)$ W/m².
- Snapshots and records: follow world lines in the $z$–$t$ plane; a probe at distance $d$ records the source delayed by $d/v$.
