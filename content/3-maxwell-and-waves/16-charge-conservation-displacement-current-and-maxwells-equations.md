*Lecture 16 · course notes §16 · slides "Conservation of Charge / Boundary Conditions" (the displacement current itself is on the Lecture 13 deck) · prev: [[2-magnetostatics/15-inductance-magnetic-energy-and-the-potentials|Lecture 15]] · next: [[3-maxwell-and-waves/17-magnetization-and-maxwells-equations-in-matter|Lecture 17]] · practice: [[practice/16-charge-conservation-and-displacement-current|12 problems]]*

> [!abstract] In one breath
> Charge is never created or destroyed on its own, so the charge inside any volume can only change by current crossing its boundary: $\oint\mathbf{J}\cdot d\mathbf{S} = -dQ/dt$, or at a point, $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{J} = -\partial\rho/\partial t$ — the **continuity equation**. Now take the divergence of the static Ampère law $\nabla\times\mathbf{H} = \mathbf{J}$: the left side is identically zero, so it insists $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{J} = 0$, which is false wherever charge is accumulating (a charging capacitor, a draining sphere, any antenna). Maxwell's 1861 repair adds the **displacement current** $\partial\mathbf{D}/\partial t$; its divergence is $\partial\rho/\partial t$ by Gauss's law, and the contradiction becomes the continuity equation. That one term closes the system — changing $\mathbf{E}$ now makes $\mathbf{H}$ just as changing $\mathbf{B}$ makes $\mathbf{E}$ — and predicts electromagnetic waves, confirmed by Hertz in 1888. The lecture ends by restating the four boundary conditions for the dynamic case: the same four, derived from the integral laws with a pillbox and a loop.

Every process that makes or removes charge does so in $\pm$ pairs: photoionization turns a neutral atom into a free ion and a free electron (bound carriers become free ones — net charge unchanged); recombination reverses it; electron–positron annihilation and pair creation do the same at higher energy. So if the total charge $Q_V = \int_V\rho\,dV$ in a fixed volume changes, the change must be transported across the bounding surface $S$ by a current.

Make it quantitative with two open surfaces $S_1$ (below) and $S_2$ (above) spanning the same rim and together enclosing $V$, both with their $d\mathbf{S}$ "upward": $I_1 = \int_{S_1}\mathbf{J}\cdot d\mathbf{S}_1$ enters $V$ through the bottom and $I_2 = \int_{S_2}\mathbf{J}\cdot d\mathbf{S}_2$ leaves through the top, so $dQ_V/dt = I_1 - I_2$. Rewritten with the **outward** normal on the closed surface $S = S_1\cup S_2$ (which flips the sign on $S_1$):

> [!key] Continuity equation
> $$
> \oint_S\mathbf{J}\cdot d\mathbf{S} = -\frac{dQ_V}{dt} = -\frac{d}{dt}\int_V\rho\,dV\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \nabla\cdot\mathbf{J} = -\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t},
> $$
> the second by the divergence theorem ([[1-electrostatics/04-divergence-and-curl|Lecture 4]]) and "true for every $V$". Net current **out** = rate of **decrease** of the enclosed charge; where current lines diverge, charge is being depleted. It is the mathematical form of charge conservation, nothing more and nothing less.

> [!example] Example (slides) — rate of decrease of charge in a unit cube
> $\mathbf{J} = (x, y, z)$ A/m² in the cube $0<x,y,z<1$ m. $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{J} = 1+1+1 = 3$ A/m³, so $-dQ/dt = \int_V\nabla\cdot\mathbf{J}\,dV = 3$ A: the charge inside *decreases* at 3 C/s. Face by face: $J_x = 1$ on $x = 1$ and $0$ on $x = 0$, so 1 A leaves through the $x$ faces; likewise $y$ and $z$ — 3 A out ✓. The underlined word in the problem is *decrease*; the sign is the whole point.

Three places this equation has already been at work. [[1-electrostatics/08-conductors-dielectrics-and-polarization|Lecture 8]] combined it with $\mathbf{J} = \sigma\mathbf{E}$ and Gauss's law to get $\partial\rho/\partial t = -(\sigma/\epsilon)\rho$ — the relaxation time $\epsilon/\sigma$. [[2-magnetostatics/13-current-sheets-solenoids-and-the-vector-potential|Lecture 13]] needed $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{J} = 0$ (its static case) for the Coulomb-gauge solution of $\nabla^2\mathbf{A} = -\mu_0\mathbf{J}$ to be consistent. And the lossy capacitor of [[1-electrostatics/10-capacitance-and-conductance|Lecture 10]], $I = C\,dV/dt + GV$, is the continuity equation for the charge on one plate: current in, minus leakage out, equals $dQ/dt$.

## 2. The asymmetry, and Maxwell's displacement current

Faraday's law says a changing $\mathbf{B}$ induces an $\mathbf{E}$; Ampère's law as written, $\nabla\times\mathbf{H} = \mathbf{J}$, says nothing about a changing $\mathbf{E}$ inducing an $\mathbf{H}$. Maxwell noticed the asymmetry — and, more decisively, an inconsistency. The divergence of a curl is identically zero (the identity behind $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B} = 0$ for $\mathbf{B} = \nabla\times\mathbf{A}$), so the static law forces $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{J} = 0$ everywhere, always. That contradicts the continuity equation wherever $\partial\rho/\partial t\neq0$. His repair:

> [!key] Ampère–Maxwell equation
> $$
> \nabla\times\mathbf{H} = \mathbf{J} + \frac{\partial\mathbf{D}}{\partial t}\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \oint_C\mathbf{H}\cdot d\mathbf{l} = \int_S\Big(\mathbf{J}+\frac{\partial\mathbf{D}}{\partial t}\Big)\cdot d\mathbf{S}.
> $$
> $\partial\mathbf{D}/\partial t$ is the **displacement current density** [A/m²]; through a surface it is $I_d = \dfrac{d}{dt}\int_S\mathbf{D}\cdot d\mathbf{S} = d\psi_E/dt$, the rate of change of electric flux, in amperes. **Verification:** take the divergence. $0 = \nabla\cdot\mathbf{J} + \dfrac{\partial}{\partial t}\nabla\cdot\mathbf{D} = \nabla\cdot\mathbf{J} + \dfrac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}$ by Gauss's law — assumed, as Maxwell assumed it, to hold unchanged for time-varying fields — which is exactly the continuity equation. The extra term is not optional; it is what charge conservation *requires*.

> [!intuition] What the displacement current is
> In matter, $\partial\mathbf{D}/\partial t = \epsilon_0\,\partial\mathbf{E}/\partial t + \partial\mathbf{P}/\partial t$. The second piece is the **polarization current** of [[1-electrostatics/11-lorentz-drude-models-for-conductivity-and-susceptibility#5-polarization-current-bound-charges-that-carry-an-ac-current|Lecture 11]] — bound charges really moving. The first piece exists in empty space, with no charges moving at all; it is a genuinely new source of magnetic field, and the one that lets a wave propagate through vacuum: changing $\mathbf{E}$ makes $\mathbf{H}$, changing $\mathbf{H}$ makes $\mathbf{E}$, and the pair runs away from the source at $1/\sqrt{\mu_0\epsilon_0} = c$. Hertz produced and detected such waves around 1888; that is the experimental confirmation of this term.

> [!example] Example (slides) — MMF around a draining point charge
> A point charge $Q(t)$ at the origin drains through a wire along $+z$, $I = -dQ/dt$. Find $\oint_C\mathbf{H}\cdot d\mathbf{l}$ (the MMF) around a circle of radius $a$ in the plane $z = 0$, counter-clockwise seen from $+z$. The integral form lets you pick *any* surface on $C$; the hint says to think of a sphere and a hemisphere.
> - **Dome above** (pierced by the wire; outward normal consistent with $C$): conduction term $+I$; by Gauss's law the whole sphere carries flux $Q$, so the dome carries $Q/2$: displacement term $\dfrac{d}{dt}\dfrac Q2 = -\dfrac I2$. MMF $= I - I/2 = I/2$.
> - **Bowl below** (no wire through it): conduction term 0; its flux, with the orientation inherited from $C$, is $-Q/2$: displacement term $+I/2$. MMF $= I/2$ again.
>
> Static Ampère would have given $I$ for the dome and $0$ for the bowl — a circulation that depends on which surface you imagine, which is absurd. The answer $I/2$ is also what Biot–Savart gives for a semi-infinite wire ending at the origin ($H_\phi = I/4\pi a$ in the plane of its end). The textbook version of this example is the charging capacitor: a surface through the wire sees $I$, a surface through the gap sees $d\psi_E/dt = dQ/dt = I$.

> [!example] Example (slides) — displacement current through an area
> $\mathbf{E} = E_0\,t\,e^{-t^2}\hat z$ in free space; $A = 0.1$ m² in the $xy$-plane with $d\mathbf{S} = \hat z\,dS$. $\psi_E = \int\mathbf{D}\cdot d\mathbf{S} = \epsilon_0AE_0\,te^{-t^2}$ [C], so $I_d = d\psi_E/dt = \epsilon_0AE_0(1-2t^2)e^{-t^2}$ [A], with $\epsilon_0A = 8.85\times10^{-13}$ F. It reverses sign at $t = 1/\sqrt2$ s when the field stops growing. Units: $\epsilon_0$ [F/m] × area [m²] × $E$ [V/m] / time [s] = F·V/s = C/s = A ✓.

## 3. Maxwell's equations, complete

> [!key] Maxwell's equations (differential form, as the notes list them)
> $$
> \begin{gathered}
> \nabla\cdot\mathbf{D} = \rho\ \ (\text{Gauss}),\qquad \nabla\cdot\mathbf{B} = 0,\\[4pt]
> \nabla\times\mathbf{E} = -\frac{\partial\mathbf{B}}{\partial t}\ \ (\text{Faraday}),\qquad \nabla\times\mathbf{H} = \mathbf{J}+\frac{\partial\mathbf{D}}{\partial t}\ \ (\text{Ampère–Maxwell}),
> \end{gathered}
> $$
> with $\mathbf{D} = \epsilon_0\mathbf{E}$ and $\mathbf{B} = \mu_0\mathbf{H}$ if $\rho$ and $\mathbf{J}$ include *all* charges and currents, bound as well as free (the microscopic form, valid down to about the Compton wavelength); or, equivalently, with $\mathbf{D} = \epsilon\mathbf{E}$ and $\mathbf{B} = \mu\mathbf{H}$ and only the *free* $\rho$, $\mathbf{J}$ (the macroscopic form, Lecture 17). Integral forms: $\oint_S\mathbf{D}\cdot d\mathbf{S} = Q_{\text{enc}}$, $\oint_S\mathbf{B}\cdot d\mathbf{S} = 0$, $\oint_C\mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{l} = -\int_S\partial_t\mathbf{B}\cdot d\mathbf{S}$, $\oint_C\mathbf{H}\cdot d\mathbf{l} = \int_S(\mathbf{J}+\partial_t\mathbf{D})\cdot d\mathbf{S}$, plus the Lorentz force to say what the fields do.

This is the destination announced on day one ([[1-electrostatics/01-fields-forces-and-the-maxwell-roadmap|Lecture 1]]). Lectures 2–13 derived the static pieces; [[2-magnetostatics/14-faradays-law-and-induced-emf|Lecture 14]] replaced $\nabla\times\mathbf{E} = 0$ by Faraday's law; this lecture replaces $\nabla\times\mathbf{H} = \mathbf{J}$ by Ampère–Maxwell. Everything from here to the end of the course — waves, Poynting, phasors, reflection, transmission lines — is these four equations plus constitutive relations and boundary conditions, with no new physics.

**The unnamed equation.** $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B} = 0$ is not independent in the way the others are. Take the divergence of Faraday's law: $0 = \nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf{E}) = -\dfrac{\partial}{\partial t}\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B}$, so $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B}$ at every point is **constant in time**. It was zero for the static fields of Lectures 12–13, so it stays zero when fields start to vary. What this expresses physically is the absence of magnetic charge: if magnetic monopoles existed, $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B}$ would equal their density and $\mathbf{B}$-lines would start and end on them. None has ever been observed, at any scale or at any epoch of the universe accessible to astronomy. So $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B} = 0$.

> [!key] The four boundary conditions ($\hat n$ from medium 2 into medium 1; the notes' $\pm$ superscripts mean the same)
> $$
> \begin{gathered}
> \hat n\cdot(\mathbf{D}_1-\mathbf{D}_2) = \rho_s,\qquad \hat n\cdot(\mathbf{B}_1-\mathbf{B}_2) = 0,\\[4pt]
> \hat n\times(\mathbf{E}_1-\mathbf{E}_2) = 0,\qquad \hat n\times(\mathbf{H}_1-\mathbf{H}_2) = \mathbf{J}_s .
> \end{gathered}
> $$
> They hold for time-varying fields exactly as for static ones. The first and third were derived in [[1-electrostatics/06-circulation-and-boundary-conditions|Lecture 6]]; the second is $\oint\mathbf{B}\cdot d\mathbf{S} = 0$ on a pillbox of vanishing height (no magnetic charge, so no jump); the fourth is Ampère–Maxwell on a thin rectangle straddling the interface — the $\partial\mathbf{D}/\partial t$ flux through the rectangle vanishes with its width $w$, exactly as the $\partial\mathbf{B}/\partial t$ flux did in Lecture 6, and only a *surface* current $\mathbf{J}_s$ survives the limit (the course notes defer the detailed justification to Lecture 19). [[2-magnetostatics/13-current-sheets-solenoids-and-the-vector-potential|Lecture 13]]'s current sheet is the fourth condition in action: $\mathbf{H}$ jumps by $J_s$ across it.

Two special cases carry most of the course from here on:

- **Two perfect dielectrics** ($\sigma = 0$, so $\mathbf{J}_s = 0$, and no free charge, so $\rho_s = 0$): $D_n$, $E_t$, $B_n$, $H_t$ all continuous. No surface source sits on the interface, yet the fields still refract ([[1-electrostatics/09-static-fields-in-dielectric-media|Lecture 9]]): $E_n$ and $D_t$ change by the permittivity ratio wherever $\epsilon_1\neq\epsilon_2$. If the two media also differ in permeability, $B = \mu H$ turns continuity of $B_n$ into $\mu_1H_{1n} = \mu_2H_{2n}$ — the magnetic twin of $\epsilon_1E_{1n} = \epsilon_2E_{2n}$, written in ink on the slide and taken up in [[3-maxwell-and-waves/17-magnetization-and-maxwells-equations-in-matter|Lecture 17]].
- **Surface of a perfect conductor** (medium 2, $\sigma\to\infty$, with $\mathbf{E} = 0$ inside): Faraday's law inside gives $\partial\mathbf{B}/\partial t = -\nabla\times\mathbf{E} = 0$, so no *time-varying* $\mathbf{B}$ can exist in a perfect conductor either — that is the reason, argued in class, for setting $\mathbf{H} = 0$ inside. Then $E_{1t} = 0$ and $B_{1n} = 0$: the electric field meets the surface *normally* and ends on $\rho_s = \hat n\cdot\mathbf{D}_1$; the magnetic field runs *tangentially* and is carried by $\mathbf{J}_s = \hat n\times\mathbf{H}_1$, equivalently $\mathbf{H}_{1t} = \mathbf{J}_s\times\hat n$. (A typed slide writes "$D_{1n} = \rho$"; it means $\rho_s$, and the in-class ink corrects it.)

> [!example] Two slide exercises (worked in class)
> *Surface charge from $\mathbf{D}$ on a conductor.* (a) $\mathbf{D} = D_0\langle1,-2,2\rangle$ pointing *away* from the surface: $\hat n$ (out of the conductor) is $\langle1,-2,2\rangle/3$, so $\rho_s = \hat n\cdot\mathbf{D} = D_0(1+4+4)/3 = 3D_0$. (b) $\mathbf{D} = D_0\langle1,0,\sqrt3\rangle$ pointing *toward* the surface: $\hat n = -\langle1,0,\sqrt3\rangle/2$ and $\rho_s = -2D_0$ — negative charge, field lines ending on it.
> *Which pictures are possible just outside a perfect conductor?* An oblique arrow: neither $\mathbf{E}$ nor $\mathbf{H}$. A tangential arrow: $\mathbf{H}$ only ($B_{1n} = 0$, $\hat n\times\mathbf{H}_1 = \mathbf{J}_s$). A normal arrow: $\mathbf{E}$ only ($E_{1t} = 0$, $\hat n\cdot\mathbf{D}_1 = \rho_s$). Answer (e), circled in class. A quick way to get the sign of $\rho_s$ in the first exercise: when $\mathbf{D}$ points away from the conductor, $\hat n = \mathbf{D}/\lvert\mathbf{D}\rvert$ and $\rho_s = \lvert\mathbf{D}\rvert$; when it points in, $\rho_s = -\lvert\mathbf{D}\rvert$.

> [!trap] At a boundary, use the integral forms
> The slide's closing rule, in bold: **never** use the differential Maxwell equations at a boundary — the fields are discontinuous there and their derivatives are δ-functions. Apply the integral laws to a pillbox or a thin loop, shrink, and read off the jump. (The mnemonic in [[concepts/boundary-conditions]] — replace $\nabla$ by $\hat n$, the field by its difference, $\rho$ by $\rho_s$, $\mathbf{J}$ by $\mathbf{J}_s$, and drop the time derivatives — is the result of doing that, not a substitute for it.)

## 5. Summary

- Charge conservation: $\oint\mathbf{J}\cdot d\mathbf{S} = -dQ/dt$, $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{J} = -\partial\rho/\partial t$. Out = decrease.
- $\nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf{H}) = 0$ forces a second term: $\nabla\times\mathbf{H} = \mathbf{J}+\partial\mathbf{D}/\partial t$; its divergence *is* the continuity equation (with Gauss). $I_d = d\psi_E/dt$ [A]; in matter $\partial\mathbf{D}/\partial t = \epsilon_0\partial_t\mathbf{E} + \partial_t\mathbf{P}$.
- Two surfaces on one loop must agree: the draining charge gives $I/2$ both ways; the capacitor gives $I$ both ways.
- Maxwell's equations complete; Hertz 1888; $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B} = 0$ from Faraday + no monopoles.
- Boundary conditions unchanged by time variation: $D_n$ jumps by $\rho_s$, $E_t$ continuous, $B_n$ continuous, $H_t$ jumps by $J_s$. PEC: $\mathbf{E}$ normal (ends on $\rho_s$), $\mathbf{H}$ tangential (carried by $\mathbf{J}_s$). Integral forms at boundaries.

Concept pages: [[concepts/continuity-equation]] · [[concepts/displacement-current]] · [[concepts/maxwells-equations]] · [[concepts/boundary-conditions]].

> [!exam] On exams
> Four things get asked. (1) A continuity computation: given $\mathbf{J}$, find the rate of change of charge in a region — divergence theorem, and say *decrease* or *increase*. (2) A displacement-current computation: given $\mathbf{E}(t)$ and an area, $I_d = \epsilon\,d/dt\int\mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{S}$, with units checked. (3) The conceptual question — why the term is needed (divergence of a curl is zero; charge conservation), or the two-surface paradox. (4) Boundary conditions with a surface current: $\hat n\times(\mathbf{H}_1-\mathbf{H}_2) = \mathbf{J}_s$ and the perfect-conductor cases, with $\hat n$ drawn first — the same rubric as Exam 1 problem 3, now with four conditions instead of two. Later: every reflection problem is condition-matching at an interface.
