> [!abstract] In one breath
> [[3-maxwell-and-waves/19-dalembert-solutions-and-radiation-from-current-sheets|Lecture 19]] stated that the Poynting vector $\mathbf{S} = \mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{H}$ carries power. This lecture derives it. Dot Faraday's law with $\mathbf{H}$, dot the Ampère–Maxwell law with $\mathbf{E}$, subtract, and Maxwell's equations become **Poynting's theorem**, $\partial w/\partial t + \nabla\cdot\mathbf{S} + \mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{E} = 0$. Here $w = \tfrac12\epsilon E^2 + \tfrac12\mu H^2$ is the stored energy density, the $\tfrac12\epsilon E^2$ of Lecture 10 plus the $\tfrac12\mu H^2$ of Lecture 15. The theorem has the shape of the continuity equation of [[3-maxwell-and-waves/16-charge-conservation-displacement-current-and-maxwells-equations|Lecture 16]], with $w$ in the role of $\rho$ and $\mathbf{S}$ in the role of $\mathbf{J}$. It also has one extra term, $\mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{E}$: the power per cubic metre that the field hands to charges. In a resistor that is Joule heating, $\sigma E^2$. Where it is negative (inside a battery, or on Lecture 19's radiating sheet) the charges feed energy into the field. Integrated over a volume, the theorem says that stored energy can only leave through the surface or go to the charges inside; the $I^2R$ of a resistor, for example, enters through its sides. The notes' example is a sheet current $\hat x\,2\cos\omega t$ A/m. It launches **monochromatic** (single-frequency) waves whose $\mathbf{S} = \pm\hat z\,\eta_0\cos^2(\omega t\mp\beta z)$ always points away from the sheet. With $\langle\cos^2\rangle = \tfrac12$, each side carries $\eta_0/2\approx188$ W/m² on average, and the sheet injects $\langle-\mathbf{J}_s\cdot\mathbf{E}\rangle = \eta_0\approx377$ W/m², exactly the sum.

## 1. Where does the energy go?

Several earlier results are pieces of one energy story.

- A charged capacitor stores $\tfrac12CV^2$. [[1-electrostatics/10-capacitance-and-conductance#5-energy-stored-in-a-capacitor-and-in-the-field|Lecture 10]] relocated that energy into the field, at $\tfrac12\epsilon E^2$ per cubic metre. A current-carrying inductor stores $\tfrac12LI^2$, which [[2-magnetostatics/15-inductance-magnetic-energy-and-the-potentials|Lecture 15]] relocated at $\tfrac12\mu H^2$ per cubic metre.
- A resistor turns electrical power into heat. A leaky capacitor ([[1-electrostatics/10-capacitance-and-conductance#6-conductance-the-leaky-capacitor|Lecture 10 §6]]) loses its stored energy that way; the notes for Lecture 10 call it ohmic dissipation.
- On the radiating sheet of [[3-maxwell-and-waves/19-dalembert-solutions-and-radiation-from-current-sheets#5-reading-the-result|Lecture 19]], $\mathbf{E}$ points against $\mathbf{J}_s$, and two waves carry $\mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{H}$ away from it.

What ties these together? For charge, [[3-maxwell-and-waves/16-charge-conservation-displacement-current-and-maxwells-equations#1-charge-conservation-and-the-continuity-equation|Lecture 16]] answered with the continuity equation: the charge in a volume changes only by current crossing its surface. Energy is conserved too, but *electromagnetic* energy is not conserved on its own. It turns into heat in a resistor and is created from chemical energy in a battery. So a ledger for field energy needs a flux term *and* an exchange term. The notes extract exactly that from Maxwell's equations, with no new physics added. The result is Poynting's theorem.

## 2. Deriving the theorem from Maxwell's equations

Start from the two curl equations of [[3-maxwell-and-waves/16-charge-conservation-displacement-current-and-maxwells-equations#3-maxwells-equations-complete|Lecture 16]] and follow the notes' two steps.

**Step 1: dot Faraday's law with H, and the Ampère–Maxwell law with E.**

$$
\begin{gathered}
\mathbf{H}\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf{E}) = -\mathbf{H}\cdot\frac{\partial\mathbf{B}}{\partial t},\\[4pt]
\mathbf{E}\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf{H}) = \mathbf{E}\cdot\mathbf{J} + \mathbf{E}\cdot\frac{\partial\mathbf{D}}{\partial t}.
\end{gathered}
$$

Each line is now a scalar, and every term has units of power per unit volume (W/m³).

**Step 2: subtract the second line from the first.**

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\underbrace{\mathbf{H}\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf{E}) - \mathbf{E}\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf{H})}_{\nabla\cdot(\mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{H})}\\
&\qquad = -\mathbf{E}\cdot\frac{\partial\mathbf{D}}{\partial t} - \mathbf{H}\cdot\frac{\partial\mathbf{B}}{\partial t} - \mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{E}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Two facts tidy this up, and the notes write both as underbraces.

- **The left side** is a divergence, by the identity $\nabla\cdot(\mathbf{A}\times\mathbf{B}) = \mathbf{B}\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf{A}) - \mathbf{A}\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf{B})$ of the [[0-toolkit/02-vector-calculus-cheatsheet#identities-valid-for-any-smooth-fields|toolkit]], with $\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{E}$ and $\mathbf{B} = \mathbf{H}$. The derivation is folded below.
- **The first two terms on the right** are a time derivative. In a linear medium whose $\epsilon$ and $\mu$ do not change in time, $\mathbf{D} = \epsilon\mathbf{E}$ and $\mathbf{B} = \mu\mathbf{H}$. Then the chain rule run backwards, $\partial(\mathbf{E}\cdot\mathbf{E})/\partial t = 2\,\mathbf{E}\cdot\partial\mathbf{E}/\partial t$, gives

$$
\begin{gathered}
\mathbf{E}\cdot\frac{\partial\mathbf{D}}{\partial t} = \frac{\partial}{\partial t}\Big(\tfrac12\epsilon\,\mathbf{E}\cdot\mathbf{E}\Big),\\[4pt]
\mathbf{H}\cdot\frac{\partial\mathbf{B}}{\partial t} = \frac{\partial}{\partial t}\Big(\tfrac12\mu\,\mathbf{H}\cdot\mathbf{H}\Big).
\end{gathered}
$$

> [!key] Poynting's theorem (point form)
> $$
> \begin{gathered}
> \frac{\partial}{\partial t}\Big(\tfrac12\epsilon\,\mathbf{E}\cdot\mathbf{E} + \tfrac12\mu\,\mathbf{H}\cdot\mathbf{H}\Big)\\
> {} + \nabla\cdot(\mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{H}) + \mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{E} = 0 ,
> \end{gathered}
> $$
> or, with the stored energy density $w\equiv\tfrac12\epsilon E^2 + \tfrac12\mu H^2$ [J/m³] and the Poynting vector $\mathbf{S}\equiv\mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{H}$ [W/m²],
> $$
> \frac{\partial w}{\partial t} + \nabla\cdot\mathbf{S} + \mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{E} = 0\qquad[\text{W/m}^3].
> $$
> It holds at every point and every instant, for fields that obey Maxwell's equations in a linear, isotropic medium whose $\epsilon$ and $\mu$ do not change in time (they may vary from place to place). $\mathbf{J}$ is the free current; bound charges and currents are already inside $\epsilon$ and $\mu$. The notes use these conditions without stating them.

The conditions are not a formality. In a ferromagnet with hysteresis ([[3-maxwell-and-waves/17-magnetization-and-maxwells-equations-in-matter|Lecture 17]]), $\mathbf{B}$ is not $\mu\mathbf{H}$ with a fixed $\mu$, so $\mathbf{H}\cdot\partial\mathbf{B}/\partial t$ is not the rate of change of a stored energy: part of it is lost as heat in every cycle (the area of the loop).

> [!derivation]- The vector identity, and the same result in one dimension
> Write out the divergence of the cross product in Cartesian components:
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> \nabla\cdot(\mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{H}) &= \frac{\partial}{\partial x}(E_yH_z - E_zH_y)\\
> &\quad + \frac{\partial}{\partial y}(E_zH_x - E_xH_z)\\
> &\quad + \frac{\partial}{\partial z}(E_xH_y - E_yH_x).
> \end{aligned}
> $$
> The product rule splits each of the six products into two pieces. Collect the six pieces in which $\mathbf{E}$ is differentiated:
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> &H_x\Big(\frac{\partial E_z}{\partial y} - \frac{\partial E_y}{\partial z}\Big) + H_y\Big(\frac{\partial E_x}{\partial z} - \frac{\partial E_z}{\partial x}\Big)\\
> &\quad + H_z\Big(\frac{\partial E_y}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial E_x}{\partial y}\Big) = \mathbf{H}\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf{E}).
> \end{aligned}
> $$
> The six pieces in which $\mathbf{H}$ is differentiated collect the same way, except that each bracket comes out with its two terms in the opposite order. They give $-\mathbf{E}\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf{H})$.
>
> **One-dimensional check (the route in Rao's textbook).** Take the planar fields of [[3-maxwell-and-waves/18-the-wave-equation-and-plane-tem-waves#3-the-planar-problem-only-exzt-and-hyzt|Lecture 18]], $\mathbf{E} = \hat x\,E_x(z,t)$ and $\mathbf{H} = \hat y\,H_y(z,t)$, and allow a current $J_x$. The two component equations are $\partial E_x/\partial z = -\mu\,\partial H_y/\partial t$ and $-\partial H_y/\partial z = J_x + \epsilon\,\partial E_x/\partial t$. With $S_z = E_xH_y$,
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> \frac{\partial S_z}{\partial z} &= H_y\frac{\partial E_x}{\partial z} + E_x\frac{\partial H_y}{\partial z}\\
> &= -\mu H_y\frac{\partial H_y}{\partial t} - E_x\Big(J_x + \epsilon\frac{\partial E_x}{\partial t}\Big)\\
> &= -\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\Big(\tfrac12\mu H_y^2 + \tfrac12\epsilon E_x^2\Big) - J_xE_x ,
> \end{aligned}
> $$
> which is the theorem for this field. This site's verification script also checks the general form numerically. It builds fields from arbitrary potentials, so that Faraday's law holds, and defines $\mathbf{J}$ from Ampère–Maxwell. The three terms then cancel to about one part in a million at every point tested.

## 3. Reading the three terms

Every term is a power per unit volume. Read the theorem as: rate of increase of stored energy, plus net outflow, plus power handed to charges, equals zero.

**$\partial w/\partial t$: stored energy changing.** $w = w_e + w_m$, with $w_e = \tfrac12\epsilon E^2$ and $w_m = \tfrac12\mu H^2$ [J/m³]. Lecture 10 found $w_e$ by spreading $\tfrac12CV^2$ over a capacitor's gap, and Lecture 15 found $w_m$ by spreading $\tfrac12LI^2$ over a solenoid's interior ([[concepts/electrostatic-energy]], [[concepts/magnetic-energy]]). Poynting's theorem shows that the same densities work for any time-varying field, not only for the two devices they were read off. Picture each cubic metre of field holding an amount of energy that rises and falls as the fields change.

**$\nabla\cdot\mathbf{S}$: energy flowing away.** $\mathbf{S} = \mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{H}$ is the **energy flux density**. Its direction is the direction in which energy flows, and its size is the power crossing a unit area perpendicular to it. The notes' units are (V/m)(A/m) = VA/m² = W/m² = (J/s)/m², which is exactly how $\mathbf{J}$ counts charge, in (C/s)/m² = A/m². Where $\mathbf{S}$ lines spread out ($\nabla\cdot\mathbf{S}>0$), energy is leaving that point.

**$\mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{E}$: energy handed to charges.** Charge of density $\rho$ moving with velocity $\mathbf{u}$ feels a force $\rho(\mathbf{E} + \mathbf{u}\times\mathbf{B})$ per unit volume. The magnetic part is perpendicular to $\mathbf{u}$ and does no work ([[2-magnetostatics/12-magnetic-force-biot-savart-and-amperes-law|Lecture 12]]). So the field does work on the charges at the rate $\rho\,\mathbf{E}\cdot\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{E}$ per unit volume, with $\mathbf{J} = \rho\mathbf{u}$. The notes call this term **Joule heating**. In an ohmic conductor, $\mathbf{J} = \sigma\mathbf{E}$ ([[1-electrostatics/08-conductors-dielectrics-and-polarization#1-two-ideal-materials|Lecture 8]]), so

$$
\mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{E} = \sigma E^2 = \frac{J^2}{\sigma}\ge0\qquad[\text{W/m}^3],
$$

the point form of $I^2R$. The carriers of the Drude model ([[1-electrostatics/11-lorentz-drude-models-for-conductivity-and-susceptibility|Lecture 11]]) pass this energy to the lattice in collisions, as heat. The same term becomes kinetic energy for an electron beam in vacuum, and chemical energy in a battery being charged. The notes' parenthesis is worth keeping: the term can be non-zero only where there is a current.

**Negative $\mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{E}$ is a source.** If the current flows *against* $\mathbf{E}$ (positive charges pushed against the field, or negative ones along it), something other than the field is driving the charges, and that something delivers energy *to* the field. So $-\mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{E}$ is the power injected per unit volume. Inside a battery the current flows from the negative terminal to the positive one, against the field of the terminal charges ([[1-electrostatics/05-curl-free-fields-and-the-electrostatic-potential#1-why-a-potential-energy-per-unit-charge|Lecture 5]]). On Lecture 19's sheet, $\mathbf{E} = -\tfrac\eta2\mathbf{J}_s$ points against the current. The notes compare this with circuits: in the passive sign convention an element with $vi>0$ absorbs power, and one with $vi<0$ supplies it.

> [!key] Poynting's theorem next to the continuity equation
> | | charge (Lecture 16) | electromagnetic energy (Lecture 20) |
> |---|---|---|
> | density | $\rho$ [C/m³] | $w = \tfrac12\epsilon E^2 + \tfrac12\mu H^2$ [J/m³] |
> | flux density | $\mathbf{J}$ [A/m² = (C/s)/m²] | $\mathbf{S} = \mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{H}$ [W/m² = (J/s)/m²] |
> | point form | $\partial\rho/\partial t + \nabla\cdot\mathbf{J} = 0$ | $\partial w/\partial t + \nabla\cdot\mathbf{S} = -\mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{E}$ |
> | exchange term | none: charge is never created or destroyed | $\mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{E}$: to heat, motion or chemistry ($>0$), or from them ($<0$) |
> | density moving at a speed | $\mathbf{J} = \rho\,\mathbf{u}$ | $\mathbf{S} = v\,w\,\hat u$ for one plane wave (§7) |
>
> The notes' summary: Poynting's theorem is the conservation law for electromagnetic energy, just as the continuity equation is the conservation law for charge.

**Four situations, four combinations of terms.** Which terms are non-zero tells you the story.

| situation | $\partial w/\partial t$ | $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{S}$ | $\mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{E}$ | what happens |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| plane wave in a lossless medium | oscillates | $-\partial w/\partial t$ | 0 | pure transport, at the wave speed |
| leaky capacitor discharging | $-\sigma E^2$ | 0 | $\sigma E^2$ | stored energy becomes heat where it is |
| wire with a steady current (§5) | 0 | $-\sigma E^2$ | $\sigma E^2$ | energy flows in and becomes heat |
| battery; radiating sheet (§6) | 0 (on average) | positive | negative | the driven charges feed energy into the field, and it flows away |

> [!example] Example — a leaky capacitor empties without any energy flow
> A parallel-plate capacitor is filled with a lossy dielectric, $\epsilon_r = 4$ and $\sigma = 10^{-5}$ S/m. It is charged to $E_0 = 10$ kV/m (10 V across 1 mm) and disconnected. From [[1-electrostatics/10-capacitance-and-conductance#6-conductance-the-leaky-capacitor|Lecture 10]], $E = E_0e^{-t/\tau}$ with $\tau = \epsilon/\sigma = 3.54$ µs. Between the plates the conduction current density $\sigma\mathbf{E}$ and the displacement current density $\epsilon\,\partial\mathbf{E}/\partial t = -(\epsilon/\tau)\mathbf{E} = -\sigma\mathbf{E}$ cancel exactly. The total current in Ampère–Maxwell is zero, so (ignoring the edges) $\mathbf{H} = 0$ and there is no $\mathbf{S}$ at all. The theorem reduces to $\partial w/\partial t = -\mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{E}$: each cubic metre turns its own stored energy into heat.
>
> At $t = 0$, $w = \tfrac12\epsilon E_0^2 = 1.77$ mJ/m³ and $\mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{E} = \sigma E_0^2 = 1000$ W/m³. That is $2w/\tau$, the rate at which $w\propto e^{-2t/\tau}$ falls. The stored energy halves every $(\tau/2)\ln2 = 1.23$ µs, twice as fast as the charge (2.45 µs).

## 4. The integral form: a ledger for any volume

Integrate the point form over a volume $V$ and turn the divergence into a surface integral with the divergence theorem ([[0-toolkit/02-vector-calculus-cheatsheet#integral-theorems|toolkit]]). The area element is written $d\mathbf{A}$ here, pointing outward; it is the $d\mathbf{S}$ of the continuity equation, renamed so that it does not clash with $\mathbf{S}$.

> [!key] Poynting's theorem (integral form)
> $$
> \begin{gathered}
> -\frac{dW}{dt} = \oint_A\mathbf{S}\cdot d\mathbf{A} + \int_V\mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{E}\,dV,\\[4pt]
> W = \int_V\Big(\tfrac12\epsilon E^2 + \tfrac12\mu H^2\Big)dV .
> \end{gathered}
> $$
> The rate at which the stored energy inside $V$ decreases equals the power flowing out through the closed surface $A$ plus the power handed to the charges inside. Read with a source inside: the power the source supplies, $-\int\mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{E}\,dV$ over the source region, goes into storage ($dW/dt$), out through the surface, and into the heat of any resistive region inside.

This is "net current out equals the rate of decrease of charge" for energy, with one extra term.

> [!recipe] Using the integral form
> 1. **Choose the closed surface to fit the question:** a pillbox around a sheet, a cylinder around a wire, a sphere around an antenna, or a box whose faces lie beyond a wavefront.
> 2. **Outflow:** integrate $\mathbf{S}\cdot d\mathbf{A}$ over each face, with the outward normal. A face where $\mathbf{S} = 0$, or where $\mathbf{S}$ lies in the face, contributes nothing.
> 3. **Exchange:** integrate $\mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{E}$ over the volume ($\mathbf{J}_s\cdot\mathbf{E}$ over the area of any sheet inside).
> 4. **Storage:** find $dW/dt$. It is zero in a steady state, and its time average is zero in any periodic steady state (§7).
> 5. **Check** that the three add to zero. Whichever one you did not compute is now known.

> [!example] Example — DC power travels in the dielectric of a coax, not in the copper
> A coax with perfect conductors of radii $a$ and $b$ carries DC from a battery to a load. The inner conductor is at voltage $V$ above the outer one and carries the current $I$ along $+\hat z$, which returns in the outer conductor. Between the conductors $\mathbf{E} = \hat\rho\,V/\big(\rho\ln(b/a)\big)$ (the coax of [[1-electrostatics/10-capacitance-and-conductance#3-the-coaxial-cable|Lecture 10]]) and $\mathbf{H} = \hat\phi\,I/(2\pi\rho)$ (Ampère's law, Lecture 12). With $\hat\rho\times\hat\phi = \hat z$,
> $$
> \begin{gathered}
> \mathbf{S} = \hat z\,\frac{VI}{2\pi\rho^2\ln(b/a)},\\[4pt]
> \int_a^bS_z\,2\pi\rho\,d\rho = \frac{VI}{\ln(b/a)}\int_a^b\frac{d\rho}{\rho} = VI .
> \end{gathered}
> $$
> All of the power $VI$ flows through the dielectric, toward the load, mostly close to the inner conductor where $S\propto1/\rho^2$. Inside the perfect conductors $\mathbf{E} = 0$, so $\mathbf{S} = 0$: the copper guides the energy but does not carry it. Unit 4 builds transmission lines on this picture.

> [!intuition] What S does and does not tell you
> The theorem fixes $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{S}$, so only the flux of $\mathbf{S}$ through a *closed* surface is unambiguous. Reading $\mathbf{S}$ point by point as "the power through this square metre" is natural, and it is how waves, wires and cables are always described. For static fields, though, it can look strange. Put a charge next to a bar magnet: $\mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{H}\neq0$ all around them, yet nothing changes and nothing heats up. There $\nabla\times\mathbf{E} = 0$ and $\nabla\times\mathbf{H} = \mathbf{J} = 0$ (no free current), so $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{S} = 0$ everywhere. The lines of $\mathbf{S}$ close on themselves and carry no net energy into or out of any volume.

## 5. Joule heating at work: a wire is heated through its surface

A straight wire of radius $a$, length $\ell$ and conductivity $\sigma$ carries a steady current $I$ along $+\hat z$. In the steady state $\partial w/\partial t = 0$, so the theorem says $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{S} = -\sigma E^2<0$: energy must converge on every point of the wire. From where?

- **E.** Inside, Ohm's law gives $\mathbf{E} = \mathbf{J}/\sigma = \hat z\,I/(\sigma\pi a^2)$. Tangential $\mathbf{E}$ is continuous ([[3-maxwell-and-waves/16-charge-conservation-displacement-current-and-maxwells-equations#4-boundary-conditions--same-for-dynamics|Lecture 16]]), so the same $E_z$ exists just outside the surface. It is produced by surface charges on the wire, which also give the outside field a radial part ([[1-electrostatics/11-lorentz-drude-models-for-conductivity-and-susceptibility#6-what-drives-the-current-in-a-wire-a-footnote-worth-a-section|Lecture 11 §6]]).
- **H.** Ampère's law gives $\mathbf{H} = \hat\phi\,I\rho/(2\pi a^2)$ inside and $\hat\phi\,I/(2\pi\rho)$ outside.
- **S at the surface.** $\hat z\times\hat\phi = -\hat\rho$, so $\mathbf{S} = \mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{H} = -\hat\rho\,E_zH_\phi(a)$: radially **inward**, perpendicular to the current.

The power entering through the side of the wire is

$$
\begin{aligned}
E_zH_\phi(a)\cdot2\pi a\ell &= \frac{I}{\sigma\pi a^2}\cdot\frac{I}{2\pi a}\cdot2\pi a\ell\\
&= I^2\,\frac{\ell}{\sigma\pi a^2} = I^2R,
\end{aligned}
$$

with $R = \ell/(\sigma\pi a^2)$, the resistance of [[1-electrostatics/08-conductors-dielectrics-and-polarization#1-two-ideal-materials|Lecture 8]] and [[1-electrostatics/10-capacitance-and-conductance|Lecture 10]]. Nothing enters through the end faces: inside the wire $\mathbf{E}\parallel\hat z$, so $\mathbf{S}$ has no $z$-component. Inside, $S_\rho = -E_z\,I\rho/(2\pi a^2)$, and the power still crossing the cylinder of radius $\rho$ is $I^2R\,(\rho/a)^2$, exactly the heat produced inside that cylinder. The energy is absorbed bit by bit as it flows toward the axis.

## 6. The notes' example: monochromatic waves from a cosine sheet

The notes return to Lecture 19's radiator, now with a single-frequency current.

> [!example] Example 1 (notes) — a cosine current sheet in vacuum
> On the plane $z = 0$ flows the surface current $\mathbf{J}_s = \hat x\,f(t) = \hat x\,2\cos(\omega t)$ A/m, with free space on both sides. Find (a) the radiated fields $\mathbf{E}(z,t)$ and $\mathbf{H}(z,t)$ for $z\gtrless0$, (b) the Poynting vectors $\mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{H}$, and (c) $\mathbf{J}_s\cdot\mathbf{E}$ on the sheet.

**(a) The fields.** Use the sheet result of [[3-maxwell-and-waves/19-dalembert-solutions-and-radiation-from-current-sheets#3-what-launches-a-plane-wave-the-notes-route|Lecture 19 §3]], $\mathbf{E} = -\hat x\,\tfrac\eta2f(t\mp z/v)$ and $\mathbf{H} = \mp\hat y\,\tfrac12f(t\mp z/v)$ for $z\gtrless0$. In vacuum $v = c$ and $\eta = \eta_0\approx120\pi\ \Omega$. Delaying a cosine shifts its phase:

$$
\begin{gathered}
f\Big(t\mp\frac zc\Big) = 2\cos\Big(\omega t\mp\frac{\omega}{c}z\Big) = 2\cos(\omega t\mp\beta z),\\[4pt]
\beta\equiv\frac\omega c .
\end{gathered}
$$

> [!key] Example 1 (a): the radiated fields
> $$
> \begin{gathered}
> \mathbf{E}(z,t) = -\hat x\,\eta_0\cos(\omega t\mp\beta z)\ \ \text{V/m},\\[4pt]
> \mathbf{H}(z,t) = \mp\hat y\,\cos(\omega t\mp\beta z)\ \ \text{A/m},\quad z\gtrless0 .
> \end{gathered}
> $$
> Upper signs for $z>0$, lower signs for $z<0$. The amplitudes are $\eta_0\cdot2/2 = \eta_0\approx377$ V/m and $2/2 = 1$ A/m.

$\beta$ counts radians of phase per metre, and $\lambda = 2\pi/\beta$ is the wavelength ([[3-maxwell-and-waves/19-dalembert-solutions-and-radiation-from-current-sheets|Lecture 19 §7]]); [[3-maxwell-and-waves/21-monochromatic-waves-and-phasor-notation|Lecture 21]] builds that vocabulary properly. A source at one frequency $\omega$ in a linear, time-invariant medium produces fields at that same frequency everywhere. That is why the notes call them **monochromatic waves**: waves of a single "colour". They are written here as real cosines of time; the phasor shorthand is Lecture 21.

**(b) The Poynting vectors.** On each side, multiply the components and the unit vectors:

- $z>0$: $(-\eta_0\cos)(-\cos)\,\hat x\times\hat y = +\hat z\,\eta_0\cos^2(\omega t - \beta z)$;
- $z<0$: $(-\eta_0\cos)(+\cos)\,\hat x\times\hat y = -\hat z\,\eta_0\cos^2(\omega t + \beta z)$.

> [!key] Example 1 (b): the Poynting vectors
> $$
> \begin{gathered}
> \mathbf{S} = \mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{H} = \pm\hat z\,\eta_0\cos^2(\omega t\mp\beta z)\ \ \text{W/m}^2,\\[4pt]
> z\gtrless0 .
> \end{gathered}
> $$

$\mathbf{S}$ never points back toward the sheet, because $\cos^2\ge0$. The notes observe that the *time average* of $\mathbf{S}$ points along the direction of travel. For a single wave the instantaneous $\mathbf{S}$ does too, because $\mathbf{E}$ and $\mathbf{H}$ change sign together. The energy flows outward in pulses at $2\omega$; at any instant the humps of $\lvert\mathbf{S}\rvert$ are $\lambda/2$ apart, and they move away at $c$ (panel 1 of the figure below).

**(c) $\mathbf{J}_s\cdot\mathbf{E}$ on the sheet.** The electric field at $z = 0$ is the same from both sides (tangential $\mathbf{E}$ is continuous): $\mathbf{E}(0,t) = -\hat x\,\eta_0\cos\omega t$. So

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{J}_s\cdot\mathbf{E} &= \big(\hat x\,2\cos\omega t\big)\cdot\big(-\hat x\,\eta_0\cos\omega t\big)\\
&= -2\eta_0\cos^2(\omega t)\ \ \text{W/m}^2 .
\end{aligned}
$$

The unit is W/m² because this is power per unit *area* of sheet: a surface current [A/m] times a field [V/m]. It is the volume term $\mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{E}$ integrated through the sheet's thickness, since $\mathbf{J} = \mathbf{J}_s\,\delta(z)$ gives $\int\mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{E}\,dz = \mathbf{J}_s\cdot\mathbf{E}$. It is negative at every instant except the zeros of $\cos\omega t$, so the sheet is a source: whatever drives the current works against $\mathbf{E}$ and gives the field $2\eta_0\cos^2\omega t$ watts per square metre of sheet. In the notes' words, $\pm\mathbf{J}_s\cdot\mathbf{E}$ is the power absorbed or injected per unit area of a surface.

**The books balance at every instant at the sheet.** Apply the integral form to a pillbox: two faces of area $A$ just above and just below the sheet. Out through the top face goes $\mathbf{S}(0^+)\cdot\hat z\,A = \eta_0\cos^2\omega t\,A$; out through the bottom face goes $\mathbf{S}(0^-)\cdot(-\hat z)\,A = \eta_0\cos^2\omega t\,A$. A pillbox of vanishing height stores nothing, and the sheet inside contributes $\mathbf{J}_s\cdot\mathbf{E}\,A = -2\eta_0\cos^2\omega t\,A$. The three add to zero at every instant.

> [!key] Poynting's theorem across a current sheet
> $$
> \hat n\cdot(\mathbf{S}_1 - \mathbf{S}_2) = -\mathbf{J}_s\cdot\mathbf{E},
> $$
> with $\hat n$ pointing from side 2 into side 1. The jump in the normal Poynting flux equals the power per unit area that the sheet injects. It follows the pattern of the boundary conditions of [[3-maxwell-and-waves/16-charge-conservation-displacement-current-and-maxwells-equations#4-boundary-conditions--same-for-dynamics|Lecture 16]]: $\nabla$ becomes $\hat n$, the field becomes its jump, $\mathbf{J}$ becomes $\mathbf{J}_s$, and the time derivative drops out because a thin pillbox stores nothing. Here, with $\hat n = \hat z$: $\eta_0\cos^2\omega t - (-\eta_0\cos^2\omega t) = 2\eta_0\cos^2\omega t$ ✓.

**Away from the sheet, inflow and outflow match only on average.** Take instead the slab $\lvert z\rvert<d$. Energy still enters at $2\eta_0\cos^2\omega t$ per square metre, but it leaves through the two faces at $2\eta_0\cos^2(\omega t - \beta d)$, because the fields at $\pm d$ are delayed by $d/c$. The difference changes the energy stored in the slab:

$$
\frac{dW}{dt} = 2\eta_0\big[\cos^2\omega t - \cos^2(\omega t - \beta d)\big],
$$

per square metre of sheet. With the faces a quarter wavelength from the sheet ($\beta d = \pi/2$) this is $2\eta_0\cos2\omega t$: energy piles up in the slab and drains out again twice per cycle. Over a whole period it averages to zero. That is why the notes compare *time averages*, the subject of §7.

## 7. Time averages

For a quantity that repeats with period $T = 2\pi/\omega$, the time average is

$$
\langle f\rangle\equiv\frac1T\int_{t_0}^{t_0+T}f(t)\,dt,
$$

taken over any whole period. The notes' tool is the identity $\cos^2(\omega t + \phi) = \tfrac12\big[1 + \cos(2\omega t + 2\phi)\big]$: a constant plus a ripple at $2\omega$, and the ripple averages to zero over a period.

> [!key] The averages you need
> $$
> \begin{gathered}
> \langle\cos^2(\omega t + \phi)\rangle = \langle\sin^2(\omega t + \phi)\rangle = \tfrac12,\\[4pt]
> \langle\cos(\omega t + \phi)\rangle = 0,\qquad \langle\sin\omega t\cos\omega t\rangle = 0,
> \end{gathered}
> $$
> for every phase $\phi$. The phase may depend on position, as in $\phi = \mp\beta z$, so the average is $\tfrac12$ at every point.

**The notes' sheet, averaged.** Apply $\langle\cos^2\rangle = \tfrac12$ to the results of §6:

> [!key] Energy balance of the notes' sheet
> For $\mathbf{J}_s = \hat x\,2\cos\omega t$ A/m in vacuum:
> $$
> \begin{gathered}
> \langle\mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{H}\rangle = \pm\hat z\,\frac{\eta_0}{2}\approx\pm\hat z\,188\ \text{W/m}^2,\\[4pt]
> \langle-\mathbf{J}_s\cdot\mathbf{E}\rangle = \eta_0\approx377\ \text{W/m}^2 ,
> \end{gathered}
> $$
> with the upper sign for $z>0$. The power injected per square metre of sheet equals the sum of the average fluxes leaving the two faces: $\eta_0/2 + \eta_0/2 = \eta_0$. The notes use $\eta_0 = 120\pi$, which gives $60\pi$ W/m² per side and $120\pi$ W/m² injected. For a sheet current $\hat x\,J_0\cos\omega t$ in a medium of impedance $\eta$, the same steps give $\langle S\rangle = \eta J_0^2/8$ on each side and $\langle-\mathbf{J}_s\cdot\mathbf{E}\rangle = \eta J_0^2/4$.

**Why the stored energy drops out of the averages.** In a periodic steady state, $w$ at each point returns to the same value after one period, so $\langle\partial w/\partial t\rangle = [w(t_0 + T) - w(t_0)]/T = 0$. Averaging the theorem leaves:

> [!key] Time-averaged Poynting theorem (periodic steady state)
> $$
> \begin{gathered}
> \nabla\cdot\langle\mathbf{S}\rangle = -\langle\mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{E}\rangle,\\[4pt]
> \oint_A\langle\mathbf{S}\rangle\cdot d\mathbf{A} = -\int_V\langle\mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{E}\rangle\,dV .
> \end{gathered}
> $$
> On average, the power flowing out of any closed surface equals the power injected inside it, minus the power dissipated inside it. For the slab $\lvert z\rvert<d$ around the notes' sheet, this gives $\eta_0/2 + \eta_0/2 = \eta_0$ for every $d$.

**A single plane wave.** For $\mathbf{E} = \hat x\,E_0\cos(\omega t - \beta z)$ and $\mathbf{H} = \hat y\,(E_0/\eta)\cos(\omega t - \beta z)$ ([[3-maxwell-and-waves/18-the-wave-equation-and-plane-tem-waves#5-h-from-e-the-intrinsic-impedance|Lecture 18 §5]]):

$$
\langle\mathbf{S}\rangle = \hat z\,\frac{E_0^2}{2\eta} = \hat z\,\tfrac12\eta H_0^2 = \hat z\,\tfrac12E_0H_0 .
$$

The electric and magnetic energy densities are equal at every instant, because $\tfrac12\mu(E/\eta)^2 = \tfrac12\epsilon E^2$ when $\eta^2 = \mu/\epsilon$. So $w = \epsilon E^2$, $\langle w\rangle = \tfrac12\epsilon E_0^2$, and $\langle S\rangle = v\langle w\rangle$: the energy travels at the wave speed, just as $\mathbf{J} = \rho\mathbf{u}$ for moving charge. In the notes' example $\langle w\rangle = \eta_0/(2c) = 6.3\times10^{-7}$ J/m³ on each side.

> [!example] Example — J·E in sea water (the exam's question, new numbers)
> Sea water (conductivity about 4 S/m) carries a low-frequency field $\mathbf{E} = \hat y\,0.5\cos\omega t$ V/m. Then $\mathbf{J} = \sigma\mathbf{E} = \hat y\,2\cos\omega t$ A/m² and $\mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{E} = \sigma E^2 = \cos^2\omega t$ W/m³. The peak is 1 W/m³ and the average is 0.5 W/m³. The unit says what it is: power per unit volume turned into heat, half a milliwatt per litre on average. Summer 2019 HE3 #1b asks exactly this, with other numbers.

> [!example] Example — sunlight and a radio station as fields
> - **Sunlight** above the atmosphere delivers about 1361 W/m² (the solar constant). If it were one cosine wave, $E_0 = \sqrt{2\eta_0\langle S\rangle}\approx1.01$ kV/m and $H_0 = E_0/\eta_0\approx2.69$ A/m (rms values 716 V/m and 1.90 A/m). Its energy density is $\langle S\rangle/c\approx4.5\ \mu$J/m³.
> - **A 50 kW transmitter radiating equally in all directions** (an idealization; real antennas have a radiation pattern). Apply the time-averaged integral form to a sphere of radius $r$ centred on it. The sphere encloses the transmitter and nothing else, so $4\pi r^2\langle S\rangle = 50$ kW for every $r$: $\langle S\rangle$ falls as $1/r^2$. Far from the antenna (many wavelengths away) the wave is locally a plane wave, so $E_0 = \sqrt{2\eta_0\langle S\rangle}$ and the field falls as $1/r$. Close to the antenna the field also has parts that store energy without carrying any away on average, and the plane-wave relation fails. At 10 km, many wavelengths at any broadcast frequency, $\langle S\rangle = 3.98\times10^{-5}$ W/m² and $E_0 = 0.173$ V/m. With $\eta_0 = 120\pi$ the far-field shortcut is $E_0 = \sqrt{60P}/r$. (Rao's textbook makes this $1/r$ argument for the far field; the numbers are this site's.)

> [!example] Example — perpendicular components carry power independently
> The wave $\mathbf{E} = \hat x\,E_1\cos(\omega t - \beta z) + \hat y\,E_2\cos(\omega t - \beta z + \phi)$ travels toward $+z$, with $\mathbf{H} = \hat z\times\mathbf{E}/\eta$. Then $S_z = E_xH_y - E_yH_x = (E_x^2 + E_y^2)/\eta$ at every instant: there is no cross term. So $\langle\mathbf{S}\rangle = \hat z\,(E_1^2 + E_2^2)/(2\eta)$, whatever $\phi$ is. With $E_1 = 3$ V/m and $E_2 = 4$ V/m in vacuum, that is $25/(2\times377) = 33$ mW/m². Exam papers use this to "split, then add": the power of a circularly polarized wave (Lecture 24) is the sum of the powers of its two linear parts.

> [!example] Example — two equal waves in opposite directions: S ≠ 0 but ⟨S⟩ = 0
> Superpose $\hat x\,E_0\cos(\omega t - \beta z)$ and $-\hat x\,E_0\cos(\omega t + \beta z)$, two d'Alembert waves of [[3-maxwell-and-waves/18-the-wave-equation-and-plane-tem-waves|Lecture 18]] of equal size, travelling in opposite directions. Each brings its own $\mathbf{H}$, and the sums are
> $$
> \begin{gathered}
> \mathbf{E} = \hat x\,2E_0\sin\omega t\,\sin\beta z,\\[4pt]
> \mathbf{H} = \hat y\,\frac{2E_0}{\eta}\cos\omega t\,\cos\beta z,\\[4pt]
> \mathbf{S} = \hat z\,\frac{E_0^2}{\eta}\sin2\omega t\,\sin2\beta z .
> \end{gathered}
> $$
> $\mathbf{S}$ changes sign every quarter period, and $\langle\mathbf{S}\rangle = 0$ at every $z$: the two waves carry equal and opposite average power. (It is also $(\lvert\mathbf{E}_1\rvert^2 - \lvert\mathbf{E}_2\rvert^2)/\eta$, as on [[concepts/poynting-vector]].) The energy, however, is not zero. $\langle w\rangle = \epsilon E_0^2$ at every $z$, and it sloshes between electric energy near the maxima of $\mathbf{E}$ and magnetic energy near the maxima of $\mathbf{H}$ every quarter period. At a point where $\mathbf{E}$ is always zero ($\sin\beta z = 0$), $w$ itself drops to zero at the instants when $\cos\omega t = 0$; the energy in each half wavelength stays constant. Lecture 26 treats such standing waves.

> [!trap] Where time averages go wrong
> - **The ½ belongs to the average of a squared cosine.** A DC field has no ½, and the *peak* of $\cos^2$ is 1, not ½.
> - **Average the product, not the factors.** $\langle E\rangle\langle H\rangle = 0\times0$, but $\langle EH\rangle = \tfrac12E_0H_0$ for a single wave.
> - **Per area or per volume?** $\mathbf{J}_s\cdot\mathbf{E}$ is in W/m² (per unit area of sheet); $\mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{E}$ is in W/m³. Exam papers call $\langle\mathbf{J}_s\cdot\mathbf{E}\rangle$ "the average Joule heating rate" even at a source, where it is negative.
> - **A sheet's power splits evenly** (same medium on both sides). Each side carries half of $\langle-\mathbf{J}_s\cdot\mathbf{E}\rangle$, not all of it.
> - **E and H out of phase.** In lossy media (Lectures 22–23) $\mathbf{H}$ lags $\mathbf{E}$, and $\langle S\rangle$ is no longer $\tfrac12E_0H_0$. For two cosines with different phases, $\cos a\cos b = \tfrac12[\cos(a - b) + \cos(a + b)]$ gives $\langle\cos(\omega t + \phi_1)\cos(\omega t + \phi_2)\rangle = \tfrac12\cos(\phi_1 - \phi_2)$. Lecture 21 turns this into the phasor rule for $\langle\mathbf{S}\rangle$.

## 8. Connections

- **Lecture 16.** The continuity equation is the template: density, flux, and (for energy) an exchange term. The boundary-condition pattern gives the sheet form $\hat n\cdot(\mathbf{S}_1 - \mathbf{S}_2) = -\mathbf{J}_s\cdot\mathbf{E}$.
- **Lectures 10 and 15.** $\tfrac12\epsilon E^2$ and $\tfrac12\mu H^2$ were read off a capacitor and a solenoid; the theorem shows they are the energy densities of any field.
- **Lectures 8, 10 and 11.** $\mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{E} = \sigma E^2$ is the point form of $I^2R$; a leaky capacitor turns its stored energy into it on the spot; the wire is heated through its surface by the field that its surface charges set up.
- **Lecture 19.** $\mathbf{E}$ opposing $\mathbf{J}_s$ is now a statement about power: the sheet injects $\tfrac\eta2J_s^2$ per square metre at every instant, half into each wave.
- **Lectures 21–23.** Lecture 21 rewrites the averages with phasors. In a conductor $\mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{E} = \sigma E^2$ takes energy out of a wave as it travels, so the power falls as $e^{-2\alpha z}$ when the field falls as $e^{-\alpha z}$ (Lectures 22–23).
- **Unit 4.** Power on a cable travels in the dielectric (§4); a transmission line is a guide for $\mathbf{S}$.

## 9. Summary

- Poynting's theorem: $\partial w/\partial t + \nabla\cdot\mathbf{S} + \mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{E} = 0$, with $w = \tfrac12\epsilon E^2 + \tfrac12\mu H^2$ [J/m³], $\mathbf{S} = \mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{H}$ [W/m²], $\mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{E}$ [W/m³]. Derivation: $\mathbf{H}\cdot$(Faraday) $-\ \mathbf{E}\cdot$(Ampère–Maxwell) and $\nabla\cdot(\mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{H}) = \mathbf{H}\cdot\nabla\times\mathbf{E} - \mathbf{E}\cdot\nabla\times\mathbf{H}$, in a linear medium with $\epsilon$, $\mu$ constant in time.
- It is the conservation law for electromagnetic energy, the twin of the continuity equation, plus the exchange term $\mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{E}$: Joule heating $\sigma E^2\ge0$ in a conductor, a source where $\mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{E}<0$ (battery, antenna current, radiating sheet).
- Integral form: $-dW/dt = \oint\mathbf{S}\cdot d\mathbf{A} + \int\mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{E}\,dV$. A resistor's $I^2R$ enters through its sides; DC power on a coax flows in the dielectric.
- The notes' sheet $\hat x\,2\cos\omega t$ A/m in vacuum: $\mathbf{E} = -\hat x\,\eta_0\cos(\omega t\mp\beta z)$, $\mathbf{H} = \mp\hat y\cos(\omega t\mp\beta z)$, $\mathbf{S} = \pm\hat z\,\eta_0\cos^2(\omega t\mp\beta z)$, $\mathbf{J}_s\cdot\mathbf{E} = -2\eta_0\cos^2\omega t$. At the sheet, $\hat n\cdot(\mathbf{S}_1 - \mathbf{S}_2) = -\mathbf{J}_s\cdot\mathbf{E}$ at every instant.
- Time averages: $\langle\cos^2\rangle = \tfrac12$, so $\langle\mathbf{S}\rangle = \pm\hat z\,\eta_0/2\approx\pm188$ W/m² per side and $\langle-\mathbf{J}_s\cdot\mathbf{E}\rangle = \eta_0\approx377$ W/m². In general $\eta J_0^2/8$ per side and $\eta J_0^2/4$ injected.
- In a periodic steady state $\langle\partial w/\partial t\rangle = 0$, so $\oint\langle\mathbf{S}\rangle\cdot d\mathbf{A} = -\int\langle\mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{E}\rangle\,dV$. One plane wave: $\langle S\rangle = E_0^2/2\eta = v\langle w\rangle$. Two equal opposite waves: $\langle S\rangle = 0$ while $\langle w\rangle\neq0$.

> [!exam] On exams
> Lecture 20 rarely stands alone: it is usually the closing part of a problem from Lectures 19, 21, 23 or 24. The Exam 3 papers on hand ask the following.
> 1. **Time-averaged power of a stated wave.** $\langle S\rangle = E_0^2/(2\eta)$ along the direction of travel, with $E_0$ read off the given field (Summer 2017/2018 HE3, SP17 #4b, Summer 2020 FE #4c). When the wave is given as a phasor, Lecture 21's rule is the same computation. Answers are usually wanted in exact form with $\eta_0 = 120\pi$.
> 2. **Split, then add.** First $\langle S\rangle$ for one linear component, then for the whole circularly polarized wave. Perpendicular components carry power independently (the example in §7).
> 3. **J·E at a point in a conductor.** The peak $\sigma E_0^2$, the average $\sigma E_0^2/2$, and what the unit W/m³ means (Summer 2019 HE3 #1b and its conflict version; the sea-water example in §7).
> 4. **Energy bookkeeping at a radiating sheet.** $\langle\mathbf{J}_s\cdot\mathbf{E}\rangle$ at the sheet (negative, because the sheet is a source), then the average power per unit area carried into one half-space, which is half of it in magnitude (Summer 2020 HE3 #2c–d, with the hint "related to (c)"). The worked problem [[problems/energy-bookkeeping-for-a-radiating-sheet|Energy bookkeeping for a radiating sheet]] runs these steps with a different plane, current direction, waveform and medium, and adds the stored energy; this site does not reproduce the exam's key.
> 5. **True/false items on standing waves** (SP17 #1c, FA23 #1(c)). Decide them by writing $\mathbf{S}$ and $w$ for two equal waves travelling in opposite directions and averaging, as in the last example of §7. Watch the words: "instantaneous" or "time-averaged", "at a point" or "in the region".
> 6. **Power against depth in a lossy medium** (Summer 2018/2019 HE3 #3b(v), FA23 #6(b)). The power density is proportional to $E^2$, so it falls as $e^{-2\alpha z}$ when the field falls as $e^{-\alpha z}$ (Lectures 22–23).
> 7. **Power through an area.** For a uniform plane wave, $\langle S\rangle$ times the area, for example the cross-section of a parallel-plate line (SP19 #2c, with Unit 4).
