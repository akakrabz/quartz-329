> [!abstract] In one breath
> [[3-maxwell-and-waves/19-dalembert-solutions-and-radiation-from-current-sheets|Lecture 19]] fed a cosine current into a sheet and got waves $\cos(\omega t\mp\beta z)$. This lecture studies that single-frequency, or **monochromatic**, wave for its own sake. It repeats in time with period $T = 2\pi/\omega$ and in space with wavelength $\lambda = 2\pi/\beta$, so $\beta$ is a spatial frequency, the twin of $\omega$. Its crests move at $v_p = \omega/\beta = \lambda f$. The relation between $\omega$ and $\beta$, the **dispersion relation**, is where the physics of each kind of wave lives: a straight line for light in a dielectric and for sound in a gas, a hyperbola for radio waves in a plasma, whose crests outrun light. In a linear system driven at one frequency everything oscillates at that frequency, so each quantity is fully described by an amplitude and a phase, which one complex number holds: the **phasor**, with $a(t) = \text{Re}\{\tilde Ae^{j\omega t}\}$. Picture $\tilde Ae^{j\omega t}$ as an arrow turning in the complex plane; $a(t)$ is its shadow on the real axis. A sine is a cosine a quarter period late, so its phasor picks up $-j$; differentiating turns the arrow a quarter turn forward, so $d/dt\to j\omega$. A plane wave toward $+z$ becomes $\tilde{\mathbf{E}} = E_0e^{-j\beta z}\hat x$ (note the minus sign), its partner $\tilde{\mathbf{H}}$ is $\tilde{\mathbf{E}}$ divided by $\eta$ and rotated, and the time-average power density is $\langle\mathbf{S}\rangle = \tfrac12\text{Re}\{\tilde{\mathbf{E}}\times\tilde{\mathbf{H}}^*\}$. These are the same numbers [[3-maxwell-and-waves/20-poynting-theorem-and-monochromatic-waves|Lecture 20]] got by averaging $\cos^2$, now without an integral.

## 1. Monochromatic waves: one frequency, two periodicities

[[3-maxwell-and-waves/18-the-wave-equation-and-plane-tem-waves|Lecture 18]] explained why a plane wave can carry any waveform $f(t\mp z/v)$: a sum of time-shifted, rescaled cosines solves the wave equation, and by Fourier analysis such sums build any signal. The notes now study the building block itself. Put $f(t) = A\cos(\omega t)$:

$$
\begin{gathered}
A\cos\Big[\omega\Big(t\mp\frac zv\Big)\Big] = A\cos(\omega t\mp\beta z),\\[4pt]
\beta\equiv\frac{\omega}{v} = \omega\sqrt{\mu\epsilon}\ \ [\text{rad/m}].
\end{gathered}
$$

A wave with one frequency is **monochromatic** ("one colour", the optics word). Exam papers also say *co-sinusoidal*, and the notes' p. 8 margin says *time-harmonic*. The notes call $\beta$ the **wave-number**, by analogy with the **wave-frequency** $\omega$. Lecture 19's slides and the textbook (Rao) call it the *phase constant*, and physics books write $k$. All three name the same thing: radians of phase per metre.

**Two periodicities.** Hold $z$ fixed and $A\cos(\omega t\mp\beta z)$ repeats each time $\omega t$ grows by $2\pi$. Hold $t$ fixed and it repeats each time $\beta z$ grows by $2\pi$. The notes draw $\cos\omega t$ against $t$ and $\cos\beta z$ against $z$ one above the other in the margin to make the point: the two plots have the same shape, and $\beta$ plays in space exactly the role $\omega$ plays in time. The notes flag this in bold as a point to understand well, because it carries over to signal processing of images. A striped pattern, or any picture, has a spectrum in *spatial* frequency, measured in radians (or cycles) per metre, just as a sound has one in temporal frequency.

> [!key] Period, wavelength and phase velocity
> $$
> \begin{gathered}
> \text{fixed } z:\quad T = \frac{2\pi}{\omega} = \frac1f\quad\text{(period, s)},\\[4pt]
> \text{fixed } t:\quad \lambda = \frac{2\pi}{\beta}\quad\text{(wavelength, m)},\\[4pt]
> v_p = \frac{\omega}{\beta} = \frac{\lambda}{T} = \lambda f\quad\text{(phase velocity, m/s)}.
> \end{gathered}
> $$
> A point of constant phase, $\omega t - \beta z = \phi_0$, sits at $z = (\omega t - \phi_0)/\beta$, so it moves at $dz/dt = \omega/\beta$. In one period the pattern advances by one wavelength, which is $v_p = \lambda/T$. For a TEM wave in a perfect dielectric $v_p = 1/\sqrt{\mu\epsilon}$, the $v$ of Lecture 18, at every frequency. The notes write $v$; this site writes $v_p$ when it means the speed of the phase, as Lecture 19's slides did, because §2 meets media in which other speeds differ from it.

> [!example] Example — everything from one expression (this site's numbers)
> In a lossless, non-magnetic medium, $\mathbf{E} = 12\cos\big(4\pi\times10^8\,t - \tfrac{8\pi}{3}\,y\big)\,\hat z$ V/m, with $t$ in seconds and $y$ in metres.
> - $\omega = 4\pi\times10^8$ rad/s, so $f = \omega/2\pi = 200$ MHz and $T = 1/f = 5$ ns.
> - $\beta = 8\pi/3\approx8.38$ rad/m, so $\lambda = 2\pi/\beta = 0.75$ m.
> - The $t$ term and the $y$ term have opposite signs, so the wave travels toward $+y$ ([[3-maxwell-and-waves/18-the-wave-equation-and-plane-tem-waves#6-reading-a-wave|Lecture 18 §6]]), at $v_p = \omega/\beta = 1.5\times10^8$ m/s $= c/2$. Check: $\lambda f = 0.75\times2\times10^8$ m/s, the same.
> - $c/v_p = \sqrt{\mu_r\epsilon_r} = 2$. With $\mu_r = 1$ that gives $\epsilon_r = 4$ (taking $c = 3\times10^8$ m/s), and $\eta = \eta_0/\sqrt{\epsilon_r} = 60\pi\approx188\ \Omega$.
> - Two probes 25 cm apart along $y$: the downstream one lags by $\beta\,\Delta y = 2\pi/3$ rad (120°). It sees the same cosine $\Delta y/v_p = 1.67$ ns $= T/3$ later.
>
> A speed read off an expression gives the product $\mu_r\epsilon_r$, not $\epsilon_r$ by itself. The word "non-magnetic" is what lets you split it.

**The spectrum (notes p. 4).** The notes reproduce a chart of the electromagnetic spectrum. It lists typical wavelengths for each band and compares them with the sizes of familiar things, from buildings (radio) to atomic nuclei (gamma rays). It marks which bands get through the atmosphere: radio and visible light do, which is why radio and optical telescopes work from the ground. ## 2. Dispersion relations and phase velocity

The notes widen the view. A monochromatic solution of *any* one-dimensional wave equation, whether in acoustics, optics, plasma physics or seismology, has the same form $A\cos(\omega t\mp\beta z)$. What changes from one kind of wave to another is how $\beta$ depends on $\omega$. That relation is the wave's **dispersion relation**. It has to be derived from the physical laws governing that wave, and once it is known the phase velocity always follows from it as $v_p = \omega/\beta = \lambda f$. The notes give three examples.

> [!key] Three dispersion relations (notes)
> | wave | dispersion relation | phase velocity $\omega/\beta$ |
> |---|---|---|
> | TEM wave in a perfect dielectric | $\beta = \omega\sqrt{\mu\epsilon}$ | $1/\sqrt{\mu\epsilon}$ at every $\omega$ |
> | sound in a monatomic gas | $\beta = \omega\sqrt{\dfrac{m}{\frac53k_BT}}$ | $\sqrt{5k_BT/3m}$ at every $\omega$ |
> | TEM wave in a collisionless plasma | $\beta = \dfrac1c\sqrt{\omega^2 - \omega_p^2}$ | $\dfrac{c}{\sqrt{1 - \omega_p^2/\omega^2}}$, faster than $c$ |
>
> In row 2, $T$ is the gas temperature in kelvin, $m$ the mass of one atom and $k_B = 1.381\times10^{-23}$ J/K Boltzmann's constant. In row 3, $\omega_p = \sqrt{Ne^2/(m\epsilon_0)}$ is the **plasma frequency**, with $N$ free electrons per m³ of charge $-e$ and mass $m$. A relation in which $v_p$ does not depend on $\omega$ (rows 1 and 2) is **non-dispersive**. One in which it does (row 3) is **dispersive**.

Notation: the notes write Boltzmann's constant as $K$ in the same line that gives the unit kelvin as "(K)", and use $T$ for the temperature here and for the period a page later; $m$ is an atomic mass in row 2 and the electron mass in row 3. On this page $T$ means the period everywhere except inside the row-2 formula.

**1. Light in a perfect dielectric.** This is Lecture 18's result in new clothes. Because $v_p = 1/\sqrt{\mu\epsilon}$ is the same for every frequency, all the Fourier components of a pulse travel together and the pulse keeps its shape. That is the "every frequency travels at the same $v$" of [[3-maxwell-and-waves/18-the-wave-equation-and-plane-tem-waves#4-the-1d-wave-equation-and-its-solutions|Lecture 18 §4]].

**2. Sound in a monatomic gas.** The wave is a pressure and density oscillation. The $\tfrac53$ is the ratio of specific heats $\gamma$ of a monatomic ideal gas: the compressions are too quick for heat to flow, so they are adiabatic. Handbook check (this site's numbers): helium at 300 K gives $v_p = 1019$ m/s and argon 323 m/s; at 20 °C the formula gives 1007 and 319 m/s, the tabulated values. Air is mostly diatomic, with $\gamma = \tfrac75$ instead of $\tfrac53$, and the same formula with $\tfrac75$ gives the familiar 343 m/s at 20 °C. Using $\tfrac53$ for air would give 375 m/s, which is wrong. Sound in an ideal gas is non-dispersive too. One consequence: a 440 Hz tone has $\lambda = v_p/f = 2.32$ m in helium and 0.73 m in argon at 300 K. Same frequency, different wavelength, because $v_p\propto1/\sqrt m$ and the ratio is $\sqrt{39.95/4.003} = 3.16$.

**3. Radio waves in a plasma.** A plasma is an ionized gas, such as the ionosphere, about 50 to 1000 km up. Its free electrons are pushed back and forth by the wave's $\mathbf{E}$, and their current changes how the wave propagates. Numerically $f_p = \omega_p/2\pi\approx8.98\sqrt{N}$ Hz with $N$ in m⁻³. The daytime ionosphere, with $N\sim10^{12}$ m⁻³, has $f_p\approx9$ MHz. Three regimes:

- **$\omega>\omega_p$: a travelling wave, with crests faster than light.** At 10 MHz and $f_p = 8.98$ MHz, $\beta = 0.0922$ rad/m, so $\lambda = 68.1$ m (30.0 m in vacuum) and $v_p = \lambda f = 6.81\times10^8$ m/s $= 2.27c$.
- **$\omega\gg\omega_p$: almost vacuum.** At 100 MHz, $v_p = 1.004c$. FM radio and satellite links pass through the ionosphere almost unaffected.
- **$\omega<\omega_p$: no travelling wave at all.** $\beta^2 = (\omega^2 - \omega_p^2)/c^2$ is negative, so no real $\beta$ exists, and the field cannot propagate into the plasma; a wave sent straight up is turned back. The same effect, for waves arriving at a slant, lets shortwave radio hop around the Earth off the ionosphere, and it is why satellite links use frequencies well above $f_p$.

Is $v_p>c$ a problem for relativity? No. A pure cosine has no beginning and no end, so it carries no message, and its crests are a pattern, not a thing. A signal is a group of nearby frequencies, and its envelope moves at the **group velocity** $v_g = d\omega/d\beta$. For the plasma $v_g = c\sqrt{1 - \omega_p^2/\omega^2}<c$ ($0.440c$ at 10 MHz), and $v_pv_g = c^2$. The notes define $v_g$ in a footnote of Lecture 24 and leave the details to ECE 450. No exam paper on hand asks about it. In a non-dispersive medium (a perfect dielectric with constant $\epsilon$, or the ideal gas), $v_g = v_p$, so the distinction never arises there.

> [!derivation]- Where the plasma relation comes from (this site's derivation, using Lecture 11)
> Take [[1-electrostatics/11-lorentz-drude-models-for-conductivity-and-susceptibility|Lecture 11]]'s Drude equation for an electron and drop the collisions (a *collisionless* plasma, $\nu\to0$): $m\,dv_x/dt = -eE_x$. With $N$ electrons per m³ the current density is $J_x = -Nev_x$, so
> $$
> \frac{\partial J_x}{\partial t} = \frac{Ne^2}{m}E_x .
> $$
> Keep $\mathbf{J}$ in Lecture 18's curl–curl step: $\nabla\times\nabla\times\mathbf{E} = -\mu_0\,\partial_t\big(\mathbf{J} + \epsilon_0\,\partial_t\mathbf{E}\big)$. A transverse plane wave has $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{E} = 0$, so the left side is $-\nabla^2\mathbf{E}$ and
> $$
> \frac{\partial^2E_x}{\partial z^2} = \mu_0\frac{Ne^2}{m}E_x + \mu_0\epsilon_0\frac{\partial^2E_x}{\partial t^2}.
> $$
> Try $E_x = E_0\cos(\omega t - \beta z)$. Each second derivative brings down $-\beta^2$ or $-\omega^2$:
> $$
> \begin{gathered}
> -\beta^2 = \mu_0\frac{Ne^2}{m} - \frac{\omega^2}{c^2}\ \Longrightarrow\ \beta^2 = \frac{\omega^2 - \omega_p^2}{c^2},\\[4pt]
> \omega_p^2 = \frac{Ne^2}{m\epsilon_0}.
> \end{gathered}
> $$
> In Lecture 11's language this is the AC conductivity $\sigma(\omega) = Ne^2/\big(m(\nu + j\omega)\big)$ with $\nu = 0$. It is purely imaginary, $-jNe^2/(m\omega)$, so the electron current lags $\mathbf{E}$ by a quarter period. §7 shows that such a current absorbs no power on average, which is why the collisionless plasma is lossless even though current flows in it. With collisions, $\sigma$ gets a real part and the wave decays: that is the conducting media of Lectures 22–23.

> [!trap] Speeds and symbols in §2
> - $v = \omega/\beta$ is the **phase** velocity. The notes call it "the propagation velocity for all types of wave motions", which is safe only for non-dispersive waves. In the plasma it exceeds $c$, while energy and signals travel at $v_g<c$.
> - Below $\omega_p$ the plasma does not carry "a slower wave". It carries no travelling wave.
> - The $\tfrac53$ belongs to monatomic gases (He, Ne, Ar). Air needs $\tfrac75$.
> - $T$ in the acoustic formula is a temperature, not the period.

## 3. Phasors: a rotating arrow and its shadow

**Why bother.** In a linear medium (constant $\mu$, $\epsilon$, $\sigma$), driven at one frequency, every field, current and charge ends up oscillating at that same frequency once transients have died away. So at each point a quantity is completely described by two numbers: its amplitude and its phase. A complex number holds exactly two numbers. That is the whole idea of a **phasor**, which ECE 210 uses for voltages and currents and this lecture uses for fields.

> [!key] The phasor of a sinusoid
> $$
> \begin{gathered}
> a(t) = A\cos(\omega t + \theta) = \text{Re}\{\tilde A\,e^{j\omega t}\},\\[4pt]
> \tilde A = A\,e^{j\theta}.
> \end{gathered}
> $$
> $A\ge0$ is the amplitude and $\theta$ the phase, so $\lvert\tilde A\rvert = A$ and $\angle\tilde A = \theta$. Why it works: by Euler's identity $e^{j\phi} = \cos\phi + j\sin\phi$, $\tilde Ae^{j\omega t} = Ae^{j(\omega t + \theta)} = A\cos(\omega t + \theta) + jA\sin(\omega t + \theta)$, and its real part is $a(t)$. The phasor contains no $t$: the frequency is agreed once for the whole problem and then left out. This course uses the cosine as the reference and the **peak** amplitude (not the rms value) as $\lvert\tilde A\rvert$.

**The picture.** As $t$ runs, $\tilde Ae^{j\omega t} = Ae^{j(\omega t + \theta)}$ is a point on a circle of radius $A$ in the complex plane, at angle $\omega t + \theta$. It is an arrow that turns counterclockwise once per period. Its shadow on the real axis is $a(t)$. The phasor $\tilde A$ is the snapshot of that arrow at $t = 0$, and that one snapshot fixes the whole motion, because the turning rate $\omega$ is the same for every quantity in the problem.

> [!key] The complex-number moves this lecture needs
> - Polar and rectangular forms: $x + jy = re^{j\phi}$ with $r = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}$ and $\phi$ the angle of the point $(x, y)$, *in its own quadrant*: $-4 + 3j$ is at $143.1^\circ$, not at $\tan^{-1}(3/(-4)) = -36.9^\circ$.
> - Multiplying by $e^{j\phi}$ turns an arrow by $\phi$ without changing its length. So $j = e^{j\pi/2}$ turns by $+90^\circ$, $-j = 1/j = e^{-j\pi/2}$ by $-90^\circ$, and $-1 = e^{j\pi}$ by half a turn.
> - Conjugate: $(re^{j\phi})^* = re^{-j\phi}$. Then $zz^* = \lvert z\rvert^2$ and $\text{Re}\{z\} = \tfrac12(z + z^*)$.
> - Products multiply lengths and add angles: $(r_1e^{j\phi_1})(r_2e^{j\phi_2}) = r_1r_2e^{j(\phi_1 + \phi_2)}$.

**Sines.** A sine is a cosine that peaks a quarter period later: $\sin(\omega t + \theta) = \cos(\omega t + \theta - \pi/2)$. Its phasor is therefore $Ae^{j(\theta - \pi/2)} = -jAe^{j\theta}$. In the picture, $-j$ turns the arrow back by 90°, which is a lag of a quarter period. Check with $\theta = 0$: $\text{Re}\{-je^{j\omega t}\} = \text{Re}\{-j\cos\omega t + \sin\omega t\} = \sin\omega t$.

> [!key] Time function → phasor
> | time function | phasor | why |
> |---|---|---|
> | $A\cos\omega t$ | $A$ | $\theta = 0$ |
> | $A\sin\omega t$ | $-jA$ | a quarter period late: $e^{-j\pi/2} = -j$ |
> | $-A\cos\omega t$ | $-A$ | half a period: $e^{j\pi} = -1$ |
> | $-A\sin\omega t$ | $+jA$ | $(-1)(-j) = j$ |
> | $A\cos(\omega t + \theta)$ | $Ae^{j\theta}$ | the definition |
> | $A\sin(\omega t + \theta)$ | $-jAe^{j\theta} = Ae^{j(\theta - \pi/2)}$ | sine rule |
> | $A\cos(\omega t - \beta z)$ | $Ae^{-j\beta z}$ | the phase at position $z$ is $-\beta z$ |
> | $A\sin(\omega t + \beta y)$ | $-jAe^{j\beta y}$ | sine rule, phase $+\beta y$ |
>
> Rewrite every term as a cosine with a plus sign in front before reading off the phase, or use the rows above.

**Back to the time domain.** Multiply by $e^{j\omega t}$ and take the real part. The quickest route is the polar form: write $\tilde A = \lvert\tilde A\rvert e^{j\angle\tilde A}$, and then $a(t) = \lvert\tilde A\rvert\cos(\omega t + \angle\tilde A)$. For example, $\tilde{\mathbf{E}} = (4 - 3j)\,e^{-j\beta z}\hat x$ V/m has $4 - 3j = 5e^{-j36.9^\circ}$, so $\mathbf{E} = 5\cos(\omega t - \beta z - 36.9^\circ)\,\hat x$ V/m. That equals $4\cos(\omega t - \beta z) + 3\sin(\omega t - \beta z)$, because the $-3j$ part turns back into $+3\sin$. A common slip is to take $\text{Re}\{\tilde A\}$ first and multiply by $\cos\omega t$ afterwards; that keeps the 4 and loses the 3.

**The rules that make phasors useful.** Each is one line from the definition.

- **Linearity.** The phasor of $a + b$ is $\tilde A + \tilde B$; the phasor of $ka$, with $k$ real, is $k\tilde A$. Adding arrows is adding sinusoids.
- **Uniqueness.** If $\text{Re}\{\tilde Ae^{j\omega t}\} = \text{Re}\{\tilde Be^{j\omega t}\}$ for all $t$, then $\tilde A = \tilde B$. Compare $t = 0$ (the real parts agree) and $\omega t = \pi/2$ (the imaginary parts agree). So an equation between sinusoids can be replaced by the same equation between their phasors.
- **Integral: $\int dt\to1/(j\omega)$**, the reverse of the derivative rule below.
- **Delay: $a(t - \tau)\to\tilde Ae^{-j\omega\tau}$.** A delay is a lag in phase of $\omega\tau$. This is the bridge from Lecture 18 to plane-wave phasors (§4).

**Derivative: $d/dt\to j\omega$.** Taking the real part and differentiating in $t$ can be done in either order (the derivative acts on the real and imaginary parts separately), so

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{d}{dt}\text{Re}\{\tilde Ae^{j\omega t}\} &= \text{Re}\Big\{\tilde A\,\frac{d}{dt}e^{j\omega t}\Big\}\\
&= \text{Re}\big\{(j\omega\tilde A)\,e^{j\omega t}\big\}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The phasor of $da/dt$ is $j\omega\tilde A$: stretch the arrow by $\omega$ and turn it a quarter turn *forward*. A cosine rises fastest a quarter period before its peak, so its derivative peaks a quarter period early. Check: $\tfrac{d}{dt}\cos\omega t = -\omega\sin\omega t$, and $\text{Re}\{j\omega e^{j\omega t}\} = -\omega\sin\omega t$.

> [!key] Calculus becomes algebra
> $$
> \begin{gathered}
> \frac{da}{dt}\ \leftrightarrow\ j\omega\tilde A,\qquad \int a\,dt\ \leftrightarrow\ \frac{\tilde A}{j\omega},\\[4pt]
> a(t-\tau)\ \leftrightarrow\ \tilde Ae^{-j\omega\tau}.
> \end{gathered}
> $$
> A linear differential equation in $t$ with constant coefficients becomes an algebraic equation for the phasors.

You have used this before. In ECE 210, $i = C\,dv/dt$ becomes $\tilde I = j\omega C\tilde V$, the impedance $1/(j\omega C)$. In [[1-electrostatics/11-lorentz-drude-models-for-conductivity-and-susceptibility#3-ac-conductivity-when-the-carriers-cannot-keep-up|Lecture 11 §3]], the Drude equation $m\,d\mathbf{v}/dt = q\mathbf{E} - m\nu\mathbf{v}$ became $j\omega m\tilde{\mathbf{v}} = q\tilde{\mathbf{E}} - m\nu\tilde{\mathbf{v}}$, which gave the AC conductivity $\sigma(\omega) = Nq^2/\big(m(\nu + j\omega)\big)$: the first phasor of this course. [[3-maxwell-and-waves/22-phasor-maxwell-equations-and-damped-waves|Lecture 22]] applies $\partial/\partial t\to j\omega$ to every term of Maxwell's equations.

**What phasors cannot do: products.** The product of two signals at frequency $\omega$ contains a constant and a part at $2\omega$ (think $\cos^2\omega t = \tfrac12 + \tfrac12\cos2\omega t$), so it has no phasor at $\omega$, and $\text{Re}\{\tilde A\tilde Be^{j\omega t}\}$ is *not* $a(t)b(t)$. Power is a product, so it needs a rule of its own (§7).

**Vector phasors.** A field is a vector of three sinusoids, each with its own amplitude and phase. Its phasor is a vector with complex components, $\tilde{\mathbf{E}} = \tilde E_x\hat x + \tilde E_y\hat y + \tilde E_z\hat z$, and
$$
\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{r}, t) = \text{Re}\{\tilde{\mathbf{E}}(\mathbf{r})\,e^{j\omega t}\}.
$$
The unit vectors are real; the complex numbers sit in the components. The notes' first example is the $x$-polarized wave
$$
\begin{gathered}
\mathbf{E} = E_0\cos(\omega t\mp\beta z)\,\hat x\ \ [\text{V/m}]\\[2pt]
\updownarrow\\[2pt]
\tilde{\mathbf{E}} = E_0e^{\mp j\beta z}\,\hat x\ \ [\text{V/m}],
\end{gathered}
$$
and Euler's identity confirms $\text{Re}\{\tilde{\mathbf{E}}e^{j\omega t}\} = E_0\cos(\omega t\mp\beta z)\,\hat x$. The phasor still depends on position: $e^{\mp j\beta z}$ records how the phase changes from place to place.

> [!trap] Mixing the two domains
> A phasor never contains $t$, and a time-domain field is never complex. "$\mathbf{E}(z, t) = 5e^{-j\beta z}\hat x$" mixes the two. Either write the phasor $\tilde{\mathbf{E}}(z) = 5e^{-j\beta z}\hat x$, or the field $\mathbf{E}(z, t) = 5\cos(\omega t - \beta z)\,\hat x$, but not a mixture of the two.

## 4. Plane-wave phasors and the direction-of-travel trap

The delay rule turns Lecture 18 into phasors in one line. A wave toward $+z$ satisfies $\mathbf{E}(z, t) = \mathbf{E}(0, t - z/v_p)$: the point at $z$ sees what the origin saw $z/v_p$ earlier. A delay of $z/v_p$ multiplies the phasor by $e^{-j\omega z/v_p} = e^{-j\beta z}$. For a wave toward $-z$ the shift is $t + z/v_p$ and the factor is $e^{+j\beta z}$.

> [!key] Plane-wave phasors
> $$
> \begin{gathered}
> \text{toward }{+z}:\ \ \tilde{\mathbf{E}}(z) = \tilde{\mathbf{E}}_0\,e^{-j\beta z},\\[4pt]
> \text{toward }{-z}:\ \ \tilde{\mathbf{E}}(z) = \tilde{\mathbf{E}}_0\,e^{+j\beta z},\\[4pt]
> \text{toward }\hat u:\ \ \tilde{\mathbf{E}}(\mathbf{r}) = \tilde{\mathbf{E}}_0\,e^{-j\beta\,\hat u\cdot\mathbf{r}},\quad \tilde{\mathbf{E}}_0\perp\hat u .
> \end{gathered}
> $$
> $\lvert\tilde{\mathbf{E}}\rvert$ is the same everywhere (a uniform plane wave). The phase *falls* by $\beta$ radians per metre in the direction of travel: downstream points repeat what upstream points did, later. $\tilde{\mathbf{E}}_0$ may be complex, and its components may have different phases (Lecture 24 builds circular polarization that way).

**The trap.** In the time domain, Lecture 18's rule reads the direction from the relative sign of the $t$ and $z$ terms: opposite signs mean $+z$. A phasor has no $t$ term, so the rule cannot be applied directly, and $e^{+j\beta z}$ looks "positive". Restore the time factor: $e^{+j\beta z}e^{j\omega t} = e^{j(\omega t + \beta z)}$, same signs, travel toward $-z$. The short version: **a minus sign in the exponent means travel toward plus.** Or think of the phase: the wave travels toward where the phase lags.

**Any direction, and the exam template.** With the wave vector $\beta\hat u = (\beta_x, \beta_y, \beta_z)$, the factor is $e^{-j(\beta_xx + \beta_yy + \beta_zz)}$ (Rao §8.1 writes the same in the time domain, $\cos(\omega t - \beta_xx - \beta_zz)$). Some exam papers write the phasor as $(A\hat x + B\hat y + C\hat z)\,e^{j(ax + by + cz)}$, with a plus sign in the template. Matching the two,
$$
(a, b, c) = -\beta\,\hat u,\qquad a^2 + b^2 + c^2 = \beta^2:
$$
the signs of $a$, $b$, $c$ are opposite to the direction of travel. Two examples with this site's numbers:
- Toward $-\hat z$ at 450 MHz in a non-magnetic dielectric with $\epsilon_r = 4$: $\beta = 2\pi f\sqrt{\epsilon_r}/c = 6\pi$ rad/m, the factor is $e^{+j6\pi z}$, and $(a, b, c) = (0, 0, +6\pi)$ rad/m.
- Toward $(\hat x + \hat y)/\sqrt2$ with $\beta = 2\pi$ rad/m: the factor is $e^{-j\sqrt2\pi(x + y)}$, so $a = b = -\sqrt2\pi\approx-4.44$ rad/m and $c = 0$. The amplitude vector must be perpendicular to $\hat u$: $\hat z$, or $\hat x - \hat y$, or a complex combination of the two.

> [!trap] Reading the direction from a phasor
> - $e^{-j\beta z}$ travels toward $+z$, and $e^{+j\beta z}$ toward $-z$. When unsure, multiply by $e^{j\omega t}$ and use Lecture 18's sign rule.
> - In a template $e^{j(ax + by + cz)}$, $(a, b, c)$ points *against* the travel.
> - A factor $-j$, $e^{j\pi/3}$ or $(4 - 3j)$ in front is a phase, not a direction. Only the position-dependent exponent says which way the wave goes.

## 5. The partner field: divide by η and rotate

For a single plane wave in a lossless medium the rule of Lectures 18–19, $\mathbf{H} = \tfrac1\eta\hat u\times\mathbf{E}$, carries straight over to phasors. $\eta$ is real and $\hat u$ is a real unit vector, so taking the real part and taking $\hat u\times(\cdot)/\eta$ can be done in either order.

> [!key] The Ẽ ↔ H̃ rule (one wave, lossless medium)
> $$
> \begin{gathered}
> \tilde{\mathbf{H}} = \frac1\eta\,\hat u\times\tilde{\mathbf{E}},\qquad \tilde{\mathbf{E}} = \eta\,\tilde{\mathbf{H}}\times\hat u,\\[4pt]
> \tilde{\mathbf{S}}\equiv\tilde{\mathbf{E}}\times\tilde{\mathbf{H}}^* = \frac{\lvert\tilde{\mathbf{E}}\rvert^2}{\eta}\,\hat u .
> \end{gathered}
> $$
> In the notes' words: to get $\tilde{\mathbf{H}}$, divide $\tilde{\mathbf{E}}$ by $\eta$ and rotate the vector so that $\tilde{\mathbf{S}} = \tilde{\mathbf{E}}\times\tilde{\mathbf{H}}^*$ points in the direction of travel. To get $\tilde{\mathbf{E}}$, multiply $\tilde{\mathbf{H}}$ by $\eta$ and rotate the same way. Any complex factor (a $-j$, a phase) rides along unchanged, because $\eta$ is real.

**Why the conjugate.** $\tilde{\mathbf{E}}$ and $\tilde{\mathbf{H}}$ of one wave carry the same factor $e^{-j\beta\hat u\cdot\mathbf{r}}$. In $\tilde{\mathbf{E}}\times\tilde{\mathbf{H}}^*$ that factor meets its conjugate and cancels, so $\tilde{\mathbf{S}}$ is the same real vector everywhere. With the identity $\mathbf{a}\times(\mathbf{b}\times\mathbf{c}) = \mathbf{b}(\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{c}) - \mathbf{c}(\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b})$ and $\tilde{\mathbf{E}}\cdot\hat u = 0$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\tilde{\mathbf{E}}\times\tilde{\mathbf{H}}^* &= \frac1\eta\,\tilde{\mathbf{E}}\times\big(\hat u\times\tilde{\mathbf{E}}^*\big)\\
&= \frac1\eta\Big[\hat u\,\big(\tilde{\mathbf{E}}\cdot\tilde{\mathbf{E}}^*\big) - \tilde{\mathbf{E}}^*\big(\tilde{\mathbf{E}}\cdot\hat u\big)\Big] = \frac{\lvert\tilde{\mathbf{E}}\rvert^2}{\eta}\,\hat u .
\end{aligned}
$$
Without the conjugate, $\tilde{\mathbf{E}}\times\tilde{\mathbf{H}} = (\tilde{\mathbf{E}}\cdot\tilde{\mathbf{E}}/\eta)\,\hat u$ would carry $e^{-2j\beta z}$: it still lies along $\hat u$, but its coefficient keeps turning along $z$, so its real part changes sign every quarter wavelength. The notes promise more on $\tilde{\mathbf{S}}$; it is the **complex Poynting vector** of §7.

> [!derivation]- Why "divide by η" works, from Faraday's law in phasor form
> [[3-maxwell-and-waves/18-the-wave-equation-and-plane-tem-waves#3-the-planar-problem-only-exzt-and-hyzt|Lecture 18 §3]] reduced Maxwell's curl equations, for $\mathbf{E} = \hat xE_x(z, t)$ and $\mathbf{H} = \hat yH_y(z, t)$, to $\partial E_x/\partial z = -\mu\,\partial H_y/\partial t$ and $-\partial H_y/\partial z = \epsilon\,\partial E_x/\partial t$. With phasors, $\partial/\partial t\to j\omega$:
> $$
> \frac{d\tilde E_x}{dz} = -j\omega\mu\,\tilde H_y,\qquad -\frac{d\tilde H_y}{dz} = j\omega\epsilon\,\tilde E_x .
> $$
> For $\tilde E_x = E_0e^{\mp j\beta z}$ the $z$-derivative is $\mp j\beta\tilde E_x$. The first equation then gives
> $$
> \begin{gathered}
> \tilde H_y = \pm\frac{\beta}{\omega\mu}\,\tilde E_x = \pm\frac{\tilde E_x}{\eta},\\[4pt]
> \text{since}\quad \frac{\beta}{\omega\mu} = \frac{\omega\sqrt{\mu\epsilon}}{\omega\mu} = \sqrt{\frac\epsilon\mu} = \frac1\eta .
> \end{gathered}
> $$
> The second equation is then satisfied too, because $\beta/\eta = \omega\epsilon$. The $\pm$ is the travel direction, exactly as in Lecture 18's $H_y = \pm f/\eta$. [[3-maxwell-and-waves/22-phasor-maxwell-equations-and-damped-waves|Lecture 22]] does this for the full set of Maxwell's equations and then lets $\sigma\neq0$, which makes $\eta$ complex: then $\tilde{\mathbf{H}}$ also picks up the phase $-\angle\eta$ relative to $\tilde{\mathbf{E}}$, and $\mathbf{E}$ and $\mathbf{H}$ are no longer in step.

**The notes' Example 1** is a table to study. Here it is with the check for each row (every row is right):

| field | phasor | the check |
|---|---|---|
| $\mathbf{E} = \cos(\omega t + \beta y)\,\hat z$ | $\tilde{\mathbf{E}} = e^{j\beta y}\,\hat z$ | $+j\beta y$: travels toward $-y$, so $\hat u = -\hat y$ |
| (its partner) | $\tilde{\mathbf{H}} = -\dfrac{e^{j\beta y}}{\eta}\,\hat x$ | $\tfrac1\eta(-\hat y)\times\hat z = -\tfrac1\eta\hat x$; then $\hat z\times(-\hat x) = -\hat y$ ✓ |
| $\mathbf{H} = \sin(\omega t - \beta z)\,\hat y$ | $\tilde{\mathbf{H}} = -je^{-j\beta z}\,\hat y$ | sine → $-j$; travels toward $+z$ |
| $\mathbf{E} = \eta\sin(\omega t - \beta z)\,\hat x$ | $\tilde{\mathbf{E}} = -j\eta e^{-j\beta z}\,\hat x$ | $\eta\tilde{\mathbf{H}}\times\hat z$ with $\hat y\times\hat z = \hat x$; the $-j$ rides along |

In the last row the time-domain field sits in the left column, which the notes' comment calls "the right column".

> [!example] Example 2 (notes) — two waves at once, one rule each
> $\mathbf{H} = \hat xH^+\cos(\omega t - \beta z) + \hat yH^-\sin(\omega t + \beta z)$, with real amplitudes $H^\pm$: a wave toward $+z$ plus a wave toward $-z$. Convert term by term, with the sine picking up $-j$:
> $$
> \tilde{\mathbf{H}} = \hat xH^+e^{-j\beta z} - j\,\hat yH^-e^{j\beta z}.
> $$
> Now apply $\tilde{\mathbf{E}} = \eta\tilde{\mathbf{H}}\times\hat u$ to each wave **with its own** $\hat u$:
> - toward $+z$: $\eta H^+e^{-j\beta z}\,(\hat x\times\hat z) = -\hat y\,\eta H^+e^{-j\beta z}$;
> - toward $-z$: $\eta(-jH^-e^{j\beta z})\,\big(\hat y\times(-\hat z)\big) = \eta(-jH^-e^{j\beta z})(-\hat x) = +j\,\hat x\,\eta H^-e^{j\beta z}$.
>
> Adding the two waves,
> $$
> \tilde{\mathbf{E}} = -\hat y\,\eta H^+e^{-j\beta z} + j\,\hat x\,\eta H^-e^{j\beta z},
> $$
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> \mathbf{E} = &-\hat y\,\eta H^+\cos(\omega t - \beta z)\\
> &- \hat x\,\eta H^-\sin(\omega t + \beta z),
> \end{aligned}
> $$
> using $\text{Re}\{je^{j(\omega t + \beta z)}\} = -\sin(\omega t + \beta z)$. The notes invite you to check every sign and report an error. There is none: the pair satisfies both of Lecture 18's curl equations (checked numerically on this site). Using one $\hat u$ for the whole sum would flip one of the two terms.

> [!recipe] From a field to its partner, in phasors
> 1. Convert to a phasor term by term: sines pick up $-j$, extra phases become $e^{j\theta}$.
> 2. Group the terms into waves by their exponents. For each wave read $\hat u$: $e^{-j\beta z}$ means $+z$, $e^{+j\beta z}$ means $-z$, and in general $e^{-j\beta\hat u\cdot\mathbf{r}}$.
> 3. For each wave: $\tilde{\mathbf{H}} = \tfrac1\eta\hat u\times\tilde{\mathbf{E}}$, or $\tilde{\mathbf{E}} = \eta\tilde{\mathbf{H}}\times\hat u$, with the medium's $\eta$.
> 4. Check each wave: $\tilde{\mathbf{E}}\times\tilde{\mathbf{H}}^*$ is along $+\hat u$, and $\lvert\tilde{\mathbf{E}}\rvert/\lvert\tilde{\mathbf{H}}\rvert = \eta$.
> 5. If the time domain is wanted, multiply by $e^{j\omega t}$ and take the real part.

> [!example] Example — the 200 MHz wave of §1
> $\tilde{\mathbf{E}} = 12e^{-j(8\pi/3)y}\,\hat z$ V/m in $\epsilon_r = 4$, travelling toward $+\hat y$. Then $\tilde{\mathbf{H}} = \tfrac{1}{60\pi}\,\hat y\times\hat z\;12e^{-j(8\pi/3)y} = \tfrac{1}{5\pi}e^{-j(8\pi/3)y}\,\hat x\approx63.7\,e^{-j(8\pi/3)y}\,\hat x$ mA/m. Check: $\hat z\times\hat x = +\hat y$ ✓. In the time domain, $\mathbf{H} = 63.7\cos\big(4\pi\times10^8t - \tfrac{8\pi}{3}y\big)\,\hat x$ mA/m.

## 6. The notes' Example 3: a monochromatic current sheet in phasor form

The current sheet of Lecture 19 radiates $\mathbf{E} = -\tfrac\eta2\mathbf{J}_s(t - d/v)$ and $\mathbf{H} = \tfrac12\mathbf{J}_s(t - d/v)\times\hat n$, where $d$ is the distance from the sheet and $\hat n$ points from the sheet toward the field point. For a sinusoidal current the delay $d/v$ becomes the factor $e^{-j\beta d}$:

> [!key] A sinusoidal current sheet, in phasors
> For $\mathbf{J}_s(t) = \text{Re}\{\tilde{\mathbf{J}}_se^{j\omega t}\}$ in a lossless medium,
> $$
> \tilde{\mathbf{E}} = -\frac\eta2\,\tilde{\mathbf{J}}_s\,e^{-j\beta d},\qquad \tilde{\mathbf{H}} = \frac12\,\tilde{\mathbf{J}}_s\times\hat n\;e^{-j\beta d}.
> $$
> For a sheet on $z = 0$, $d = \lvert z\rvert$ and $\hat n = \pm\hat z$ for $z\gtrless0$. Each wave satisfies $\tilde{\mathbf{S}}\parallel+\hat n$: away from the sheet.

**The notes' example.** On $z = 0$ in vacuum, $\mathbf{J}_s = \hat x\,2\cos(\omega t)$ A/m $= \text{Re}\{\hat x\,2e^{j\omega t}\}$, so $\tilde{\mathbf{J}}_s = 2\hat x$ A/m. Lecture 19's fields, $E_x = -\tfrac\eta2f(t\mp z/v)$ and $H_y = \mp\tfrac12f(t\mp z/v)$ with $f(t) = 2\cos\omega t$, are $E_x = -\eta\cos(\omega t\mp\beta z)$ and $H_y = \mp\cos(\omega t\mp\beta z)$, with $\beta = \omega/c$ and $\eta = \eta_0\approx120\pi\ \Omega$. As phasors,
$$
\begin{gathered}
\tilde{\mathbf{E}}^\pm = -\eta\,e^{\mp j\beta z}\,\hat x\ \ \text{V/m},\\[4pt]
\tilde{\mathbf{H}}^\pm = \mp e^{\mp j\beta z}\,\hat y\ \ \text{A/m}\qquad(z\gtrless0).
\end{gathered}
$$
The box gives the same: $\tfrac12(2\hat x)\times(\pm\hat z) = \pm\hat x\times\hat z = \mp\hat y$. Check the direction: $\tilde{\mathbf{E}}^+\times\tilde{\mathbf{H}}^{+*} = (-\eta\hat x)\times(-\hat y) = +\eta\hat z$ above the sheet and $(-\eta\hat x)\times(+\hat y) = -\eta\hat z$ below it ✓.

**A sine instead of a cosine.** Let the same sheet carry $\mathbf{J}_s = \hat x\,2\sin(\omega t)$ A/m (this site's variation). Then $\tilde{\mathbf{J}}_s = -2j\hat x$: the source is delayed by a quarter period. Every phasor is the old one times $-j$, $\tilde{\mathbf{E}}^\pm = j\eta e^{\mp j\beta z}\hat x$ and $\tilde{\mathbf{H}}^\pm = \pm je^{\mp j\beta z}\hat y$, which in the time domain is $E_x = -\eta\sin(\omega t\mp\beta z)$ and $H_y = \mp\sin(\omega t\mp\beta z)$. As §7 shows, the average power is unchanged, because a common phase cancels against its own conjugate.

## 7. Time averages straight from phasors

[[3-maxwell-and-waves/20-poynting-theorem-and-monochromatic-waves|Lecture 20]] found the time-average Poynting vector of the sheet's waves by writing out $\mathbf{S} = \mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{H} = \pm\eta\cos^2(\omega t\mp\beta z)\,\hat z$ and averaging $\cos^2$ to $\tfrac12$. That works, but it needs the time functions and a trigonometric identity for every new problem. Phasors give the average directly, once the product problem of §3 is handled.

> [!derivation] The product rule ⟨a b⟩ = ½ Re{Ã B̃*}
> Let $a(t) = \text{Re}\{\tilde Ae^{j\omega t}\}$ and $b(t) = \text{Re}\{\tilde Be^{j\omega t}\}$. Write each real part as half the sum of a number and its conjugate, $\text{Re}\{z\} = \tfrac12(z + z^*)$, and multiply out:
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> a\,b &= \tfrac14\big(\tilde Ae^{j\omega t} + \tilde A^*e^{-j\omega t}\big)\big(\tilde Be^{j\omega t} + \tilde B^*e^{-j\omega t}\big)\\[4pt]
> &= \frac{\tilde A\tilde B^* + \tilde A^*\tilde B}{4} + \frac{\tilde A\tilde Be^{j2\omega t} + \tilde A^*\tilde B^*e^{-j2\omega t}}{4}\\[4pt]
> &= \tfrac12\text{Re}\{\tilde A\tilde B^*\} + \tfrac12\text{Re}\{\tilde A\tilde B\,e^{j2\omega t}\}.
> \end{aligned}
> $$
> The second term is a sinusoid at $2\omega$; over one period it averages to zero. The first term does not depend on $t$ at all. So
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> \langle a\,b\rangle &= \frac1T\int_0^Ta(t)\,b(t)\,dt\\
> &= \tfrac12\text{Re}\{\tilde A\tilde B^*\} = \tfrac12AB\cos(\theta_a - \theta_b).
> \end{aligned}
> $$
> Here $\tilde A = Ae^{j\theta_a}$ and $\tilde B = Be^{j\theta_b}$. This is the margin proof in the notes, which does it for a circuit element's power $\langle v(t)i(t)\rangle = \tfrac12\text{Re}\{VI^*\}$ (ECE 210). Only the *phase difference* matters: a common phase factor $e^{j\phi}$ on both phasors cancels in $\tilde A\tilde B^*$.

Special cases worth knowing on sight:

- $\langle a^2\rangle = \tfrac12\lvert\tilde A\rvert^2$. With $\tilde A = 1$ this is Lecture 20's $\langle\cos^2\rangle = \tfrac12$.
- In phase ($\theta_a = \theta_b$): $\tfrac12AB$. In quadrature (90° apart): zero. In antiphase: $-\tfrac12AB$.
- Example: $a = 3\cos(\omega t + 20^\circ)$ and $b = 2\cos(\omega t - 40^\circ)$ give $\langle ab\rangle = \tfrac12\cdot3\cdot2\cos60^\circ = 1.5$.

The vector versions follow component by component. Each component of $\mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{H}$ is a sum of products such as $E_yH_z - E_zH_y$, and the rule applies to each product. The dot product works the same way.

> [!key] Time averages of power densities
> $$
> \begin{gathered}
> \langle\mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{H}\rangle = \tfrac12\text{Re}\{\tilde{\mathbf{E}}\times\tilde{\mathbf{H}}^*\}\ \ [\text{W/m}^2],\\[4pt]
> \langle\mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{E}\rangle = \tfrac12\text{Re}\{\tilde{\mathbf{J}}\cdot\tilde{\mathbf{E}}^*\}\ \ [\text{W/m}^3],\\[6pt]
> \tilde{\mathbf{S}}\equiv\tilde{\mathbf{E}}\times\tilde{\mathbf{H}}^*\ \ (\text{complex Poynting vector}),\\[4pt]
> \langle\mathbf{S}\rangle = \tfrac12\text{Re}\{\tilde{\mathbf{S}}\}.
> \end{gathered}
> $$
> For a sheet, $\langle\mathbf{J}_s\cdot\mathbf{E}\rangle = \tfrac12\text{Re}\{\tilde{\mathbf{J}}_s\cdot\tilde{\mathbf{E}}^*\}$ in W/m². For **one plane wave in a lossless medium**, §5 gives
> $$
> \langle\mathbf{S}\rangle = \frac{\lvert\tilde{\mathbf{E}}_0\rvert^2}{2\eta}\,\hat u = \frac{\eta\lvert\tilde{\mathbf{H}}_0\rvert^2}{2}\,\hat u,
> $$
> where $\lvert\tilde{\mathbf{E}}_0\rvert^2 = \lvert\tilde E_{0x}\rvert^2 + \lvert\tilde E_{0y}\rvert^2 + \lvert\tilde E_{0z}\rvert^2$ adds the powers of perpendicular components, whatever their phases.

The $\tfrac12$ is there because phasor magnitudes are peak values. (Power engineers use rms phasors, $\lvert\tilde A\rvert/\sqrt2$, and drop the $\tfrac12$; this course does not.) The 200 MHz wave of §1, with 12 V/m in $\eta = 60\pi\ \Omega$, carries $\langle\mathbf{S}\rangle = 144/(120\pi)\,\hat y = 0.382\,\hat y$ W/m².

> [!example] Same numbers, two routes: the notes' sheet (Lecture 20 by cos², Lecture 21 by phasors)
> With $\tilde{\mathbf{J}}_s = 2\hat x$ A/m and $\tilde{\mathbf{E}}^\pm = -\eta e^{\mp j\beta z}\hat x$, $\tilde{\mathbf{H}}^\pm = \mp e^{\mp j\beta z}\hat y$ from §6:
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> \langle\mathbf{S}^\pm\rangle &= \tfrac12\text{Re}\big\{(-\eta e^{\mp j\beta z}\hat x)\times(\mp e^{\pm j\beta z}\hat y)\big\}\\
> &= \pm\frac\eta2\,\hat z = \pm60\pi\,\hat z\ \ \text{W/m}^2,
> \end{aligned}
> $$
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> \langle-\mathbf{J}_s\cdot\mathbf{E}(0, t)\rangle &= \tfrac12\text{Re}\{-\tilde{\mathbf{J}}_s\cdot\tilde{\mathbf{E}}^*(0)\}\\
> &= \tfrac12\text{Re}\{-(2\hat x)\cdot(-\eta\hat x)\}\\
> &= \eta\approx120\pi\ \ \text{W/m}^2 .
> \end{aligned}
> $$
> These are Lecture 20's numbers, $\pm60\pi\,\hat z$ ($\approx188$ W/m²) on the two sides and 377 W/m² delivered by the current, obtained there from $\pm\eta\cos^2(\omega t\mp\beta z)$ and $2\eta\cos^2\omega t$. The energy balance closes: the current gives up $\eta$ per unit area and each side carries away $\eta/2$. The $e^{\mp j\beta z}$ factors cancel in the conjugate product, so the answer is the same at every $z$, as it must be in a lossless medium. With the sine current of §6 every phasor gains a $-j$, and $(-j)(-j)^* = 1$: the same averages.

> [!example] Example 2's power: counter-propagating waves subtract (this site's extension)
> With the notes' Example 2 phasors, $\tilde{\mathbf{E}} = -\hat y\eta H^+e^{-j\beta z} + j\hat x\eta H^-e^{j\beta z}$ and $\tilde{\mathbf{H}}^* = \hat xH^+e^{j\beta z} + j\hat yH^-e^{-j\beta z}$. Of the four cross products, two vanish ($\hat y\times\hat y$ and $\hat x\times\hat x$) and two survive:
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> \tilde{\mathbf{E}}\times\tilde{\mathbf{H}}^* &= -\eta(H^+)^2\,(\hat y\times\hat x)\\
> &\quad + j\cdot j\,\eta(H^-)^2\,(\hat x\times\hat y)\\
> &= \eta\big[(H^+)^2 - (H^-)^2\big]\hat z,
> \end{aligned}
> $$
> so $\langle\mathbf{S}\rangle = \tfrac\eta2\big[(H^+)^2 - (H^-)^2\big]\hat z$. Each wave carries its own power, and the net flow is the difference. (Here the cross terms vanish because the two waves are perpendicularly polarized. For parallel polarizations they do appear in $\tilde{\mathbf{S}}$, but they are purely imaginary, so they still drop out of $\langle\mathbf{S}\rangle$. The instantaneous version of this cancellation is on the [[concepts/poynting-vector|Poynting vector page]].)

**Joule heating, and Lecture 11 once more.** For a current driven by the field, $\tilde{\mathbf{J}} = \sigma\tilde{\mathbf{E}}$, the rule gives $\langle\mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{E}\rangle = \tfrac12\text{Re}\{\sigma\}\,\lvert\tilde{\mathbf{E}}\rvert^2$. With a real $\sigma$ this is $\tfrac12\sigma E_0^2$, the time average of the Joule heating $\sigma E^2$ of [[1-electrostatics/10-capacitance-and-conductance|Lecture 10]]. Only the part of the current *in phase* with $\mathbf{E}$ absorbs power. For the collisionless plasma of §2, $\sigma = -jNe^2/(m\omega)$ is purely imaginary, the current is 90° out of phase with $\mathbf{E}$, and $\langle\mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{E}\rangle = 0$: the electrons take energy from the wave for a quarter period and hand it back during the next one (the bottom panel of the figure). That is why the plasma's dispersion relation describes a lossless wave. With collisions, Lecture 11's $\sigma = \sigma_{\text{DC}}/(1 + j\omega/\nu)$ has a real part $\sigma_{\text{DC}}/(1 + \omega^2/\nu^2)$, and the medium absorbs energy. Damped waves of that kind are [[3-maxwell-and-waves/22-phasor-maxwell-equations-and-damped-waves|Lecture 22]] and [[3-maxwell-and-waves/23-imperfect-dielectrics-and-good-conductors|Lecture 23]].

> [!trap] Power from phasors
> - Forgetting the $\tfrac12$. Phasor magnitudes are peaks, and the average of a squared cosine is half its peak.
> - Forgetting the conjugate. $\tfrac12\text{Re}\{\tilde{\mathbf{E}}\times\tilde{\mathbf{H}}\}$ is not an average; for a single linearly polarized wave it varies along $z$ as $\cos2\beta z$.
> - Multiplying phasors to get "the phasor of the power". A product has no phasor at $\omega$; only its average comes out of $\tilde A\tilde B^*$.
> - Expecting the instantaneous $\mathbf{S}$ to equal $\langle\mathbf{S}\rangle$. For one linearly polarized wave it swings between 0 and twice the average, at $2\omega$. If its perpendicular components are out of phase it never reaches 0 (the [[problems/phasors-e-and-power-from-a-given-h|worked problem]], part (f)).
> - Dropping a perpendicular component. Powers of the $\hat x$ and $\hat y$ parts of one wave add; the phase between them does not matter for $\langle\mathbf{S}\rangle$.

## 8. Connections

- **Lectures 18–19.** $\beta = \omega\sqrt{\mu\epsilon}$, $\eta$, $\mathbf{H} = \hat u\times\mathbf{E}/\eta$ and the sheet's $-\tfrac\eta2\mathbf{J}_s$, $\tfrac12\mathbf{J}_s\times\hat n$ are unchanged. What is new: the delay $d/v$ becomes the phase factor $e^{-j\beta d}$, and every time function becomes one complex vector.
- **Lecture 20.** Time averages by $\langle\cos^2\rangle = \tfrac12$ are the special case $\tilde A = \tilde B$ of $\tfrac12\text{Re}\{\tilde A\tilde B^*\}$. The two routes give the same numbers (§7).
- **Lectures 10–11.** Joule heating $\mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{E}$ averages to $\tfrac12\text{Re}\{\sigma\}\lvert\tilde{\mathbf{E}}\rvert^2$. The Drude AC conductivity was already a phasor result, and its collisionless limit gives the plasma dispersion relation of §2.
- **ECE 210.** Impedance, $d/dt\to j\omega$ and $\langle p\rangle = \tfrac12\text{Re}\{VI^*\}$ are the circuit versions of this page.
- **Lecture 22** applies $\partial/\partial t\to j\omega$ to Maxwell's equations, gets the Helmholtz equation and, with $\sigma\neq0$, a complex propagation constant $\gamma = \alpha + j\beta$ and a complex $\eta$. **Lecture 24** superposes perpendicular phasor components with a $\pm j$ between them (circular polarization) and defines the group velocity. **Unit 4** runs transmission lines in sinusoidal steady state with voltage and current phasors $\tilde V(z)$, $\tilde I(z)$.

## 9. Summary

- Monochromatic wave: $A\cos(\omega t\mp\beta z)$ with $\beta = \omega/v_p$, the wave-number (phase constant). Period $T = 2\pi/\omega = 1/f$, wavelength $\lambda = 2\pi/\beta$, phase velocity $v_p = \omega/\beta = \lambda f$. $\beta$ is a spatial frequency.
- Dispersion relation $\beta(\omega)$, from the physics of each wave: $\omega\sqrt{\mu\epsilon}$ for light in a dielectric and $\omega\sqrt{m/(\tfrac53k_BT)}$ for sound in a monatomic gas (both non-dispersive); $\tfrac1c\sqrt{\omega^2 - \omega_p^2}$ in a collisionless plasma (dispersive, $v_p>c$, no wave below $\omega_p$, $f_p\approx9\sqrt N$ Hz). Signals travel at $v_g = d\omega/d\beta$ (Lecture 24).
- Phasor: $a(t) = A\cos(\omega t + \theta) = \text{Re}\{\tilde Ae^{j\omega t}\}$ with $\tilde A = Ae^{j\theta}$, an arrow turning at $\omega$ whose shadow is $a(t)$. Sine → $-j$; $d/dt\to j\omega$; $\int dt\to1/j\omega$; delay $\tau\to e^{-j\omega\tau}$. Back: $\lvert\tilde A\rvert\cos(\omega t + \angle\tilde A)$.
- Plane wave: $\tilde{\mathbf{E}} = \tilde{\mathbf{E}}_0e^{-j\beta\hat u\cdot\mathbf{r}}$. A minus sign in the exponent means travel toward plus; in $e^{j(ax + by + cz)}$, $(a, b, c) = -\beta\hat u$.
- $\tilde{\mathbf{H}} = \hat u\times\tilde{\mathbf{E}}/\eta$, $\tilde{\mathbf{E}} = \eta\tilde{\mathbf{H}}\times\hat u$, one wave at a time; $\tilde{\mathbf{E}}\times\tilde{\mathbf{H}}^* = \hat u\lvert\tilde{\mathbf{E}}\rvert^2/\eta$. Sheet: $\tilde{\mathbf{E}} = -\tfrac\eta2\tilde{\mathbf{J}}_se^{-j\beta d}$, $\tilde{\mathbf{H}} = \tfrac12\tilde{\mathbf{J}}_s\times\hat n\,e^{-j\beta d}$.
- $\langle ab\rangle = \tfrac12\text{Re}\{\tilde A\tilde B^*\}$; $\langle\mathbf{S}\rangle = \tfrac12\text{Re}\{\tilde{\mathbf{E}}\times\tilde{\mathbf{H}}^*\}$; $\langle\mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{E}\rangle = \tfrac12\text{Re}\{\tilde{\mathbf{J}}\cdot\tilde{\mathbf{E}}^*\}$; one lossless wave carries $\lvert\tilde{\mathbf{E}}_0\rvert^2/2\eta$. The notes' sheet: $\pm60\pi\,\hat z$ W/m² each side, $120\pi$ W/m² from the current, as in Lecture 20.

Method: §5's recipe, with the direction read from the exponent first.
> 3. **Reading numbers off an expression**: $\omega$, $f$, $T$, $\beta$, $\lambda$, $v_p$ (SP17 Exam 3 #3c, Summer 2020 HE3 #2a and #2f, Summer 2020 FE #3a), and in the Summer papers the template $(A\hat x + B\hat y + C\hat z)e^{j(ax + by + cz)}$ built from a direction, a frequency and snapshots of $\mathbf{E}$ (Summer 2017 HE3 #2a, Summer 2018 HE3 #3a, Summer 2019 HE3 #3a). Method: §1's example and §4's $(a, b, c) = -\beta\hat u$.
> 4. **$\langle\mathbf{S}\rangle = \tfrac12\text{Re}\{\tilde{\mathbf{E}}\times\tilde{\mathbf{H}}^*\}$** as the closing part: SP17 Exam 3 #4b, SP19 Exam 3 #2c (times the cross-section of a parallel-plate line), Summer 2020 FE #4c, FA23 Exam 3 #6(a) (a damped wave, Lectures 22–23). Kudeki's papers often ask first for the power of one component and then for the whole wave; the component powers add.
>
> The traps the papers set: the direction of travel from $(\omega t + \beta x)$ or $e^{+j\beta x}$; the $-j$ of a sine; the plus sign of the $e^{j(ax + by + cz)}$ template; orienting $\tilde{\mathbf{H}}$ so that $\tilde{\mathbf{E}}\times\tilde{\mathbf{H}}^*$ points along the travel; the $\tfrac12$. The no-calculator papers use $\eta_0 = 120\pi\ \Omega$ and $c = 3\times10^8$ m/s so that answers come out exact.
