*Lecture 15 · course notes §15 · slides "Potentials" and "Inductance" · prev: [[2-magnetostatics/14-faradays-law-and-induced-emf|Lecture 14]] · next: [[3-maxwell-and-waves/16-charge-conservation-displacement-current-and-maxwells-equations|Lecture 16]]*

> [!abstract] In one breath
> [[1-electrostatics/10-capacitance-and-conductance|Lecture 10]] packaged electrostatics into a circuit element: charge the conductors, $Q = CV$. This lecture does the same for magnetostatics plus Faraday: run a current, and the loop links its *own* flux, $\Psi = LI$. Change the current and Faraday's law produces a self-emf $-L\,dI/dt$ — a voltage rise in the direction of $I$ that circuit theory writes as the drop $V = L\,dI/dt$. Shorted through a resistor the current decays with $\tau = L/R$, the twin of $RC$. Computing $L$ is the capacitance chain with the letters changed: Ampère's law gives $\mathbf{H}$, $\mu$ gives $\mathbf{B}$, a surface integral gives $\Psi$. The energy $\tfrac12LI^2$ lives in the field at $\tfrac12\mu H^2$ per cubic metre, and for a coax or a pair of plates the inductance and capacitance per metre multiply to $\mu\epsilon$ — the seed of every transmission line in Unit 4. Finally, with time variation the scalar potential alone cannot describe $\mathbf{E}$; the pair $(\Phi,\mathbf{A})$ can, up to a gauge transformation.

## 1. Self-inductance

A coil carrying $I$ makes a field $\mathbf{B}\propto I$ (Biot–Savart is linear in the current), so the flux of that field through the coil's own surface is proportional to $I$:

> [!key] Inductance
> $$
> \Psi = LI,\qquad L = \frac{\Psi}{I}\ \ge0\quad[\text{H} = \text{Wb/A}].
> $$
> $L$ depends on the geometry and the permeability only — the twin of $C = Q/V$. For a coil of $N$ turns each linking the same single-turn flux $\Psi$, the $N$ emfs add, so $\mathcal{E} = -N\,d\Psi/dt\equiv-L\,dI/dt$ gives $L = N\Psi/I$ (what other books call the flux *linkage* $N\Psi$ over $I$). *Mutual* inductance, $M = \Psi_{\text{coil 1 due to }I_2}/I_2$, also in henries, is the transformer's parameter; the course names it and moves on (ECE 330 takes it up).

Now let $I$ change. Faraday's law applied to the coil's own loop gives a **self-emf**

$$
\mathcal{E} = -\frac{d\Psi}{dt} = -L\frac{dI}{dt},
$$

a voltage *rise* around the coil in the direction of the current (also called the **back-emf**, to distinguish it from emfs due to externally produced flux). Circuit courses state the same fact from the other side:

> [!key] The inductor as a circuit element
> $$
> V = L\frac{dI}{dt}
> $$
> is the voltage **drop** across the inductor in the direction of $I$ (passive sign convention), and $-L\,dI/dt$ is the equal and opposite *rise* that Faraday supplies. For a resistive coil with no other source, KVL around the loop reads $RI = -L\,dI/dt$: the rise matches the drop. (A typed slide once wrote this with the wrong sign — see the [[0-toolkit/04-errata-in-the-course-materials|errata page]]; the circuit symbol with $+$ and $-$ on it is right.)

$RI = -L\,dI/dt$ is a first-order ODE with solution $I(t) = I(0)e^{-t/\tau}$, $\tau = L/R$. The self-emf obeys the same equation, $R\mathcal{E} = -L\,d\mathcal{E}/dt$.

> [!example] Example 1 (course notes) — a coil discharging into itself
> An $N$-turn coil with $R = 1\ \Omega$ and $L = 1\ \mu$H carries 3 A at $t = 0$ and is then left alone (shorted). $RI = -L\,dI/dt \Rightarrow I(t) = 3e^{-10^6t}$ A: the current dies in microseconds, and $N$ never enters once $L$ is given.

> [!intuition] Capacitor ↔ inductor
> $\tau = RC$ for the leaky capacitor ([[1-electrostatics/10-capacitance-and-conductance#6-conductance-the-leaky-capacitor|Lecture 10 §6]]) ↔ $\tau = L/R$ here. A large $C$ holds its voltage while it slowly discharges — a slowly varying **voltage source**; a large $L$ holds its *current* while it slowly releases its energy — a slowly varying **current source**, maintaining whatever voltage rise $-L\,dI/dt$ the rest of the circuit requires. (Interrupt the current of a big inductor abruptly and $dI/dt\to\infty$: the spark across the switch is the inductor insisting.)

In general the current in a resistive loop is found by solving a *differential equation* built from the loop's total emf. [[2-magnetostatics/14-faradays-law-and-induced-emf|Lecture 14]] used the algebraic $I = \mathcal{E}/R$ throughout; that is the special case in which the self-emf $-L\,dI/dt$ of the induced current is negligible next to the externally produced emf — true for the small loops and modest rates of change in those examples (for the 60 Hz generator, $L\,dI/dt\sim$ millivolts against 118 V).

> [!recipe] Inductance, five steps
> Assume a current $I$ → find $\mathbf{H}$ by Ampère's law (or Biot–Savart) → $\mathbf{B} = \mu\mathbf{H}$ → $\Psi = \int_S\mathbf{B}\cdot d\mathbf{S}$ through the surface bounded by the current path (times $N$ for $N$ turns) → $L = N\Psi/I$. The $I$ you assumed cancels at the end, as $Q$ did in the capacitance chain.

**Long solenoid.** $n$ turns per metre, cross-section $A$, length $\ell$ ($\ell\gg$ radius so that edge effects are minor), $N = n\ell$ turns. From [[2-magnetostatics/13-current-sheets-solenoids-and-the-vector-potential|Lecture 13]], $\mathbf{B} = \mu_0nI\hat z$ inside, so each turn links $\Psi = \mu_0nIA$ and

> [!key] Inductance of a long solenoid
> $$
> L = \frac{N\Psi}{I} = \frac{n\ell\cdot\mu_0nIA}{I} = n^2\mu_0A\ell = N^2\mu_0\frac{A}{\ell}.
> $$
> Quadratic in the winding density: doubling the turns doubles the field *and* doubles the number of turns that link it. (A slide writes the formula with $N$ and $n$ swapped; the physics is in which symbol is "per metre".)

## 4. Energy: ½LI², and ½μH² per cubic metre

An inductor in a circuit with a quasi-static current (physical size $\ll\lambda = c/f$, the same proviso as for capacitors) has the terminal voltage $V = L\,dI/dt$ and absorbs power

$$
P = VI = LI\frac{dI}{dt} = \frac{d}{dt}\Big(\tfrac12LI^2\Big),
$$

> [!key] Stored magnetic energy and its density
> $$
> W = \tfrac12LI^2 = \tfrac12(n^2\mu_0A\ell)I^2 = \frac{(\mu_0nI)^2}{2\mu_0}A\ell = \frac{B_z^2}{2\mu_0}A\ell = \tfrac12\mu_0H_z^2\cdot A\ell
> $$
> for the solenoid — the energy is the volume occupied by the field times
> $$
> w = \tfrac12\mu_0\,\mathbf{H}\cdot\mathbf{H} = \tfrac12\mathbf{B}\cdot\mathbf{H}\quad[\text{J/m}^3],
> $$
> taken, like $\tfrac12\epsilon E^2$, as the **energy density of the magnetic field** in general. In a material medium $\mu_0\to\mu = (1+\chi_m)\mu_0$ in $L$, $W$ and $w$, in analogy with $\epsilon = (1+\chi_e)\epsilon_0$; Lecture 17 explains $\chi_m$. The total $\tfrac12\epsilon E^2 + \tfrac12\mu H^2$ is what Poynting's theorem (Lecture 20) will keep track of.

Since $W = \int w\,dV\ge0$ with $w\ge0$, $L = 2W/I^2\ge0$ — the "non-negative" in the definition is not an assumption.

**Shorted coax.** Inner conductor of radius $a$, outer of inner radius $b$, length $\ell\gg b$, shorted at the far end so that $I$ flows down the inner conductor and back along the inside surface of the outer one. Ampère on a circle of radius $a<r<b$: $B_\phi = \dfrac{\mu I}{2\pi r}$; outside $r>b$ the two currents cancel and $\mathbf{B} = 0$ — the flux is confined to the cable. The flux linked by the current path threads the rectangle $a<r<b$, $0<z<\ell$ (its normal is $\hat\phi$, along $\mathbf{B}$):

$$
\Psi = \int_S\mathbf{B}\cdot d\mathbf{S} = \ell\int_a^b\frac{\mu I}{2\pi r}dr = \frac{\mu\ell I}{2\pi}\ln\frac ba,
$$

> [!key] Coax and parallel-plate inductance per unit length
> $$
> \begin{gathered}
> L = \frac{\mu\ell}{2\pi}\ln\frac ba,\qquad \mathcal{L}\equiv\frac L\ell = \frac{\mu}{2\pi}\ln\frac ba\ [\text{H/m}]\\[4pt]
> \text{vs.}\qquad \mathcal{C} = \frac{2\pi\epsilon}{\ln(b/a)}\ [\text{F/m}]\ (\text{Lecture 10}).
> \end{gathered}
> $$
> For shorted parallel plates of width $W$ and separation $d$: the surface current $I/W$ on each plate makes $H = I/W$ between them (the current-sheet rule, twice), so $\Psi = \mu\dfrac IW d\ell$ and $\mathcal{L} = \mu\dfrac dW$, beside $\mathcal{C} = \epsilon\dfrac Wd$. In both geometries the geometric factor of $\mathcal{L}$ is the **arithmetic inverse** of the geometric factor of $\mathcal{C}$. (The notes' sentence "$\mathcal{L}$ and $\mathcal{C}$ are proportional to $\epsilon_0$ and $\mu_0$ respectively" has the constants swapped — errata page.)

This is the external inductance: the flux inside the inner conductor itself is ignored (surface current, or $r>a$ only), which is the right idealization at the frequencies where cables matter.

> [!key] The line parameters, and what they multiply to
> | | parallel plates | coax |
> |---|---|---|
> | $\mathcal{C}$ | $\epsilon W/d$ | $2\pi\epsilon/\ln(b/a)$ |
> | $\mathcal{G}$ | $\sigma W/d$ | $2\pi\sigma/\ln(b/a)$ |
> | $\mathcal{L}$ | $\mu d/W$ | $(\mu/2\pi)\ln(b/a)$ |
>
> $$
> \mathcal{L}\mathcal{C} = \mu\epsilon,\qquad \frac{\mathcal{G}}{\mathcal{C}} = \frac\sigma\epsilon,\qquad \frac{1}{\sqrt{\mathcal{L}\mathcal{C}}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu\epsilon}}\ (= c\ \text{in vacuum}).
> $$
> Both relations hold for *any* two-conductor line filled with one homogeneous medium, because all three parameters share one geometric factor. The slides write $c = 1/\sqrt{\mu_0\epsilon_0}$ next to $v_p = 1/\sqrt{\mathcal{LC}}$: that is the speed of a signal on the line, and Unit 4 begins there.

The slide challenge is a good test of the table: increase the plate separation $d$ and $\mathcal{L}$ rises, $\mathcal{C}$ falls, $\mathcal{G}$ falls (answer (c)) — the field has more room, so the same current links more flux and the same charge produces a bigger voltage drop. [[problems/coax-inductance-and-the-lc-product]] runs the whole table for a coax with numbers.

## 6. The potentials when fields vary in time

In statics $\mathbf{E} = -\nabla V$ because $\nabla\times\mathbf{E} = 0$. Faraday's law breaks that: $\mathbf{E}$ now has a curl, so no scalar potential alone can produce it. But $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B} = 0$ still holds, so $\mathbf{B} = \nabla\times\mathbf{A}$ still works, and substituting it into Faraday's law gives

$$
\nabla\times\mathbf{E} = -\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\nabla\times\mathbf{A}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\nabla\times\Big(\mathbf{E}+\frac{\partial\mathbf{A}}{\partial t}\Big) = 0 .
$$

The combination in parentheses *is* curl-free, so it is the gradient of a scalar:

> [!key] Fields from potentials, in general
> $$
> \mathbf{B} = \nabla\times\mathbf{A},\qquad \mathbf{E} = -\nabla\Phi - \frac{\partial\mathbf{A}}{\partial t}.
> $$
> (The slides write $\Phi$ for the scalar potential the notes call $V$.) Six field components are replaced by four potential components, and the two homogeneous Maxwell equations ($\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B} = 0$, Faraday) are satisfied identically. The $-\partial\mathbf{A}/\partial t$ term is the induced, circulating part of $\mathbf{E}$ — the part a voltmeter path integral cannot ignore. In the static limit it vanishes, $\mathbf{E} = -\nabla\Phi$, and Gauss's law gives back Poisson's equation.

**Gauge freedom.** The potentials are not unique. For any scalar $\lambda(\mathbf{r},t)$,

$$
\mathbf{A}' = \mathbf{A} + \nabla\lambda,\qquad \Phi' = \Phi - \frac{\partial\lambda}{\partial t}
$$

give the same $\mathbf{B}$ (because $\nabla\times\nabla\lambda = 0$) and the same $\mathbf{E}$ (the $-\nabla\partial_t\lambda$ from $\Phi'$ cancels the $-\partial_t\nabla\lambda$ from $\mathbf{A}'$). The slide's challenge question asks which transformation leaves the fields unchanged; this pair, answer (b), is it. The Coulomb gauge $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{A} = 0$ of [[2-magnetostatics/13-current-sheets-solenoids-and-the-vector-potential#5-the-vector-potential|Lecture 13]] is one way of spending the freedom; a wave-friendly alternative (the Lorenz gauge) appears when radiation is discussed.

## 7. Summary

- $\Psi = LI$, $L = N\Psi/I$ [H]; self-emf $-L\,dI/dt$ (rise), circuit drop $V = L\,dI/dt$; $M$ for two coils.
- Shorted coil: $RI = -L\,dI/dt$, $I = I(0)e^{-t/\tau}$, $\tau = L/R$; large $L$ ≈ current source; $I = \mathcal{E}/R$ needs $L\,dI/dt\ll\mathcal{E}$.
- Chain: $I\to\mathbf{H}\to\mathbf{B}\to\Psi\to L$. Solenoid $L = n^2\mu_0A\ell = N^2\mu_0A/\ell$.
- $W = \tfrac12LI^2$; $w = \tfrac12\mu\mathbf{H}\cdot\mathbf{H}$ [J/m³]; $\mu = (1+\chi_m)\mu_0$.
- Coax $\mathcal{L} = (\mu/2\pi)\ln(b/a)$, plates $\mathcal{L} = \mu d/W$; $\mathcal{LC} = \mu\epsilon$, $\mathcal{G}/\mathcal{C} = \sigma/\epsilon$, $1/\sqrt{\mathcal{LC}} = 1/\sqrt{\mu\epsilon}$.
- $\mathbf{B} = \nabla\times\mathbf{A}$, $\mathbf{E} = -\nabla\Phi - \partial\mathbf{A}/\partial t$; gauge: $\mathbf{A}+\nabla\lambda$, $\Phi - \partial_t\lambda$.

> [!exam] On exams
> "Find the inductance (per unit length) of …" is the magnetic twin of Exam 1 problem 4, and the grading rubric is the same chain: a correct $\mathbf{H}$ from Ampère's law with the right loop, $\mathbf{B} = \mu\mathbf{H}$ with the *medium's* $\mu$, a flux integral over the right surface (the one bounded by the current path — for a coax, the $r$–$z$ rectangle, not a circle), units of H or H/m, and $N$ handled once (field) and again (turns linked). Energy questions want $\tfrac12LI^2$ or $\int\tfrac12\mu H^2dV$ and expect you to show they agree. Sign questions want $V = L\,dI/dt$ as a drop in the direction of $I$. And the one-liner that keeps appearing: $\mathcal{LC} = \mu\epsilon$ — if your coax numbers do not satisfy it, something in the chain is wrong.
