*Lecture 12 · course notes §12 · slides "Magnetic Flux and Magnetic Fields / Biot–Savart Law / Ampère's Law" · prev: [[1-electrostatics/11-lorentz-drude-models-for-conductivity-and-susceptibility|Lecture 11]] · next: [[2-magnetostatics/13-current-sheets-solenoids-and-the-vector-potential|Lecture 13]] · practice: [[practice/12-magnetic-force-biot-savart-and-ampere|12 problems]]*

> [!abstract] In one breath
> Two parallel wires carrying currents in the same direction pull on each other, even though neither carries net charge. The explanation is relativistic: in the frame of the drifting electrons of one wire, the other wire's ion and electron spacings are Lorentz-contracted by different amounts, so that wire *is* charged there, and its ordinary Coulomb field pushes on the electrons. Back in the lab we package the same force as $\mathbf{F} = q\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B}$ with a new field, $\mathbf{B} = \dfrac{\mu_0I}{2\pi r}\hat\phi$, circling the wire. Two laws then let you find $\mathbf{B}$ for any steady current: **Biot–Savart** (add up $1/R^2$ contributions of current elements — Coulomb's law with a cross product) and **Ampère** (the circulation of $\mathbf{H} = \mathbf{B}/\mu_0$ around any loop equals the current through it — Gauss's law with a loop instead of a surface). In differential form, $\nabla\times\mathbf{H} = \mathbf{J}$ and $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B} = 0$: the mirror image of electrostatics.

Ampère's 1820 experiment: two wires with currents in the same direction attract; opposite directions repel; the force per unit length falls off as $1/r$. The force on a short piece of wire 2 is proportional to its current and length, $I_2\,d\mathbf{l}_2$, and to the current of wire 1, and it is perpendicular to the wire — so it is written as a cross product with a field that wire 1 produces:

> [!key] Magnetic force and the magnetic flux density
> $$
> d\mathbf{F} = I\,d\mathbf{l}\times\mathbf{B},\qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad \mathbf{F} = q\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B}\quad\text{on a charge }q\text{ moving at }\mathbf{v},
> $$
> because a current element is moving charge: $I\,d\mathbf{l} = (\text{charge per length}\cdot v)\,d\mathbf{l} = q\mathbf{v}$ summed over the carriers in $d\mathbf{l}$. $\mathbf{B}$ is the **magnetic flux density**, in tesla: $\text{T} = \text{N/(A·m)} = \text{Wb/m}^2$. This is the second half of the Lorentz force $\mathbf{F} = q(\mathbf{E}+\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B})$ announced in [[1-electrostatics/01-fields-forces-and-the-maxwell-roadmap|Lecture 1]], now with a source for $\mathbf{B}$.

Two consequences to keep in view. The magnetic force is perpendicular to $\mathbf{v}$, so it **does no work**: static magnetic fields deflect charges, they never speed them up. And the force on a *stationary* charge near a current-carrying wire is zero — the wire is neutral — which is exactly what makes the next section surprising.

## 2. Where the force comes from: a charged wire, in the right frame

Take an ideal wire: a lattice of positive ions at rest, electrons drifting left at speed $v$, current $I$ flowing right. In the lab the ion and electron line densities cancel, $\lambda_+ = -\lambda_-$, and $I = v\lambda_+$ ($\lambda$ is the notes' symbol here for a line charge density, $\rho_l$ elsewhere on this site). A test charge $q$ moving *with* the electrons (left, at $v$) sees, in **its own frame**, the ions moving right at $v$ and the electrons at rest. Lorentz contraction (a moving ruler is shorter by $1/\gamma$, $\gamma = 1/\sqrt{1-v^2/c^2}$) now acts on the two line charges differently:

> [!derivation]- The relativistic bookkeeping (course notes, footnote 3)
> - In the lab the *electrons* are moving, so their spacing is already contracted: the lab density $\lambda_-$ is $\gamma$ times the density $\lambda_-'$ they have in their own frame. Hence in the electron frame $\lambda_-' = \lambda_-/\gamma$ — sparser.
> - In the electron frame the *ions* are moving, so their spacing is contracted there: $\lambda_+' = \gamma\lambda_+$ — denser.
> - Net line charge seen by the test charge: $\lambda' = \gamma\lambda_+ + \lambda_-/\gamma = \lambda_+\big(\gamma - 1/\gamma\big) = \gamma\lambda_+\dfrac{v^2}{c^2}$, positive.
>
> With $\lambda_+ = I/v$: $\lambda' = \gamma I v/c^2 = \gamma Iv\,\mu_0\epsilon_0$, using $1/c^2 = \mu_0\epsilon_0$. The wire's Coulomb field there is the line-charge field of [[1-electrostatics/03-gauss-law-at-work|Lecture 3]], $\mathbf{E}' = \dfrac{\lambda'}{2\pi\epsilon_0r}\hat r = \dfrac{\gamma Iv\mu_0}{2\pi r}\hat r$, and the force on the (stationary, in this frame) test charge is $\mathbf{F}' = q\mathbf{E}'$. Transforming a transverse force back to the lab divides by $\gamma$: $\mathbf{F} = \mathbf{F}'/\gamma = q\dfrac{Iv\mu_0}{2\pi r}\hat r$ — the $\gamma$'s cancel exactly, so the result holds for any $v$.

The lab-frame force is radial, proportional to $qv$ and to $I/r$. Write it as $q\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B}$ with $\mathbf{v}$ along the wire and $\mathbf{B}$ *around* it, and you have identified the field:

> [!key] The field of a long straight current
> $$
> \mathbf{B} = \frac{\mu_0I}{2\pi r}\,\hat\phi,\qquad \mu_0 = 4\pi\times10^{-7}\ \text{H/m},\qquad \mu_0\epsilon_0 = \frac1{c^2}.
> $$
> $\hat\phi$ is the azimuthal direction given by the **right-hand rule**: thumb along $I$, fingers curl along $\mathbf{B}$. The field lines are circles around the wire; they do not begin or end anywhere.

> [!intuition] Why such a tiny effect produces an everyday force
> The relative charge imbalance is $\lambda'/\lambda_+ = v^2/c^2$: for a 1 A current in a 1 mm² copper wire the drift speed is $7\times10^{-5}$ m/s, so $v^2/c^2\approx6\times10^{-26}$. But $\lambda_+$ is enormous — about $1.4\times10^4$ C/m of positive charge per metre of wire, balanced to that precision by electrons. The product, $\lambda'\sim10^{-21}$ C/m, makes a field of order $10^{-9}$ V/m at a centimetre — exactly $vB$ with $B = \mu_0I/(2\pi r) = 2\times10^{-5}$ T. Magnetism is what is left of the Coulomb force when a nearly perfect cancellation is spoiled by relativity.

The signs in the figure are worth a minute. A positive test charge moving *with the electrons* (against $I$) is repelled, as the electron-frame picture says (a positively charged wire repels it). A positive charge moving *along* $I$ is attracted — so the electrons of a second parallel wire, which move against $I_2$ but are negative, are attracted when $I_2\parallel I_1$. That is the two-wire attraction. For a real resistive wire a small surface charge also exists in the lab ([[1-electrostatics/11-lorentz-drude-models-for-conductivity-and-susceptibility#6-what-drives-the-current-in-a-wire-a-footnote-worth-a-section|Lecture 11 §6]]); the argument above is for the idealized neutral wire.

## 3. Biot–Savart: Coulomb's law for current elements

The field of the whole wire should be the sum of contributions from its pieces — superposition, as in electrostatics. The rule that reproduces $\mu_0I/(2\pi r)$ is

> [!key] Biot–Savart law
> $$
> d\mathbf{B} = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\,\frac{I\,d\mathbf{l}\times\hat{R}}{R^2},
> $$
> where $\mathbf{R} = R\hat R$ points **from the current element to the field point**. It is Coulomb's law with $\rho\,dV\to I\,d\mathbf{l}$ and a cross product that makes $d\mathbf{B}$ perpendicular to both the element and the line to the field point. Valid for elements of steady, closed current loops (a single isolated element is not a possible steady current; only the full loop integral is physical).

The slides run this through the **five-step program** of [[1-electrostatics/02-coulombs-law-superposition-and-gauss|Lecture 2]] with $d\mathbf{B}$ in place of $d\mathbf{E}$: *draw* (3-D picture, a side view containing the field point, a top view); *divide* the wire into elements $I\,dz\,\hat z$; *write* $d\mathbf{B}$ for one element, $|d\mathbf{l}\times\hat R| = dz\cos\alpha = dz\,r/R$ with $R = \sqrt{r^2+z^2}$; *use symmetry* — every element's $d\mathbf{B}$ points along $\hat\phi$, so nothing cancels and there is nothing to decompose; *integrate*:

$$
B_\phi = \frac{\mu_0Ir}{4\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{dz}{(r^2+z^2)^{3/2}} = \frac{\mu_0Ir}{4\pi}\cdot\frac{2}{r^2} = \frac{\mu_0I}{2\pi r}\quad\checkmark
$$

(the same integral that gave the line-charge field in Lecture 2; the antiderivative is $z/(r^2\sqrt{r^2+z^2})$). You cannot treat the whole wire as one element and "plug in the formula" — the slides say so in boldface, and it is the same mistake as using Coulomb's point-charge law for a line charge.

> [!recipe] The vector bookkeeping of Biot–Savart
> Order matters: $d\mathbf{l}\times\hat R$, not $\hat R\times d\mathbf{l}$. $\hat R$ runs from the source element to the field point (the slides draw it as $\hat a_{21}$, "from 2 to 1"). The force between two circuits is then a double integral, $\mathbf{F}_1 = \oint_1I_1d\mathbf{l}_1\times\mathbf{B}_2$ — and the elementary forces $d\mathbf{F}_1$, $d\mathbf{F}_2$ between two *elements* are not equal and opposite; only the totals over closed loops are.

## 4. Ampère's law: the circulation of B

Compute the circulation of the straight-wire field around a circle of radius $r$ centred on the wire: $d\mathbf{l} = r\,d\phi\,\hat\phi$, so $\oint\mathbf{B}\cdot d\mathbf{l} = \dfrac{\mu_0I}{2\pi r}\cdot2\pi r = \mu_0I$, **for any radius**. Experiment (and, later, the full theory) says the statement is true for *every* closed path and every steady current distribution:

> [!key] Ampère's law (static)
> $$
> \oint_C\mathbf{B}\cdot d\mathbf{l} = \mu_0I_C\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \oint_C\mathbf{H}\cdot d\mathbf{l} = I_C = \int_S\mathbf{J}\cdot d\mathbf{S},\qquad \mathbf{H}\equiv\frac{\mathbf{B}}{\mu_0}\ [\text{A/m}],
> $$
> where $I_C$ is the net current through **any** surface $S$ bounded by $C$, counted positive when it flows in the direction the right-hand rule assigns from the sense of $C$ (thumb along $d\mathbf{l}$, fingers through $S$; equivalently, $d\mathbf{S}$ by the Stokes convention of [[1-electrostatics/06-circulation-and-boundary-conditions|Lecture 6]]). Currents that do not pierce $S$ — that are not *linked* by $C$ — contribute nothing, however close they are. $\mathbf{H}$ is introduced "for convenience": it is the field whose circulation is the current, in amperes per metre, and in free space it is just $\mathbf{B}/\mu_0$.

> [!intuition] Gauss ↔ Ampère
> Gauss's law: the flux of $\mathbf{D}$ out of a closed *surface* equals the charge inside. Ampère's law: the circulation of $\mathbf{H}$ around a closed *loop* equals the current through. The slides write $\oint\mathbf{D}\cdot d\mathbf{S} = Q_{\text{enc}}$ at the top of the coax challenge question for exactly this reason: the recipe is identical. Choose the surface or loop that matches the symmetry so the field comes out of the integral as a constant; then $D\cdot(\text{area}) = Q_{\text{enc}}$ becomes $H\cdot(\text{length}) = I_{\text{enc}}$ — "$B\cdot2\pi r$" is the magnetic "$D\cdot4\pi r^2$". The names match too: $\oint\mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{l}$ is an **EMF** [V] and $\oint\mathbf{H}\cdot d\mathbf{l}$ an **MMF** [A]; in statics the first is zero and the second is the linked current.

**Differential form.** Stokes' theorem, $\oint_C\mathbf{H}\cdot d\mathbf{l} = \int_S(\nabla\times\mathbf{H})\cdot d\mathbf{S}$, holds for every $S$ with $\int_S\mathbf{J}\cdot d\mathbf{S}$ on the other side, so the integrands must agree:

> [!key] The laws of magnetostatics
> $$
> \nabla\times\mathbf{H} = \mathbf{J},\qquad \nabla\cdot\mathbf{B} = 0 .
> $$
> Compare electrostatics: $\nabla\times\mathbf{E} = 0$, $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{D} = \rho$. The magnetic field has its source in its **curl** and no divergence; the electric field has its source in its **divergence** and no curl. The second equation is the one met in [[1-electrostatics/03-gauss-law-at-work#6-the-magnetic-counterpart--bds--0|Lecture 3]] — no magnetic charge, lines close on themselves — and it holds for time-varying fields as well. (A slide lists "$\nabla\cdot\mathbf{H} = 0$" as the law; that is equivalent only where $\mu$ is uniform. The fundamental statement is $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B} = 0$.)

Check on the straight wire: off the axis, $(\nabla\times\mathbf{B})_z = \dfrac1r\dfrac{d}{dr}(rB_\phi) = \dfrac1r\dfrac{d}{dr}\Big(\dfrac{\mu_0I}{2\pi}\Big) = 0$ — no current there — and $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B} = \dfrac1r\partial_\phi B_\phi = 0$. The curl is concentrated on the wire itself, where $\mathbf{J}$ is a δ-function (next section).

**When "static" is good enough.** These laws also hold *quasi-statically* for a time-varying $\mathbf{J}(\mathbf{r},t)$ over a region of size $L$, provided the current changes on a time scale $\tau\gg L/c$ — the time light takes to cross the region — equivalently $L\ll\lambda = c/f$. Then $\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{r},t) = \mu_0\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{r},t)$ follows the instantaneous current, accompanied by a slowly varying $\mathbf{E}$ that Faraday's law will supply ([[2-magnetostatics/14-faradays-law-and-induced-emf|Lecture 14]]). This **magneto-quasi-static** (MQS) regime is where inductors live, just as the quasi-static condition of [[1-electrostatics/10-capacitance-and-conductance|Lecture 10]] is where capacitors live.

## 5. Describing currents: J, Jₛ, I, and δ-functions

The $\mathbf{J}$ in Ampère's law is a volume current density [A/m²] — $\sigma\mathbf{E}$ in a conductor, $Nq\mathbf{v}$ in general ([[1-electrostatics/11-lorentz-drude-models-for-conductivity-and-susceptibility|Lecture 11]]). Thinner distributions are written with δ-functions, exactly as charge densities were in [[1-electrostatics/03-gauss-law-at-work#5-charge-densities-and-δ-functions|Lecture 3]]:

| distribution | units | as a volume density $\mathbf{J}$ |
|---|---|---|
| surface current $\mathbf{J}_s(y,z)$ on the plane $x = x_0$ | A/m | $\mathbf{J}_s(y,z)\,\delta(x-x_0)$ |
| line current $I(z)$ along the line $x = x_0$, $y = y_0$ | A | $\hat z\,I(z)\,\delta(x-x_0)\,\delta(y-y_0)$ |
| point charge $Q$ moving at $\mathbf{v}$ through $\mathbf{r}_0(t)$ | — | $Q\mathbf{v}\,\delta(x-x_0)\delta(y-y_0)\delta(z-z_0)$ (time-varying) |

Each δ carries 1/m, so the units always come out as A/m². Note that $\mathbf{J}_s$ is current per unit **width** of the sheet, measured across the flow; to get amperes from it you integrate across the flow, not over an area.

> [!example] Example — total current on a sheet (course notes, Example 1)
> $\mathbf{J}_s = \hat z\,y\,\text{rect}(y - \tfrac12)$ A/m on the plane $x = 0$: a strip $0<y<1$ m wide (infinitely long in $z$) whose current density grows linearly from 0 to 1 A/m across it. The total current is the integral *across* the strip: $I = \displaystyle\int_0^1y\,dy = \tfrac12$ A. (The slide version of this bookkeeping once dropped a δ from the line current: see the [[0-toolkit/04-errata-in-the-course-materials|errata page]].)

The lecture's challenge question: a solid core of radius $a$ carries $I$ uniformly ($J = I/\pi a^2$), the thin shell at $r = b$ returns it. Where is $\mathbf{H}$ constant? By symmetry $\mathbf{H} = H_\phi(r)\hat\phi$, so for a circle of radius $r$: $H_\phi\cdot2\pi r = I_{\text{enc}}(r)$, and

$$
H_\phi = \begin{cases}\dfrac{Ir}{2\pi a^2}, & r<a\quad(I_{\text{enc}} = I\,r^2/a^2)\\[8pt] \dfrac{I}{2\pi r}, & a<r<b\\[8pt] 0, & r>b\quad(I_{\text{enc}} = I - I).\end{cases}
$$

Answer **(c)**, $r>b$: the only region where $H$ does not vary with $r$ is the one where it is zero. Inside the core the field grows linearly, like $D$ inside a uniformly charged cylinder in Lecture 3; between the conductors it falls as $1/r$, like $D$ outside a line charge; outside the cable the two currents cancel, like $D$ outside a coax with $\pm$ charges. Every line of this is a translation of a Unit 1 result.

> [!trap] Counting the enclosed current
> $I_{\text{enc}}$ is a *signed* sum. For a clockwise contour linking $I_1$ out of the page and $I_2$ into the page, $I_{\text{enc}} = I_2 - I_1$ (into the page is the right-hand direction for a clockwise loop); for a counter-clockwise contour it is $I_1 - I_2$. And only the current *through* the surface counts: a wire running past the loop without threading it contributes nothing, even though its field is present along the whole path. Finally, the sense of $\hat\phi$ is set by the current direction, not by the coordinate axes: a current in $-\hat x$ makes $\mathbf{H}$ circulate in $-\hat\phi$ about the $+x$ axis.

## 7. Summary

- $\mathbf{F} = q\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B}$, $d\mathbf{F} = I\,d\mathbf{l}\times\mathbf{B}$; magnetic forces do no work; parallel currents attract.
- The force is the Coulomb force of a wire that is charged *in the moving frame*; the lab packages it as $\mathbf{B} = \mu_0I/(2\pi r)\,\hat\phi$ with $\mu_0\epsilon_0 = 1/c^2$.
- Biot–Savart: $d\mathbf{B} = \mu_0I\,d\mathbf{l}\times\hat R/(4\pi R^2)$, $\hat R$ from source to field point; five steps; $\int dz/(r^2+z^2)^{3/2} = 2/r^2$.
- Ampère: $\oint\mathbf{H}\cdot d\mathbf{l} = I_{\text{enc}}$ through any surface on $C$, right-hand rule; $\mathbf{H} = \mathbf{B}/\mu_0$ [A/m].
- $\nabla\times\mathbf{H} = \mathbf{J}$, $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B} = 0$ — electrostatics with div and curl swapped; valid quasi-statically for $L\ll c/f$.
- $\mathbf{J}_s\,\delta(x-x_0)$, $\hat zI\,\delta(x-x_0)\delta(y-y_0)$; integrate $\mathbf{J}_s$ across the flow to get $I$.
- Coax: $H_\phi = Ir/(2\pi a^2)$, $I/(2\pi r)$, $0$.

> [!exam] On exams
> The magnetic twin of Exam 1 problem 2: "a current distribution with planar/cylindrical symmetry — find $\mathbf{H}$ everywhere". Expect to state the symmetry ($\mathbf{H} = H_\phi(r)\hat\phi$ or $H_y(x)\hat y$), draw the loop, count $I_{\text{enc}}$ region by region, and give units (A/m for $\mathbf{H}$, T for $\mathbf{B}$). Direction questions — which way does $\mathbf{B}$ point, which way is the force on a charge or a wire — are the cheap points; use the right-hand rule and $q\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B}$ explicitly rather than by instinct.
