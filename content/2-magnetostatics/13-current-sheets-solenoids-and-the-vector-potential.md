*Lecture 13 · course notes §13 · slides "Ampère's Law" · prev: [[2-magnetostatics/12-magnetic-force-biot-savart-and-amperes-law|Lecture 12]] · next: [[2-magnetostatics/14-faradays-law-and-induced-emf|Lecture 14]]*

> [!abstract] In one breath
> [[1-electrostatics/03-gauss-law-at-work|Lecture 3]] put Gauss's law to work on the three symmetric charge distributions; this lecture does the same with Ampère's law and the symmetric *current* distributions, and every answer is the Unit 1 answer with "normal" replaced by "tangential". A sheet of current makes a tangential field of magnitude $J_s/2$ that reverses across the sheet and does not fall off with distance; a slab turns the step into a ramp; a solenoid — a current sheet rolled into a cylinder — traps a uniform field $\mu_0nI$ inside and nothing outside. The second half builds the magnetic analogue of the scalar potential. Because $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B} = 0$ always, $\mathbf{B}$ can be written as $\nabla\times\mathbf{A}$; choosing $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{A} = 0$ turns Ampère's law into $\nabla^2\mathbf{A} = -\mu_0\mathbf{J}$, whose solution is the Coulomb integral with $\rho/\epsilon_0\to\mu_0\mathbf{J}$. Applied to a single loop it gives the dipole field that bar magnets, compass needles and the Earth all share.

A uniform surface current $\mathbf{J}_s = J_s\hat z$ on the plane $x = 0$, infinite in $y$ and $z$. The five-step reasoning first, because it fixes the *direction* without any integral: the sheet is a stack of filaments along $z$; each makes a field circling it; pair a filament at $+y'$ with its mirror at $-y'$ and, at a field point on the $x$ axis, the $\hat x$ parts of their fields cancel while the $\hat y$ parts add. So $\mathbf{B} = \hat yB_y(x)$, odd in $x$, independent of $y$ and $z$. ("Aha!" says the slide — now Ampère's law does the integral for free.) Take the rectangle in the figure: two long sides of length $L$ parallel to $\hat y$ at $x = \mp W/2$, two short sides crossing the sheet. Along the short sides $\mathbf{B}\perp d\mathbf{l}$; along each long side the field is parallel to the direction of travel (it reverses with $x$, and so does the direction you walk), so both contribute $+B_yL$:

$$
\oint_C\mathbf{B}\cdot d\mathbf{l} = 2B_yL = \mu_0I_C = \mu_0J_sL\quad\Rightarrow\quad B_y = \frac{\mu_0J_s}{2}\ (x>0).
$$

> [!key] Field of an infinite current sheet
> $$
> \mathbf{H} = \hat y\,\frac{J_s}{2}\,\text{sgn}(x),\qquad\mathbf{B} = \mu_0\mathbf{H},\qquad\text{in general}\qquad \mathbf{H} = \tfrac12\,\mathbf{J}_s\times\hat n,
> $$
> with $\hat n$ the unit normal pointing from the sheet *toward the field point*. Check: for $x>0$, $\hat n = \hat x$ and $\tfrac12J_s\hat z\times\hat x = \tfrac12J_s\hat y$ ✓. The magnitude is independent of distance — the sheet is infinite, so there is no scale to fall off with — and the **tangential component of $\mathbf{H}$ jumps by $J_s$** across the sheet. That jump, $\hat n\times(\mathbf{H}_1-\mathbf{H}_2) = \mathbf{J}_s$, is a boundary condition; Lecture 16 (notes and slides) states it. It is the magnetic twin of $D_n$ jumping by $\rho_s$ across a charge sheet.

The slides place the same sheet on $y = 0$ with the current along $z$ and get $\mathbf{H} = -\tfrac12J_{s0}\hat x\,\text{sgn}(y)$ — same physics, same formula $\tfrac12\mathbf{J}_s\times\hat n$, different axes. Students get $\text{sgn}$ backwards more often than any other sign in this unit; the fix is to evaluate $\mathbf{J}_s\times\hat n$ on one side explicitly rather than remembering a picture.

## 2. The current slab

Smear the same current through a slab, $\mathbf{J} = \hat zJ_0$ for $|x|<W/2$, zero outside. Same symmetry, same rectangle, now of half-width $x$ centred on the slab. For $|x|<W/2$ the enclosed current is $J_0\cdot2x\cdot L$; for $|x|\ge W/2$ it is the whole slab, $J_0WL$:

> [!key] Field of a current slab
> $$
> \mathbf{H} = \begin{cases}\hat y\,J_0x, & |x|<W/2,\\[4pt] \hat y\,J_0\dfrac W2\,\text{sgn}(x), & |x|\ge W/2.\end{cases}
> $$
> A ramp of slope $J_0$ inside — $(\nabla\times\mathbf{H})_z = dH_y/dx = J_0$ ✓ — saturating at $\pm J_0W/2$ outside, **continuous** at the faces because there is no *surface* current there. From outside, the slab is indistinguishable from a sheet with $J_s = J_0W$.

Hold this next to the charged slab of Lecture 3 ($D_x = \rho_0x$ inside, $\pm\rho_0W/2$ outside): the plots are identical, with $D_x\to H_y$ and $\rho_0\to J_0$. The worked problem [[problems/current-slab-and-sheet-by-amperes-law]] combines a slab and a sheet by superposition, exactly as the Exam 1 slab problem did with charge.

Wind a long wire into a tight helix, $n$ turns per metre, each carrying $I$ counter-clockwise when viewed from the top. From a little distance the winding is a cylindrical **current sheet** $\mathbf{J}_s = nI\,\hat\phi$. By symmetry the field is along the axis and depends only on $r$. Two Amperian rectangles of height $L$ settle everything:

- $C_1$ with both long legs *outside* the coil links $nL$ turns going one way and $nL$ going the other: $\oint_{C_1}\mathbf{B}\cdot d\mathbf{l} = 0$. So the exterior field is the same at every radius — uniform — and for an isolated infinite coil that uniform value is zero (the field of a current loop of finite size falls off, so an infinitely long coil can only have zero field at infinity and hence everywhere outside).
- $C_2$ with one leg inside and one outside links $nL$ turns all going the same way: $B_zL = \mu_0nLI$.

> [!key] Field of an infinite solenoid
> $$
> \mathbf{B} = \hat z\,\mu_0nI,\qquad \mathbf{H} = \hat z\,nI\quad\text{inside};\qquad \mathbf{B} = 0\quad\text{outside}.
> $$
> Uniform inside, independent of the coil's radius, and — read it as the sheet rule — tangential $\mathbf{H}$ jumps by $J_s = nI$ across the winding, from $0$ to $nI$.

Two honest footnotes from the course notes. Ampère's law alone cannot rule out a uniform exterior field due to *other* sources; if one exists, it adds to the interior field too, unrelated to $I$. And a real solenoid of finite length has $\mathbf{H}\approx\hat znI$ at its centre, dropping to $\tfrac12nI$ at each end, with the field lines fringing out and looping back around the outside (they must — they have nowhere to end). For a coil much longer than its radius the infinite result is excellent at the centre.

## 4. Statics, side by side

> [!key] The two static theories (course notes, Lecture 13)
> **Static electric fields** are *curl-free*: $\nabla\times\mathbf{E} = 0$, $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{D} = \rho$, with $\mathbf{D} = \epsilon\mathbf{E}$, $\epsilon = \epsilon_r\epsilon_0$.
> **Static magnetic fields** are *divergence-free*: $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B} = 0$, $\nabla\times\mathbf{H} = \mathbf{J}$, with $\mathbf{B} = \mu\mathbf{H}$, $\mu = \mu_r\mu_0$ (relative permeabilities other than 1 are explained in Lecture 17).
>
> Full translation table: the [[2-magnetostatics/index#the-dictionary-electrostatics--magnetostatics|dictionary]] on the unit page.

A curl-free field is the gradient of a scalar — that gave $V$ in [[1-electrostatics/05-curl-free-fields-and-the-electrostatic-potential|Lecture 5]]. What is a divergence-free field?

## 5. The vector potential

Any field of the form $\mathbf{B} = \nabla\times\mathbf{A}$ is automatically divergence-free, because $\nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf{A}) = 0$ for every $\mathbf{A}$ (write it as a determinant with two identical rows of $\partial$'s, or expand: $\partial_x(\partial_yA_z-\partial_zA_y) + \partial_y(\partial_zA_x-\partial_xA_z) + \partial_z(\partial_xA_y-\partial_yA_x)$ cancels pairwise). Conversely every divergence-free field can be written this way. So:

> [!key] Vector potential and the Coulomb gauge
> $$
> \mathbf{B} = \nabla\times\mathbf{A}\quad[\mathbf{A}\ \text{in Wb/m} = \text{T·m}].
> $$
> $\mathbf{A}$ is not unique: adding $\nabla\lambda$ to it, for any scalar $\lambda$, leaves $\nabla\times\mathbf{A}$ unchanged (because $\nabla\times\nabla\lambda = 0$). In electrostatics the freedom was a constant in $V$ and we spent it by "grounding" a convenient point; here the freedom is a gradient, and we spend it by prescribing $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{A}$. The convenient choice in magnetostatics is $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{A} = 0$, the **Coulomb gauge**.

With that choice, the identity $\nabla\times\nabla\times\mathbf{A} = \nabla(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{A}) - \nabla^2\mathbf{A}$ loses its first term, and Ampère's law $\nabla\times\mathbf{B} = \mu_0\mathbf{J}$ becomes

> [!key] Poisson's equation for A, and its solution
> $$
> \nabla^2\mathbf{A} = -\mu_0\mathbf{J}\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \mathbf{A}(\mathbf{r}) = \int\frac{\mu_0\mathbf{J}(\mathbf{r}')}{4\pi\lvert\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}'\rvert}\,d^3r' .
> $$
> Compare $\nabla^2V = -\rho/\epsilon_0$ and $V(\mathbf{r}) = \displaystyle\int\frac{\rho(\mathbf{r}')}{4\pi\epsilon_0\lvert\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}'\rvert}d^3r'$ from [[1-electrostatics/07-poisson-and-laplace|Lecture 7]]: the same Green's function $1/(4\pi\lvert\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}'\rvert)$, applied to each Cartesian component of $\mathbf{J}$ separately. (The vector Laplacian means "the scalar Laplacian of each Cartesian component"; in cylindrical or spherical components it does not split so simply.)

The integral solution satisfies $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{A} = 0$ automatically **provided $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{J} = 0$** — which is true for steady currents, and is the continuity equation of [[3-maxwell-and-waves/16-charge-conservation-displacement-current-and-maxwells-equations|Lecture 16]] with $\partial\rho/\partial t = 0$. Given any static (or quasi-static, $L\ll c/f$) current density, then: integrate to get $\mathbf{A}$, take a curl to get $\mathbf{B}$. The footnote in the notes gives $\mathbf{A}$ a physical meaning in this gauge — potential *momentum* per unit charge, as $V$ is potential energy per unit charge — and [[2-magnetostatics/15-inductance-magnetic-energy-and-the-potentials|Lecture 15]] shows what $\mathbf{A}$ does when fields vary in time.

| | scalar potential $V$ | vector potential $\mathbf{A}$ |
|---|---|---|
| exists because | $\nabla\times\mathbf{E} = 0$ | $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B} = 0$ |
| field from potential | $\mathbf{E} = -\nabla V$ | $\mathbf{B} = \nabla\times\mathbf{A}$ |
| freedom | $V\to V + \text{const}$ | $\mathbf{A}\to\mathbf{A}+\nabla\lambda$ |
| how it is spent | $V = 0$ at a chosen point | $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{A} = 0$ (Coulomb gauge) |
| equation | $\nabla^2V = -\rho/\epsilon_0$ | $\nabla^2\mathbf{A} = -\mu_0\mathbf{J}$ |
| meaning | energy per unit charge | momentum per unit charge |

The payoff of §5 is a field that Biot–Savart alone makes awkward: a circular loop of radius $a$ in the plane $z = 0$ carrying $I$ counter-clockwise, $\mathbf{J}(\mathbf{r}') = I\,\delta(z')\,\delta(r'-a)\,\hat\phi'$ in cylindrical source coordinates. Inserting into the $\mathbf{A}$ integral and doing the trivial $z'$ and $r'$ integrations leaves one angle:

$$
\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{r}) = \frac{\mu_0I}{4\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\frac{(-a\sin\phi',\ a\cos\phi',\ 0)}{\sqrt{(x-a\cos\phi')^2 + (y-a\sin\phi')^2 + z^2}}\,d\phi' \equiv \hat xA_x + \hat yA_y,
$$

so $A_z = 0$ and $B_x = -\partial_zA_y$, $B_y = \partial_zA_x$, $B_z = \partial_xA_y - \partial_yA_x$. By symmetry it is enough to evaluate on the plane $y = 0$, where differentiating under the integral sign gives

$$
B_x = \frac{\mu_0aI}{4\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\frac{z\cos\phi\,d\phi}{(x^2+a^2+z^2-2ax\cos\phi)^{3/2}},\qquad
B_z = \frac{\mu_0aI}{4\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\frac{(a - x\cos\phi)\,d\phi}{(x^2+a^2+z^2-2ax\cos\phi)^{3/2}},
$$

and $B_y = 0$ (odd integrand). There is no elementary closed form for general $(x,z)$ — the integrals are complete elliptic integrals — but two limits are easy:

> [!key] On the axis, and far away
> $$
> B_z(0,0,z) = \frac{\mu_0Ia^2}{2(a^2+z^2)^{3/2}}\quad\xrightarrow{|z|\gg a}\quad \frac{\mu_0Ia^2}{2|z|^3}.
> $$
> At the centre, $B_z = \mu_0I/2a$. Far away the field falls off as the **inverse cube** of the distance: the loop is a **magnetic dipole**, with moment $m = I\pi a^2$ (so the axial far field is $\mu_0m/(2\pi|z|^3)$, the same form as the electric dipole's $p/(2\pi\epsilon_0r^3)$ in [[1-electrostatics/02-coulombs-law-superposition-and-gauss|Lecture 2]]).

Why does it fall off at all, and why $1/r^3$? Every closed field line links the loop, so $\oint\mathbf{B}\cdot d\mathbf{l} = \mu_0I$ along *each* of them: a longer line, further out, must carry a weaker average field. How much weaker is what the on-axis formula says — $\mu_0Ia^2/(2|z|^3)$ — and the same $r^{-3}$ holds in every direction, with the angular pattern of a dipole. Far from the loop the field lines on the plane $y = 0$ are the curves $r = L\sin^2\theta$ in spherical coordinates ($L$ = the line's radius where it crosses the equatorial plane) — the familiar bar-magnet pattern, and the topology of the Earth's field.

Two coaxial loops with currents in the same sense attract (apply $I\,d\mathbf{l}\times\mathbf{B}$ to the upper loop in the field of the lower one: the radial component of $\mathbf{B}$ at the upper loop gives an axial force toward the lower loop). Bar magnets are stacks of atomic current loops and interact the same way, through the $q\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B}$ term of the Lorentz force; Lecture 17 turns that observation into the magnetization $\mathbf{M}$ and the permeability $\mu$.

## 7. Summary

- Sheet: $\mathbf{H} = \tfrac12\mathbf{J}_s\times\hat n$, tangential, $\pm J_s/2$, no distance dependence; tangential $\mathbf{H}$ jumps by $J_s$.
- Slab: ramp $J_0x$ inside, $\pm J_0W/2$ outside, continuous; the charged-slab plot with $D\to H$.
- Solenoid: $\mathbf{B} = \mu_0nI\hat z$ inside, $0$ outside; a rolled-up current sheet; finite coil: $\tfrac12nI$ at the ends.
- $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B} = 0\Rightarrow\mathbf{B} = \nabla\times\mathbf{A}$; Coulomb gauge $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{A} = 0$; $\nabla^2\mathbf{A} = -\mu_0\mathbf{J}$; $\mathbf{A} = \int\mu_0\mathbf{J}\,d^3r'/(4\pi\lvert\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}'\rvert)$.
- Loop: $B_z = \mu_0Ia^2/[2(a^2+z^2)^{3/2}]$ on the axis; far field $\propto1/r^3$ (dipole); lines $r = L\sin^2\theta$; parallel loops attract.

> [!exam] On exams
> The sheet, slab and solenoid are the three "find $\mathbf{H}$ by Ampère's law" set pieces, and a superposition of them (a slab plus a sheet, two sheets, a solenoid inside a solenoid) is the natural exam problem — the answer to [[problems/current-slab-and-sheet-by-amperes-law]] shows the pattern. Know $\tfrac12\mathbf{J}_s\times\hat n$ cold, with the direction worked out from the cross product, and know that $H_t$ jumps by $J_s$ while $B_n$ is continuous. The vector potential is examined lightly: $\mathbf{B} = \nabla\times\mathbf{A}$, why the gauge freedom exists, and $\nabla^2\mathbf{A} = -\mu_0\mathbf{J}$ as the twin of Poisson's equation; the loop integrals are not for exams, the on-axis result might be.
