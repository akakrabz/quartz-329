*Lecture 8 · course notes §8 · slides "Conductors" and "Dielectrics" · prev: [[1-electrostatics/07-poisson-and-laplace|Lecture 7]] · next: [[1-electrostatics/09-static-fields-in-dielectric-media|Lecture 9]] · practice: [[practice/08-conductors-dielectrics-and-polarization|12 problems]]*

> [!abstract] In one breath
> Matter is made of charges that respond to the field. **Free** charges (a conductor) move until nothing pushes them: zero interior field, an equipotential body, all charge on the surface. **Bound** charges (a dielectric) only shift, turning each atom into a tiny dipole; their sum, the **polarization** $\mathbf{P}$, opposes the applied field and *weakens* the interior field without cancelling it. $\mathbf{D} \equiv \epsilon_0\mathbf{E} + \mathbf{P}$ hides the bound charge, so $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{D} = \rho$ needs only the charge you placed. For ordinary materials $\mathbf{P}\propto\mathbf{E}$ and it all collapses into one number: $\mathbf{D} = \epsilon\mathbf{E}$.

## 1. Two ideal materials

A material's **conductivity** $\sigma$ [S/m] is the constant in the point form of Ohm's law,

> [!key] Ohm's law (the only Ohm's law this course uses)
> $$
> \mathbf{J} = \sigma\,\mathbf{E}\qquad[\text{A/m}^2 = (\text{S/m})(\text{V/m})].
> $$
> For a block of length $l$, cross-section $A$: $V = El$, $I = JA$, so $R = V/I = l/(\sigma A)$. Resistivity is $1/\sigma$ [Ω·m]. Carriers keep colliding with the lattice, so the drift velocity, and $\mathbf{J}$, settles proportional to $\mathbf{E}$ ([[1-electrostatics/11-lorentz-drude-models-for-conductivity-and-susceptibility|Lecture 11]] builds the model).

Room-temperature conductivities span over twenty orders of magnitude: copper $\approx 6\times10^{7}$ S/m, sea water $\approx 4$, germanium $\approx 2$, distilled water $\sim10^{-4}$, glass $\sim10^{-12}$, fused quartz $\sim10^{-17}$; superconductors effectively $\infty$. The two idealized ends:

| | limit | consequence | circuit analogy |
|---|---|---|---|
| **perfect electric conductor (PEC)** | $\sigma\to\infty$ | $\mathbf{E} = 0$ inside, whatever $\mathbf{J}$ flows | short circuit: $V = 0$ for any $I$ |
| **perfect dielectric (insulator)** | $\sigma\to0$ | $\mathbf{J} = 0$ inside, whatever $\mathbf{E}$ is applied | open circuit: $I = 0$ for any $V$ |

## 2. Conductors in electrostatics

A PEC has $\mathbf{E} = 0$ inside by definition; a *real* conductor reaches the same state in equilibrium, quickly.

In a uniform field $\mathbf{E}_0$, a conducting slab's free electrons ($q<0$) drift *against* the field, piling up on one face and leaving the other positive. The neutral slab's induced pair $\pm\rho_s$ makes a **secondary field** $-\mathbf{E}_0$ inside; charge moves until the total interior field is zero. Hence:

> [!key] A conductor in electrostatic equilibrium
> 1. $\mathbf{E} = 0$ everywhere inside.
> 2. $V$ is constant: the whole body, surface included, is one **equipotential** ($V(b) - V(a) = -\int_a^b\mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{l} = 0$ for any two of its points).
> 3. $\rho = \nabla\cdot\mathbf{D} = 0$ inside: **all net charge lives on the surface**, as $\rho_s$.
> 4. At the surface $\hat{n}\cdot\mathbf{D} = \rho_s$ and $\hat{n}\times\mathbf{E} = 0$, $\hat{n}$ **out of the conductor**: the field just outside is **perpendicular** to the surface, and its normal $\mathbf{D}$ *is* the local surface charge.
>
> Inside a PEC the course also takes $\mathbf{H} = \mathbf{B} = 0$ (a static $\mathbf{H}$ *could* exist, but there is no way to set one up).

> [!derivation]- How fast does a conductor reach equilibrium?
> Combine charge conservation $\partial\rho/\partial t + \nabla\cdot\mathbf{J} = 0$ (Lecture 4), Ohm's law $\mathbf{J} = \sigma\mathbf{E}$, and Gauss's law $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{E} = \rho/\epsilon$ in a homogeneous conductor:
> $$
> \frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t} + \sigma\nabla\cdot\mathbf{E} = \frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t} + \frac{\sigma}{\epsilon}\rho = 0
> \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
> \rho(t) = \rho(0)\,e^{-t/\tau},\qquad \tau = \frac{\epsilon}{\sigma} .
> $$
> Interior charge decays with the **relaxation time** $\tau = \epsilon/\sigma$, migrating to the surface. With no current driven by an external circuit, $\mathbf{J} = 0$ and so $\mathbf{E} = 0$ inside. (A wire with steady current: $\rho = 0$ inside but $\mathbf{E} = \mathbf{J}/\sigma\neq0$.) Copper, $\epsilon\approx\epsilon_0$: $\tau\sim10^{-19}$ s — shorter than the model's validity, but an isolated metal is in equilibrium for every field in this course. Sea water ($\epsilon\approx81\epsilon_0$, $\sigma\approx4$ S/m): $\tau\approx0.2$ ns. The same $\tau$ returns in [[1-electrostatics/10-capacitance-and-conductance|Lecture 10]] as a leaky capacitor's $RC = \epsilon/\sigma$.

> [!example] Example — a neutral conducting slab between two charged sheets
> Sheets $\rho_A>0$ (above) and $\rho_B>0$ (below) sandwich an uncharged conducting slab. Find the induced charges $\rho_1$ (top face) and $\rho_2$ (bottom face).
>
> The neutral slab's induced charges are equal and opposite, so *outside the slab* their fields cancel: each sheet gives $\rho_s/(2\epsilon_0)$ away from itself, and in the gaps $\mathbf{D} = \tfrac12(\rho_B - \rho_A)\,\hat{z}$ ($\hat{z}$ up). Inside the slab $\mathbf{D} = 0$. Read the faces with $\hat{n}\cdot\mathbf{D} = \rho_s$, $\hat{n}$ out of the metal:
> - top face, $\hat{n} = +\hat{z}$: $\rho_1 = \tfrac12(\rho_B - \rho_A)$;
> - bottom face, $\hat{n} = -\hat{z}$: $\rho_2 = \tfrac12(\rho_A - \rho_B) = -\rho_1$. ✓
>
> Check: inside the slab the four sheets give $\tfrac{1}{2\epsilon_0}(-\rho_A - \rho_1 + \rho_2 + \rho_B) = 0$ ✓; $\rho_A = \rho_B$ induces nothing, as symmetry demands.

> [!example] Example — a point charge above a grounded conducting plane (concept check)
> Positive $Q$ above an infinite grounded conducting plane. *E is zero above the plane* — no. *E is zero below* — **yes**: the plane shields completely. *Induced charge uniform* — no: $\rho_s = \hat{n}\cdot\mathbf{D}$ follows the field, strongest directly under $Q$. *Total induced charge $+Q$* — no: all of $Q$'s lines end on the plane (none pass through), on negative charge, so the total is $-Q$. *Tangential field at the plane* — no: $\hat{n}\times\mathbf{E} = 0$, every line meets the plane at a right angle.

## 3. Dielectrics: bound charge and polarization

An insulator's electrons are bound to their atoms; an applied field only *displaces* them slightly from the nuclei, making each atom a small **dipole** $\mathbf{p} = q\mathbf{d}$ (charge times displacement from $-$ to $+$), aligned with $\mathbf{E}$. The material is **polarized**.

Inside, each dipole's $+$ end sits next to its neighbour's $-$ end and they cancel on average. At the faces the cancellation fails: uncompensated $+$ on the face the dipoles point toward, $-$ on the other. These **bound surface charges** $\pm\rho_{sb}$ oppose the applied field, but, tethered, they cannot keep moving until it vanishes (unlike a conductor's free charge): the interior field is **reduced, not eliminated**.

> [!key] Polarization
> $$
> \mathbf{P} \equiv N\,\mathbf{p} = \text{dipole moment per unit volume}\qquad[\text{C·m/m}^3 = \text{C/m}^2],
> $$
> $N$ = dipoles per unit volume. $\mathbf{P}$ has the units of surface charge density, no accident: on any surface of a polarized body, outward normal $\hat{n}$, the bound surface charge is
> $$
> \rho_{sb} = \mathbf{P}\cdot\hat{n},
> $$
> and a non-uniform $\mathbf{P}$ leaves a bound **volume** charge $\rho_b = -\nabla\cdot\mathbf{P}$ (more dipole "heads" arrive than "tails" leave). Bound charge is real and makes real fields; you just did not place it and cannot move it.

> [!derivation]- The averaged field of a lattice of dipoles
> Lattice with spacings $\Delta x,\Delta y,\Delta z$, each cell one proton–electron pair displaced by $d$ along $\hat{z}$ ($\mathbf{p} = ed\,\hat{z}$, $N = 1/(\Delta x\Delta y\Delta z)$). Along $z$, protons form sheets $+e/(\Delta x\Delta y)$ and electrons sheets $-e/(\Delta x\Delta y)$, each $+$ sheet $d$ above its $-$ partner. Within a pair: $\mathbf{E}_1 = -\hat{z}\,\frac{e}{\epsilon_0\Delta x\Delta y}$ (opposite to $\mathbf{p}$); between pairs $\mathbf{E}_2 = 0$. Pairs fill a fraction $d/\Delta z$ of the volume, so the averaged bound-charge field is
> $$
> \mathbf{E}_p = \mathbf{E}_1\frac{d}{\Delta z} + \mathbf{E}_2\frac{\Delta z - d}{\Delta z} = -\hat{z}\frac{e\,d}{\epsilon_0\,\Delta x\Delta y\Delta z} = -\frac{N\mathbf{p}}{\epsilon_0} = -\frac{\mathbf{P}}{\epsilon_0}.
> $$
> Inside an infinite slab polarized *normal to its faces*, $\mathbf{E} = \mathbf{E}_0 + \mathbf{E}_p = \mathbf{E}_0 - \mathbf{P}/\epsilon_0$, $\mathbf{E}_0$ being the free charges' field: weaker ($\mathbf{P}\parallel\mathbf{E}_0$), not zero. (Polarized *along* its faces: no bound surface charge, $\mathbf{E}_p = 0$, $\mathbf{E} = \mathbf{E}_0$ inside.)

## 4. The displacement field D, and Gauss's law in matter

Rearranged, the slab result reads $\epsilon_0\mathbf{E} + \mathbf{P} = \epsilon_0\mathbf{E}_0$: the right side is $\epsilon_0\mathbf{E}$ *before* the dielectric was inserted, the free charges' field alone. Hence:

> [!key] The displacement field and Gauss's law for free charge
> $$
> \mathbf{D} \equiv \epsilon_0\mathbf{E} + \mathbf{P}\qquad[\text{C/m}^2],\qquad\qquad \nabla\cdot\mathbf{D} = \rho_f,\qquad \oint_S\mathbf{D}\cdot d\mathbf{S} = Q_{f,\text{enc}} .
> $$
> The original Gauss's law counts *all* charge: $\nabla\cdot(\epsilon_0\mathbf{E}) = \rho_f + \rho_b$. With $\rho_b = -\nabla\cdot\mathbf{P}$ moved left, $\nabla\cdot(\epsilon_0\mathbf{E}+\mathbf{P}) = \rho_f$: bound charge absorbed into $\mathbf{D}$. From here on, unsubscripted $\rho$ means **free** charge — the charges you control. In vacuum $\mathbf{P} = 0$, $\mathbf{D} = \epsilon_0\mathbf{E}$.

Lecture 6's boundary conditions survive (they came from the integral laws): $\hat{n}\cdot(\mathbf{D}_1-\mathbf{D}_2) = \rho_s$ with **free** $\rho_s$, and $\hat{n}\times(\mathbf{E}_1-\mathbf{E}_2) = 0$. For $\mathbf{P}$ the pillbox gives $\hat{n}\cdot(\mathbf{P}_1-\mathbf{P}_2) = -\rho_{sb}$: bound surface charge is how much normal $\mathbf{P}$ *decreases* from medium 2 into medium 1 (along $\hat{n}$).

> [!trap] "D is the same inside and outside" is a slab result, not a law
> For an infinite slab with the field *normal* to its faces, $\mathbf{D}$ inside equals $\epsilon_0\mathbf{E}_0$ outside because the bound face layers are infinite sheets whose fields cancel outside. With the field *along* the faces, $\mathbf{E}$ is unchanged inside and $\mathbf{D}$ jumps by $\mathbf{P}$ — Lecture 6's tangential-$\mathbf{D}$ jump. A dielectric *sphere* in a uniform field has a dipole-like fringe field outside and $\mathbf{E}_p = -\mathbf{P}/(3\epsilon_0)$ inside, not $-\mathbf{P}/\epsilon_0$. General: $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{D} = \rho_f$, and normal $\mathbf{D}$ continuous across an interface without free charge. With planar, cylindrical or spherical symmetry that gives $\mathbf{D}$ from free charge alone — which is why those are the problems you get.

## 5. Linear dielectrics: χe, εr, and ε

For most materials at ordinary fields, $\mathbf{P}$ is proportional to the total field:

> [!key] Constitutive relation of a linear, isotropic dielectric
> $$
> \mathbf{P} = \epsilon_0\chi_e\,\mathbf{E}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
> \mathbf{D} = \epsilon_0\mathbf{E} + \epsilon_0\chi_e\mathbf{E} = \epsilon_0(1+\chi_e)\,\mathbf{E} = \epsilon\,\mathbf{E},
> \qquad \epsilon = \epsilon_r\epsilon_0,\quad \epsilon_r = 1+\chi_e .
> $$
> $\chi_e\ge0$: dimensionless **electric susceptibility**; $\epsilon_r$: dimensionless **relative permittivity** ("dielectric constant"); $\epsilon$: **permittivity** [F/m]. The $\mathbf{E}$ in $\mathbf{P} = \epsilon_0\chi_e\mathbf{E}$ is the **total** field in the material — applied plus the polarization's own — not the applied field alone. Typical $\epsilon_r$: vacuum 1, air 1.0006, glass 4–10, dry-to-wet soil 5–10, silicon 11–12, distilled water 81.

With $\epsilon$ known, use $\mathbf{D} = \epsilon\mathbf{E}$ and the free charges; recover $\mathbf{P} = \mathbf{D} - \epsilon_0\mathbf{E}$ if asked. (Anisotropic crystals — tensor $\epsilon$, $\mathbf{P}$ not parallel to $\mathbf{E}$ — are not treated; plasmas, $\chi_e<0$, are deferred to ECE 350.)

> [!example] Example — the slab between charged plates, three ways
> Sheets $+\rho_{s0}$ (below) and $-\rho_{s0}$ (above) give $\mathbf{E}_a = \hat{z}\,\rho_{s0}/\epsilon_0$ in the gap, now filled with a dielectric of susceptibility $\chi_e$.
> - *Self-consistently:* $P = \epsilon_0\chi_eE$ puts $\pm\rho_{sb} = \pm P$ on the faces, whose field $E_p = -P/\epsilon_0 = -\chi_eE$ opposes $\mathbf{E}_a$. So $E = E_a - \chi_eE$:
> $$
> E = \frac{E_a}{1+\chi_e} = \frac{\rho_{s0}}{\epsilon_0(1+\chi_e)} = \frac{\rho_{s0}}{\epsilon},\qquad \rho_{sb} = \frac{\chi_e}{1+\chi_e}\,\rho_{s0}.
> $$
> - *Via $\mathbf{D}$:* free charge unchanged, so $D = \rho_{s0}$ as without the dielectric (Gauss for free charge, or $D_n$ continuous at faces with no *free* charge), and $E = D/\epsilon$. One line.
> - *Limits:* $\chi_e = 0$ is vacuum ($\mathbf{P}=0$); $\chi_e\to\infty$ drives $E\to0$ — conductor-like.
>
> The habit for the rest of the unit: **$\mathbf{D}$ is fixed by the free charge; $\mathbf{E}$ is reduced by $\epsilon_r$; $\mathbf{P}$ makes up the difference.**

> [!example] Example — copper plates: vacuum, then a fluid, then salt water (HW4 #5)
> Charged copper plates make $\mathbf{E} = 3\hat{z}$ V/m in the vacuum gap, zero elsewhere. (a) $\mathbf{D} = 3\epsilon_0\hat{z}$ C/m², $\mathbf{P} = 0$. (b) Bottom plate: $\rho_s = \hat{z}\cdot\mathbf{D} = 3\epsilon_0$ C/m². (c) Insulating fluid, $\epsilon = 81\epsilon_0$, plate charges unchanged: $\mathbf{D}$ unchanged, $\mathbf{E} = \mathbf{D}/\epsilon = \tfrac{1}{27}\hat{z}$ V/m, $\mathbf{P} = \mathbf{D} - \epsilon_0\mathbf{E} = \tfrac{80}{27}\epsilon_0\hat{z}$ C/m² — the fluid's bound charge cancels 80/81 of the plates' field. (d) Salt makes the fluid conduct ($\sigma\approx4$ S/m): ions drift to the plates until the interior field is *zero*; steady state $\mathbf{E} = \mathbf{D} = \mathbf{P} = 0$ in the fluid — the capacitor is shorted. Conductor versus dielectric in one experiment.

## 6. Summary

- Conductors: $\mathbf{J} = \sigma\mathbf{E}$; in equilibrium $\mathbf{E} = 0$ inside, $V$ constant, $\rho = 0$ inside, $\rho_s = \hat{n}\cdot\mathbf{D}$ and $\mathbf{E}\perp$ surface outside. Equilibrium is reached in $\tau = \epsilon/\sigma$.
- Dielectrics: bound charges shift, $\mathbf{P} = N\mathbf{p}$ [C/m²]; bound charges $\rho_{sb} = \mathbf{P}\cdot\hat{n}$, $\rho_b = -\nabla\cdot\mathbf{P}$; field reduced, not zero.
- $\mathbf{D} = \epsilon_0\mathbf{E}+\mathbf{P}$: Gauss's law counts free charge only; boundary conditions unchanged.
- Linear media: $\mathbf{P} = \epsilon_0\chi_e\mathbf{E}$ (total field), $\mathbf{D} = \epsilon\mathbf{E}$, $\epsilon = \epsilon_0(1+\chi_e) = \epsilon_r\epsilon_0$.
