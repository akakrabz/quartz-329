> [!key] Ampère–Maxwell equation
> $$
> \nabla\times\mathbf{H} = \mathbf{J} + \frac{\partial\mathbf{D}}{\partial t}\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \oint_C\mathbf{H}\cdot d\mathbf{l} = \int_S\Big(\mathbf{J}+\frac{\partial\mathbf{D}}{\partial t}\Big)\cdot d\mathbf{S}.
> $$
> $\partial\mathbf{D}/\partial t$ is the **displacement current density** [A/m²]; through a surface, $I_d = \dfrac{d}{dt}\int_S\mathbf{D}\cdot d\mathbf{S} = d\psi_E/dt$, the rate of change of electric flux, in amperes, so $\oint_C\mathbf{H}\cdot d\mathbf{l} = I_c + I_d$. **Verification:** the divergence gives $0 = \nabla\cdot\mathbf{J} + \dfrac{\partial}{\partial t}\nabla\cdot\mathbf{D} = \nabla\cdot\mathbf{J} + \dfrac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}$ by Gauss's law (assumed, as Maxwell did, to hold for time-varying fields) — the [[concepts/continuity-equation|continuity equation]]. Charge conservation *requires* the term.

**Physics.** Faraday has a changing $\mathbf{B}$ inducing $\mathbf{E}$; Ampère as written, $\nabla\times\mathbf{H} = \mathbf{J}$, has no converse. Beyond the asymmetry, Maxwell saw an inconsistency: the divergence of a curl is identically zero (the identity behind $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B} = 0$ for $\mathbf{B} = \nabla\times\mathbf{A}$), so the static law forces $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{J} = 0$ always, contradicting continuity wherever $\partial\rho/\partial t\neq0$. His 1861 repair adds the one term whose divergence is $\partial\rho/\partial t$.

What the term is. In matter, $\partial\mathbf{D}/\partial t = \epsilon_0\,\partial\mathbf{E}/\partial t + \partial\mathbf{P}/\partial t$. The second piece is the **polarization current** of [[1-electrostatics/11-lorentz-drude-models-for-conductivity-and-susceptibility#5-polarization-current-bound-charges-that-carry-an-ac-current|Lecture 11]] — bound charges really moving. The first exists in empty space with no charges moving: a genuinely new source of magnetic field, and what lets a wave cross vacuum — changing $\mathbf{E}$ makes $\mathbf{H}$, changing $\mathbf{H}$ makes $\mathbf{E}$, and the pair runs off at $1/\sqrt{\mu_0\epsilon_0} = c$. Hertz's waves (around 1888) confirmed it. In the curl-curl step of the [[concepts/wave-equation|wave equation]] it is the only term that survives on the right: without $\partial\mathbf{D}/\partial t$, $\nabla^2\mathbf{E} = 0$ (Laplace), no waves.

It also repairs Ampère's law's surface-independence: with only $\int\mathbf{J}\cdot d\mathbf{S}$ on the right, two surfaces spanning the same loop disagree whenever one is pierced by a wire and the other passes through a gap where charge accumulates; the displacement flux through the second surface makes up the difference.

**Examples.**

> [!example] Slides — MMF around a draining point charge
> A point charge $Q(t)$ at the origin drains through a wire along $+z$, $I = -dQ/dt$. Find $\oint_C\mathbf{H}\cdot d\mathbf{l}$ (the MMF) around a circle of radius $a$ in $z = 0$, counter-clockwise seen from $+z$. Any surface on $C$ will do; the hint: a sphere and a hemisphere.
> - **Dome above** (pierced by the wire; normal consistent with $C$): conduction $+I$; the whole sphere carries flux $Q$ (Gauss), the dome $Q/2$: displacement $\dfrac{d}{dt}\dfrac Q2 = -\dfrac I2$. MMF $= I - I/2 = I/2$.
> - **Bowl below** (no wire): conduction 0; its flux, oriented from $C$, is $-Q/2$: displacement $+I/2$. MMF $= I/2$ again.
>
> Static Ampère would give $I$ for the dome and $0$ for the bowl: a circulation that depends on the imagined surface, which is absurd. $I/2$ is also Biot–Savart for a semi-infinite wire ending at the origin ($H_\phi = I/4\pi a$ in the plane of its end). Numbers: [[problems/mmf-around-a-draining-charge|Worked problem — MMF around a draining point charge]]. Textbook version, the charging capacitor: a surface through the wire sees $I$, one through the gap sees $d\psi_E/dt = dQ/dt = I$.

> [!example] Slides (Lecture 13 deck) — displacement current through an area
> $\mathbf{E} = E_0\,t\,e^{-t^2}\hat z$ in free space; $A = 0.1$ m² in the $xy$-plane, $d\mathbf{S} = \hat z\,dS$. $\psi_E = \int\mathbf{D}\cdot d\mathbf{S} = \epsilon_0AE_0\,te^{-t^2}$ [C], so $I_d = d\psi_E/dt = \epsilon_0AE_0(1-2t^2)e^{-t^2}$ [A], $\epsilon_0A = 8.85\times10^{-13}$ F. It reverses sign at $t = 1/\sqrt2$ s, when the field stops growing. Units: $\epsilon_0$ [F/m] × area [m²] × $E$ [V/m] / time [s] = F·V/s = C/s = A ✓.

- Lossy capacitor: the two currents flow side by side, $I = GV + C\,dV/dt$, conduction plus displacement.

> [!trap]
> - It is not a flow of charge in vacuum; nothing moves. It is a source term for $\mathbf{H}$ that behaves like a current.
> - $\partial/\partial t$, partial; the total $d/dt$ is allowed only in front of the flux integral over a fixed surface.
> - The ratio $\lvert\partial_t\mathbf{D}\rvert/\lvert\sigma\mathbf{E}\rvert = \omega\epsilon/\sigma$ decides whether a medium acts as a dielectric or a conductor at a given frequency — the same $\sigma/\epsilon$ as the relaxation rate (Lectures 8, 10, 23).
> - At a boundary the displacement-current flux through a shrinking loop vanishes, which is why $\hat n\times(\mathbf{H}_1-\mathbf{H}_2) = \mathbf{J}_s$ contains no $\partial\mathbf{D}/\partial t$ ([[concepts/boundary-conditions|Boundary conditions at an interface]]).

> [!exam] On exams
> Given $\mathbf{E}(t)$ and an area, $I_d = \epsilon\,d/dt\int\mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{S}$, units checked. Concept: why the term is needed (divergence of a curl is zero; charge conservation), or the two-surface paradox — the same MMF from a dome pierced by the wire and from a bowl that is not.
