> [!key] Divergence, and Gauss's law in differential form
> $$
> \nabla\cdot\mathbf{D} \equiv \frac{\partial D_x}{\partial x}+\frac{\partial D_y}{\partial y}+\frac{\partial D_z}{\partial z}
> = \lim_{\Delta V\to 0}\frac{\oint\mathbf{D}\cdot d\mathbf{S}}{\Delta V},
> \qquad\boxed{\ \nabla\cdot\mathbf{D} = \rho\ }
> $$
> A **scalar**: net outward flux per unit volume. $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{D}>0$: a *source* (faucet; lines start there); negative: a *sink* (drain); zero: what flows in flows out. $\nabla$ ("del") $= \left(\dfrac{\partial}{\partial x},\dfrac{\partial}{\partial y},\dfrac{\partial}{\partial z}\right)$; dotted into a field it gives the divergence, crossed the [[concepts/curl|curl]]. Cylindrical and spherical forms on the [[0-toolkit/02-vector-calculus-cheatsheet|cheat sheet]].

**Physics.** Gauss's law at a point: the divergence of $\mathbf{D}$ *is* the local charge density. The same argument on $\oint\mathbf{B}\cdot d\mathbf{S} = 0$ gives $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B} = 0$: no magnetic sources or sinks anywhere. Continuity: $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{J} = -\partial\rho/\partial t$ — current flowing out of a point drains its charge ([[concepts/continuity-equation|Continuity equation (charge conservation)]]).

> [!derivation]- Gauss's law on a tiny box
> Apply $\oint_S\mathbf{D}\cdot d\mathbf{S} = \int_V\rho\,dV$ to a tiny box $\Delta x\,\Delta y\,\Delta z$ centred at $(x,y,z)$. Flux out of the two faces perpendicular to $\hat{x}$:
> $$
> \Big[D_x\big(x+\tfrac{\Delta x}{2}\big) - D_x\big(x-\tfrac{\Delta x}{2}\big)\Big]\Delta y\,\Delta z \approx \frac{\partial D_x}{\partial x}\,\Delta x\,\Delta y\,\Delta z ,
> $$
> likewise for the other pairs; enclosed charge $\rho\,\Delta x\Delta y\Delta z$. Divide by the volume and shrink: the key result above. Tiling a finite volume with such boxes gives the [[concepts/divergence-theorem|Divergence theorem]].

> [!intuition] "Variation along the flow"
> For a unidirectional field $\mathbf{F} = F(x,y,z)\,\hat{u}$, $\hat{u}$ fixed, divergence is nonzero exactly when $F$ changes *along* $\hat{u}$: $+x$ arrows growing with $x$ (more leaving the right face than entering the left). Change only *across* the direction gives zero divergence (the instructor's rule; that is [[concepts/curl|curl]]). When lines spread, converge or curve, geometry contributes too: the Coulomb field $Q\hat{r}/r^2$ weakens along its direction yet has *zero* divergence off the charge (the spreading compensates — all of it is concentrated in a δ at the origin). When in doubt, use the formula.

The same two fields give opposite answers to the two questions "source-like?" and "whirlpool-like?". Practise calling an arrow plot one or the other without computing.

**Examples.**
- Slides: $\mathbf{D} = 5x\,\hat{x} + 12\,\hat{y}$ C/m²: $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{D} = 5 + 0 + 0 = 5$ C/m³ — uniform charge filling all space, a "faucet of strength 5" (the constant $\hat{y}$ part adds nothing).
- The pn-junction field of [[concepts/symmetric-charge-distributions|the building-block page]], $E_x = -\rho_1(x+W_1)/\epsilon_0$ on $(-W_1,0)$ and $\rho_2(x-W_2)/\epsilon_0$ on $(0,W_2)$: $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{E} = \partial E_x/\partial x = -\rho_1/\epsilon_0$ and $+\rho_2/\epsilon_0$ respectively, zero outside: differentiating returns the charge that produced it — Gauss's law checked.
- $\mathbf{E} = E_0(y^2\hat{x}+2xy\hat{y}+3z^2\hat{z})$: $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{E} = E_0(0+2x+6z)$, so $\rho = \epsilon_0E_0(2x+6z)$ — only the "diagonal" derivatives $\partial_xE_x,\partial_yE_y,\partial_zE_z$ enter; the $y^2$ in $E_x$ is irrelevant.

> [!trap]
> - Differentiate each component with respect to **its own** coordinate only.
> - Don't forget $\epsilon_0$ (or $\epsilon$) when converting $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{E}$ to $\rho$.
> - "Curl-free" does **not** imply "divergence-free": a field can be conservative and still have charge as its source (the [[problems/curl-potential-and-charge-from-a-field|worked problem]] has both).

> [!exam] On exams
> FA26 problem 1(c): $\rho = \epsilon_0\nabla\cdot\mathbf{E}$ of a polynomial field. Tested every time: that "curl-free" says nothing about whether $\rho$ is zero; units (F/m)(V/m)/m = C/m³.
