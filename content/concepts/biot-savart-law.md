> [!key] Biot–Savart law
> $$
> \begin{gathered}
> d\mathbf{B} = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\,\frac{I\,d\mathbf{l}\times\hat{R}}{R^2},\\[4pt]
> \mathbf{B} = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\oint\frac{I\,d\mathbf{l}\times\hat R}{R^2}\quad\Big(\text{or }\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\int\frac{\mathbf{J}\times\hat R}{R^2}dV'\Big),
> \end{gathered}
> $$
> $\mathbf{R} = R\hat R$ points **from the current element to the field point**. Coulomb's law with $\rho\,dV\to I\,d\mathbf{l}$ and a cross product making $d\mathbf{B}$ perpendicular to the element and to the line to the field point. Valid for elements of steady (or quasi-static), closed current loops: an isolated element is not a possible steady current, and only the full loop integral is physical.

**Physics.** By superposition, a wire's field is the sum over its pieces; Biot–Savart is the rule for one piece that reproduces $\mu_0I/(2\pi r)$ for the long wire ([[concepts/magnetic-field|Magnetic field: B and H]]). It is the magnetic [[concepts/coulombs-law|Coulomb's law]]: $1/R^2$, superposition, and the same [[concepts/five-step-recipe|The 5-step recipe for fields of charge distributions]] — draw, divide into elements, write $d\mathbf{B}$, use symmetry to discard components that cancel, integrate. The difference is the cross product: the field *circles* its source instead of pointing away from it. For problems with symmetry, [[concepts/amperes-law|Ampère's law]] gets there faster; Biot–Savart is for everything else (finite segments, loops off-axis, coils), and it is the law that the [[concepts/vector-potential|Vector potential]] integral reproduces.

**Example — the straight wire by the five steps (slides, Lecture 12).** With $d\mathbf{B}$ for $d\mathbf{E}$: *draw* (3-D, side view through the field point, top view); *divide* into elements $I\,dz\,\hat z$; *write* $d\mathbf{B}$, with $\lvert d\mathbf{l}\times\hat R\rvert = dz\cos\alpha = dz\,r/R$, $R = \sqrt{r^2+z^2}$; *use symmetry* — every $d\mathbf{B}$ points along $\hat\phi$, so nothing cancels; *integrate*:

$$
B_\phi = \frac{\mu_0Ir}{4\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{dz}{(r^2+z^2)^{3/2}} = \frac{\mu_0Ir}{4\pi}\cdot\frac{2}{r^2} = \frac{\mu_0I}{2\pi r}\quad\checkmark
$$

(the line-charge integral of [[1-electrostatics/02-coulombs-law-superposition-and-gauss|Lecture 2]]; antiderivative $z/(r^2\sqrt{r^2+z^2})$). Treating the whole wire as one element and "plugging in the formula" is wrong — the slides say so in boldface; it is Coulomb's point-charge law applied to a line charge.

**Example — a semi-infinite wire** (site's own). Ending at the origin and running along $+z$: in the plane $z = 0$ at distance $a$, only half the straight-wire integral survives, $H_\phi = I/(4\pi a)$ — half the infinite-wire value. That is the number that [[3-maxwell-and-waves/16-charge-conservation-displacement-current-and-maxwells-equations|Lecture 16]]'s draining-charge example recovers from Ampère–Maxwell.

**Example — a loop.** On the axis of a circular loop of radius $a$, $B_z = \mu_0Ia^2/[2(a^2+z^2)^{3/2}]$; off the axis the integrals are elliptic ([[concepts/magnetic-dipole|The current loop and the magnetic dipole]]).

> [!recipe] The vector bookkeeping of Biot–Savart
> Order matters: $d\mathbf{l}\times\hat R$, not $\hat R\times d\mathbf{l}$. $\hat R$ runs from the source element to the field point (the slides' $\hat a_{21}$, "from 2 to 1"). The force between two circuits is a double integral, $\mathbf{F}_1 = \oint_1I_1d\mathbf{l}_1\times\mathbf{B}_2$; the forces $d\mathbf{F}_1$, $d\mathbf{F}_2$ between two *elements* are not equal and opposite — only the totals over closed loops are.

> [!trap]
> - Order: $d\mathbf{l}\times\hat R$, never $\hat R\times d\mathbf{l}$; and $\hat R$ from *source* to *field point*. Both slips flip the sign.
> - You cannot treat a whole wire as one element and "plug in" — integrate.
> - The same letter $r$ is the cylindrical radius in $\mu_0I/(2\pi r)$ and, in the notes, the distance $\lvert\mathbf{r}\rvert$ in $d\mathbf{B}$; this site writes $R$ for the latter.

> [!exam] On exams
> Finite segments and polygon loops are the Biot–Savart questions (the centre of a square loop; a wire with a gap). Set up $d\mathbf{l}\times\hat R$ with a sketch, state which components cancel by symmetry, and quote $\int dz/(r^2+z^2)^{3/2} = 2/r^2$ (or its finite-limit form). Anything with planar or cylindrical symmetry belongs to Ampère instead.
