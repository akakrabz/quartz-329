> [!key] Conductance, and its relation to capacitance
> $$
> I = GV,\qquad G = \frac{\sigma A}{d}\ (\text{plates}),\qquad R = \frac1G = \frac{d}{\sigma A},\qquad\boxed{\ G = \frac{\sigma}{\epsilon}\,C\ }.
> $$
> $I = \displaystyle\int\mathbf{J}\cdot d\mathbf{S}$ is the current through any surface separating the two conductors, $\mathbf{J} = \sigma\mathbf{E}$. $G$ [S] and $C$ [F] share one **geometric factor** ($A/d$ for plates, $2\pi L/\ln(b/a)$ for a coax, $4\pi ab/(b-a)$ for spheres): $C = \epsilon\times$(factor), $G = \sigma\times$(factor). So $G = (\sigma/\epsilon)C$ for *any* geometry filled with one homogeneous medium — not for layered fillings unless every layer has the same $\sigma/\epsilon$.

**Physics.** Give a capacitor's filling a small conductivity $\sigma$ too. The same $\mathbf{E}$ that stores the charge drives $\mathbf{J} = \sigma\mathbf{E}$ from the positive conductor to the negative one. For plates, $J = \sigma V/d$ and $I = JA = \dfrac{\sigma A}{d}V$ — the box above.

> [!recipe] Conductance directly
> $V\to\mathbf{E} = -\nabla V\to\mathbf{J} = \sigma\mathbf{E}\to I = \int\mathbf{J}\cdot d\mathbf{S}$ through any surface separating the conductors $\to G = I/V$ (and $\mathcal{G} = G/L$ per unit length).
>
> *Coax:* $\mathbf{J} = \dfrac{\sigma V_0}{\ln(b/a)}\dfrac1r\hat{r}$; through a cylinder of radius $r$, $I = J_r\cdot2\pi rL = \dfrac{2\pi\sigma LV_0}{\ln(b/a)}$, the same for every $r$ ($\nabla\cdot\mathbf{J} = 0$, steady current). So $G = \dfrac{2\pi\sigma L}{\ln(b/a)}$, $\mathcal{G} = \dfrac{2\pi\sigma}{\ln(b/a)}$ [S/m]. *Spheres:* shells in series, $dR = \dfrac{dr}{4\pi\sigma r^2}$, so $R = \dfrac{1}{4\pi\sigma}\Big(\dfrac1a - \dfrac1b\Big)$ and $G = 4\pi\sigma\dfrac{ab}{b-a} = \dfrac{\sigma}{\epsilon}C$ ✓.

**The circuit model.** Charge on the positive conductor grows with the external current and shrinks with the leakage: $\dfrac{dQ}{dt} = C\dfrac{dV}{dt} = I - GV$, i.e.

$$
I = C\frac{dV}{dt} + GV:
$$

a capacitance $C$ in **parallel** with $R = 1/G$. $G$ carries the conduction current $\sigma\mathbf{E}$, $C$ the displacement current $\partial\mathbf{D}/\partial t$ ([[concepts/conductivity-and-susceptibility-models|Lorentz–Drude models of σ and χe]]). With no external circuit ($I=0$), $C\,dV/dt = -GV$, so

> [!note] Self-discharge and the relaxation time
> $$
> Q(t) = Q(0)\,e^{-t/\tau},\qquad \tau = RC = \frac{C}{G} = \frac{\epsilon}{\sigma},
> $$
> the relaxation time that empties a [[concepts/conductors|conductor's]] interior, likewise independent of geometry. Driven sinusoidally, the element is resistor-like for $\omega\ll\sigma/\epsilon$ and capacitor-like for $\omega\gg\sigma/\epsilon$: a good insulator has $\sigma/\epsilon$ far below every frequency you care about ([[concepts/loss-tangent|Loss tangent]]).

**Examples.**

> [!example] Example — concentric spheres in a lossy medium (HW4 #4)
> Shells of radii $a = 1$ m and $b\to\infty$, medium $\epsilon = 2\epsilon_0$, $\sigma = 2\times10^{-6}$ S/m. $C = 4\pi\epsilon a = 8\pi\epsilon_0 \approx 222$ pF; $G = (\sigma/\epsilon)C = 8\pi\times10^{-6}\ \text{S} \approx 25\ \mu$S (shell-by-shell $\int dR$ agrees); $\tau = \epsilon/\sigma = 8.85\ \mu$s. With $Q(0) = 1$ C on the inner sphere and nothing connected, $Q(t) = e^{-t/(8.85\,\mu\text{s})}$ C: the charge flows outward through the medium, which *is* the return path.

> [!trap]
> - $G = (\sigma/\epsilon)C$ fails for layered fillings unless every layer has the same $\sigma/\epsilon$; then compute $G$ by the recipe.
> - $\sigma$ is conductivity here; surface charge is $\rho_s$.
> - $R = 1/G$ is the *leakage* resistance across the dielectric, not the resistance of the conductors.
> - $\tau = \epsilon/\sigma$ uses the filling's $\epsilon$ and $\sigma$, not $\epsilon_0$.

> [!exam] On exams
> The review formula sheet lists $Q = CV$, $G = (\sigma/\epsilon)C$ and $R = 1/G$ together — know why the middle one is true (one geometric factor). For a lossy capacitor: $C$ by the chain of [[concepts/capacitance|Capacitance]], $G$ by the ratio or by $V\to\mathbf{E}\to\mathbf{J}\to I$, then $\tau = C/G = \epsilon/\sigma$; per unit length for a coax, $\mathcal{G}$ in S/m.
