> [!key] Ohm's law (the only Ohm's law this course uses), the two ideal materials, and the conductor in equilibrium
> $$
> \mathbf{J} = \sigma\,\mathbf{E}\qquad[\text{A/m}^2 = (\text{S/m})(\text{V/m})].
> $$
> $\sigma$ [S/m] is the **conductivity**. For a block of length $l$, cross-section $A$: $V = El$, $I = JA$, so $R = V/I = l/(\sigma A)$. Resistivity is $1/\sigma$ [Ω·m].
>
> | | limit | consequence | circuit analogy |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | **perfect electric conductor (PEC)** | $\sigma\to\infty$ | $\mathbf{E} = 0$ inside, whatever $\mathbf{J}$ flows | short circuit: $V = 0$ for any $I$ |
> | **perfect dielectric (insulator)** | $\sigma\to0$ | $\mathbf{J} = 0$ inside, whatever $\mathbf{E}$ is applied | open circuit: $I = 0$ for any $V$ |
>
> **A conductor in electrostatic equilibrium:**
> 1. $\mathbf{E} = 0$ (and $\mathbf{D} = 0$) everywhere inside.
> 2. $V$ is constant: the whole body, surface included, is one **equipotential** ($V(b) - V(a) = -\int_a^b\mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{l} = 0$ for any two of its points).
> 3. $\rho = \nabla\cdot\mathbf{D} = 0$ inside: **all net charge lives on the surface**, as $\rho_s$.
> 4. At the surface $\hat{n}\cdot\mathbf{D} = \rho_s$ and $\hat{n}\times\mathbf{E} = 0$, $\hat{n}$ **out of the conductor**: the field just outside is **perpendicular** to the surface, and its normal $\mathbf{D}$ *is* the local surface charge.
>
> Inside a PEC the course also takes $\mathbf{H} = \mathbf{B} = 0$ (why: below).

**Physics.** Carriers keep colliding with the lattice, so the drift velocity, and $\mathbf{J}$, settles proportional to $\mathbf{E}$ — the Drude model of [[concepts/conductivity-and-susceptibility-models|Lorentz–Drude models of σ and χe]]. Room-temperature conductivities span over twenty orders of magnitude: copper $\approx 6\times10^{7}$ S/m, sea water $\approx 4$, germanium $\approx 2$, distilled water $\sim10^{-4}$, glass $\sim10^{-12}$, fused quartz $\sim10^{-17}$; superconductors effectively $\infty$. The table above idealizes the two ends.

A PEC has $\mathbf{E} = 0$ inside by definition; a *real* conductor reaches the same state in equilibrium, quickly. In a uniform field $\mathbf{E}_0$, a conducting slab's free electrons ($q<0$) drift *against* the field, piling up on one face and leaving the other positive. The neutral slab's induced pair $\pm\rho_s$ makes a **secondary field** $-\mathbf{E}_0$ inside; charge moves until the total interior field is zero. The four rules in the box follow.

> [!derivation]- How fast does a conductor reach equilibrium?
> Combine charge conservation $\partial\rho/\partial t + \nabla\cdot\mathbf{J} = 0$ ([[concepts/continuity-equation|Continuity equation (charge conservation)]]), Ohm's law $\mathbf{J} = \sigma\mathbf{E}$, and Gauss's law $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{E} = \rho/\epsilon$ in a homogeneous conductor:
> $$
> \frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t} + \sigma\nabla\cdot\mathbf{E} = \frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t} + \frac{\sigma}{\epsilon}\rho = 0
> \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
> \rho(t) = \rho(0)\,e^{-t/\tau},\qquad \tau = \frac{\epsilon}{\sigma} .
> $$
> Interior charge decays with the **relaxation time** $\tau = \epsilon/\sigma$, migrating to the surface. With no current driven by an external circuit, $\mathbf{J} = 0$ and so $\mathbf{E} = 0$ inside. (A wire with steady current: $\rho = 0$ inside but $\mathbf{E} = \mathbf{J}/\sigma\neq0$.) Copper, $\epsilon\approx\epsilon_0$: $\tau\sim10^{-19}$ s — shorter than the model's validity, but an isolated metal is in equilibrium for every field in this course. Sea water ($\epsilon\approx81\epsilon_0$, $\sigma\approx4$ S/m): $\tau\approx0.2$ ns. The same $\tau$ returns as a leaky capacitor's $RC = \epsilon/\sigma$ ([[concepts/conductance|Conductance and the leaky capacitor]]).

**Perfect conductor in time-varying fields** ([[3-maxwell-and-waves/16-charge-conservation-displacement-current-and-maxwells-equations#4-boundary-conditions--same-for-dynamics|Lecture 16 §4]]). Medium 2 a PEC, $\sigma\to\infty$, $\mathbf{E} = 0$ inside. Faraday inside gives $\partial\mathbf{B}/\partial t = -\nabla\times\mathbf{E} = 0$, so no *time-varying* $\mathbf{B}$ exists in it either — the class's reason for setting $\mathbf{H} = 0$ inside (a static $\mathbf{B}$ frozen in is the exception; the course never uses one). With the [[concepts/boundary-conditions|four boundary conditions]] and $\hat n$ out of the metal into medium 1:

> [!note] Fields just outside a perfect conductor ($\hat n$ out of the metal)
> $$
> \hat n\cdot\mathbf{D}_1 = \rho_s,\qquad \hat n\times\mathbf{E}_1 = 0,\qquad \hat n\cdot\mathbf{B}_1 = 0,\qquad \hat n\times\mathbf{H}_1 = \mathbf{J}_s\ \ (\text{equivalently } \mathbf{H}_{1t} = \mathbf{J}_s\times\hat n).
> $$
> $E_{1t} = 0$ and $B_{1n} = 0$: $\mathbf{E}$ meets the surface *normally* and ends on $\rho_s = \hat n\cdot\mathbf{D}_1$; $\mathbf{H}$ runs *tangentially*, carried by the surface current $\mathbf{J}_s = \hat n\times\mathbf{H}_1$. An oblique arrow just outside is neither $\mathbf{E}$ nor $\mathbf{H}$; a tangential one can only be $\mathbf{H}$, a normal one only $\mathbf{E}$ (the slide's picture quiz, answer (e)). A typed slide's "$D_{1n} = \rho$" means $\rho_s$.

**What drives the current in a wire?** $\mathbf{E} = 0$ inside a conductor *in equilibrium*; yet $\mathbf{E} = \mathbf{J}/\sigma\neq0$ inside a wire *carrying a current*. The notes' footnote (Jefimenko 1962, Parker 1970, *Am. J. Phys.*) reconciles them: the field inside a resistive wire is an **electrostatic field produced by surface charges on the wire itself**, graded along its length — denser near one terminal, sparser near the other. They arrange themselves, on the relaxation time scale, so that $\mathbf{E}$ inside follows every bend with magnitude exactly $J/\sigma$. Outside, the same charges make a field with a large radial component (demonstrated with suspended fibres, like iron filings for a magnetic field). An ideal zero-resistivity wire needs no such field or charge — the idealization [[2-magnetostatics/12-magnetic-force-biot-savart-and-amperes-law|Lecture 12]] starts from when it calls a current-carrying wire neutral.

**Examples.**

> [!example] Example — a neutral conducting slab between two charged sheets
> Sheets $\rho_A>0$ (above) and $\rho_B>0$ (below) sandwich an uncharged conducting slab. Find the induced charges $\rho_1$ (top face) and $\rho_2$ (bottom face).
>
> The neutral slab's induced charges are equal and opposite, so *outside the slab* their fields cancel: each sheet gives $\rho_s/(2\epsilon_0)$ away from itself, and in the gaps $\mathbf{D} = \tfrac12(\rho_B - \rho_A)\,\hat{z}$ ($\hat{z}$ up). Inside the slab $\mathbf{D} = 0$. Read the faces with $\hat{n}\cdot\mathbf{D} = \rho_s$, $\hat{n}$ out of the metal:
> - top face, $\hat{n} = +\hat{z}$: $\rho_1 = \tfrac12(\rho_B - \rho_A)$;
> - bottom face, $\hat{n} = -\hat{z}$: $\rho_2 = \tfrac12(\rho_A - \rho_B) = -\rho_1$. ✓
>
> Check: inside the slab the four sheets give $\tfrac{1}{2\epsilon_0}(-\rho_A - \rho_1 + \rho_2 + \rho_B) = 0$ ✓; $\rho_A = \rho_B$ induces nothing, as symmetry demands.

> [!example] Example — a point charge above a grounded conducting plane (concept check)
> Positive $Q$ above an infinite grounded conducting plane. *E is zero above the plane* — no. *E is zero below* — **yes**: the plane shields completely. *Induced charge uniform* — no: $\rho_s = \hat{n}\cdot\mathbf{D}$ follows the field, strongest directly under $Q$. *Total induced charge $+Q$* — no: all of $Q$'s lines end on the plane (none pass through), on negative charge, so the total is $-Q$. *Tangential field at the plane* — no: $\hat{n}\times\mathbf{E} = 0$, every line meets the plane at a right angle.

More, in short: a coax's $+\lambda$ per unit length on the inner conductor forces $-\lambda$ onto the inner surface of the outer one ([[problems/two-layer-coaxial-capacitor|Worked problem — Two-layer coaxial capacitor]]); salt water between copper plates lets ions drift until the interior field is zero — the capacitor is shorted ([[concepts/permittivity|HW4 #5]]). PEC surfaces (Lecture 16 slide exercises, worked on [[concepts/boundary-conditions|Boundary conditions at an interface]]): $\mathbf{D} = D_0\langle1,-2,2\rangle$ pointing away from a conductor's surface gives $\rho_s = \lvert\mathbf{D}\rvert = 3D_0$; $\mathbf{D} = D_0\langle1,0,\sqrt3\rangle$ pointing toward it gives $\rho_s = -\lvert\mathbf{D}\rvert = -2D_0$. A radiating current sheet backed by a PEC, and the PEC mirror of Lecture 26 ($\mathbf{J}_s = \hat n\times\mathbf{H}$ on the mirror): the same two conditions at work ([[concepts/current-sheet-radiation|Radiation from a current sheet]], [[concepts/standing-waves|Standing waves]]).

> [!trap]
> - Zero *net* charge does not mean zero *surface* charge, and a grounded or connected conductor need not be neutral at all.
> - Induced charge is not uniform: $\rho_s = \hat{n}\cdot\mathbf{D}$ follows the field.
> - $\sigma$ is conductivity here, never surface charge ($\rho_s$).
> - A real conductor's $\mathbf{E} = 0$ is a *steady state*: after $\tau$, with no current driven through it.
> - The Drude collision time ($\sim10^{-14}$ s) and the relaxation time $\epsilon/\sigma$ ($\sim10^{-19}$ s for copper) are both written $\tau$.
> - "$\mathbf{H} = 0$ inside a PEC" is a consequence of Faraday's law for *time-varying* fields plus the course convention, not a property of $\sigma$ alone.
> - $\mathbf{J}_s = \hat n\times\mathbf{H}_1$, not $\mathbf{H}_1\times\hat n$: check with the Lecture 13 sheet, $\mathbf{H} = \tfrac12\mathbf{J}_s\times\hat n$ on each side.

> [!exam] On exams
> FA26 4(c) tests the conductor rule: a coax's outer conductor carries $-\lambda$ on its inner surface because the field inside the metal vanishes — "conductors are neutral, so 0" is the wrong reflex. Read every plate or shell charge with $\rho_s = \hat{n}\cdot\mathbf{D}$, $\hat{n}$ out of the metal ([[problems/two-layer-coaxial-capacitor|Worked problem — Two-layer coaxial capacitor]]). PEC cases of the boundary conditions: draw $\hat n$ first, then $\rho_s = \hat n\cdot\mathbf{D}_1$, $\mathbf{J}_s = \hat n\times\mathbf{H}_1$; know which field pictures are possible outside a conductor.
