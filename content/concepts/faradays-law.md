> [!key] Faraday's law
> $$
> \nabla\times\mathbf{E} = -\frac{\partial\mathbf{B}}{\partial t}\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \oint_C\mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{l} = -\int_S\frac{\partial\mathbf{B}}{\partial t}\cdot d\mathbf{S},
> $$
> for every surface $S$ bounded by every closed, **directed** path $C$, $d\mathbf{S}$ tied to the sense of $C$ by the right-hand rule (the Stokes convention of [[1-electrostatics/06-circulation-and-boundary-conditions|Lecture 6]]). It holds at all points, in all frames. Field definitions unchanged: $\mathbf{E}$ is force per unit *stationary* charge, $\mathbf{B}$ gives the extra $\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B}$ per unit moving charge, in whatever frame you use ([[concepts/lorentz-force|Lorentz force]]). For a loop moving or deforming with velocity $\mathbf{v}$,
> $$
> \mathcal{E}\equiv\oint_C(\mathbf{E}+\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B})\cdot d\mathbf{l} = -\frac{d\Psi}{dt},\qquad \Psi = \int_S\mathbf{B}\cdot d\mathbf{S},
> $$
> the **flux rule**, however the flux change is produced ([[concepts/electromotive-force|Electromotive force (emf) and path-dependent voltage]] derives it).

**Physics.** The first curl equation with a time derivative, and the end of path-independent voltage: $\mathbf{E}$ gains a circulating part ($-\partial\mathbf{A}/\partial t$, with $\mathbf{A}$ the [[concepts/vector-potential|Vector potential]]). The right side is built from the **magnetic flux linking $C$**,

$$
\Psi\equiv\int_S\mathbf{B}\cdot d\mathbf{S}\quad[\text{Wb} = \text{T·m}^2],
$$

the same for every surface spanning $C$ because $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B} = 0$ (two such surfaces form a closed surface with zero net flux — [[concepts/magnetic-flux|Magnetic flux Ψ]]). The contributing field lines thread the loop like links of a chain, hence "linking". For a loop **fixed** in the measurement frame, $d/dt$ comes outside the integral:

$$
\oint_C\mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{l} = -\frac{d}{dt}\int_S\mathbf{B}\cdot d\mathbf{S} = -\frac{d\Psi}{dt}.
$$

Where does the induced $\mathbf{E}$ come from? The notes' first footnote: extending the relativistic argument of [[concepts/magnetic-field|Lecture 12]] to *time-varying* motion, Coulomb interactions require both a time-varying $\mathbf{B}$ and a time-varying $\mathbf{E}$. Faraday's law relates the two fields; the source of both is the changing **current** $\mathbf{J}$, not the changing $\mathbf{B}$ as such.

**Lenz's rule, and the three ways to change Ψ.** The minus sign makes the induced current's field **oppose the change** in flux that made it (the opposite sign would make induced currents grow without bound). $-d\Psi/dt\ne0$ whenever

1. $C$ is fixed but $\mathbf{B}$ varies in time (transformer action; the emf is all $\oint\mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{l}$);
2. $\mathbf{B}$ is constant but $C$ changes — moves, rotates, changes size (generator action; the emf is all $\oint(\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B})\cdot d\mathbf{l}$);
3. $\mathbf{B}$ is static but *inhomogeneous* and $C$ moves through it (also motional — a loop *translating rigidly* through a *uniform* static field links a constant flux and has no emf).

Case 2 or 3 seen by an observer riding with the loop is case 1: she sees a time-varying $\mathbf{B}'$ and an electric field $\mathbf{E}' = \mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B}$. Same emf, two descriptions — the (near) invariance of the Lorentz force between slowly moving frames.

**In a wave.** Faraday's law is half of the E–H recursion that makes a wave; the displacement current is the other. For $\mathbf{E} = \hat xE_x(z,t)$ it reads $\partial E_x/\partial z = -\mu\,\partial H_y/\partial t$: where $\mathbf{E}$ varies along the travel direction, $\mathbf{H}$ changes in time. Integrated in time, it gives [[3-maxwell-and-waves/18-the-wave-equation-and-plane-tem-waves|Lecture 18]] the $\mathbf{H}$ of a given $\mathbf{E}$: $\mathbf{H} = \pm\hat y\,f(t\mp z/v)/\eta$ ([[concepts/plane-waves|Plane waves]]).

**Examples** (course notes, Lecture 14, Examples 1–3; the motional ones, 4–6, are on [[concepts/motional-emf|Motional emf and the voltmeter paradox]]).

> [!example] Example 1 — a loop in a decaying field
> $\mathbf{B} = B_0e^{-t/\tau}\hat z$; stationary circle of radius $r = 10$ m in $z = 0$, counter-clockwise seen from $+z$, so $d\mathbf{S} = \hat z\,dS$, $\Psi = B_0e^{-t/\tau}\cdot\pi10^2$ Wb and
> $$
> \mathcal{E} = -\frac{d\Psi}{dt} = \pi\,10^2\,\frac{B_0}{\tau}\,e^{-t/\tau}\ \text{V} > 0 .
> $$
> $I = \mathcal{E}/R$ flows counter-clockwise and makes a $+\hat z$ field inside that *props up* the decaying $B_z$. Lenz ✓.

> [!example] Example 2 — a loop receding from a line current, both ways
> Current $I$ along the $x$ axis makes $\mathbf{B} = \dfrac{\mu_0I}{2\pi r}\hat\phi$, which on the $xy$-plane at $y>0$ is $\dfrac{\mu_0I}{2\pi y}\hat z$ ($r$, $\hat\phi$ cylindrical *about the $x$ axis*). A 2 m × 2 m loop, edges along $x$ and $y$, has its centre at $y = 2t$ m ($\mathbf{v} = 2\hat y$ m/s; valid for $t>\tfrac12$ s, loop entirely above the wire). Counter-clockwise $C$, $d\mathbf{S} = \hat z\,dx\,dy$:
> $$
> \begin{gathered}
> \Psi = \int_{-1}^{1}dx\int_{2t-1}^{2t+1}\frac{\mu_0I}{2\pi y}dy = \frac{\mu_0I}{\pi}\ln\frac{2t+1}{2t-1},\\[6pt]
> \mathcal{E} = -\frac{d\Psi}{dt} = \frac{\mu_0I}{\pi}\cdot\frac{4}{(2t+1)(2t-1)} = \frac{\mu_0I}{\pi\,(t^2-\tfrac14)} .
> \end{gathered}
> $$
> Motional route: $\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B} = 2\cdot\dfrac{\mu_0I}{2\pi y}\hat x$, so only the edges parallel to $x$ contribute — near edge ($y = 2t-1$, traversed $+\hat x$, length 2) and far edge ($y = 2t+1$, traversed $-\hat x$):
> $$
> \mathcal{E} = \frac{2\mu_0I}{2\pi(2t-1)}\cdot2 - \frac{2\mu_0I}{2\pi(2t+1)}\cdot2 = \frac{\mu_0I}{\pi(t^2-\tfrac14)}\quad\checkmark
> $$
> Lenz: the $+\hat z$ flux falls; the counter-clockwise current's $+\hat z$ field opposes the decrease. [[problems/emf-of-a-loop-moving-near-a-line-current|Worked problem — EMF of a loop moving away from a line current]] re-parameterizes this one.

> [!example] Example 3 — a generator
> A loop of radius $0.1$ m rotates about the $x$ axis at $f = 60$ Hz in constant $\mathbf{B} = 10\hat z$ T; $R = 12\ \Omega$. The flux peaks, $\Psi_0 = \pi(0.1)^2\cdot10 = 0.1\pi$ Wb, with the loop in $z = 0$; $\Psi(t) = \Psi_0\cos\omega t$, $\omega = 120\pi$ rad/s. $\mathcal{E} = -d\Psi/dt = 120\pi\cdot0.1\pi\sin(120\pi t) = 12\pi^2\sin(120\pi t)$ V, $I = \mathcal{E}/R = \pi^2\sin(120\pi t)$ A, about 10 A peak. Case 2 — constant $\mathbf{B}$, moving $C$ — the power grid in one line. (Loop self-inductance neglected; [[concepts/inductance|Inductance]] says when that is allowed: $L\,dI/dt\sim$ millivolts here against 118 V.)

*Slides.* Lenz demos — a magnet approaching a coil, a copper ring jumping off a switched-on coil, a shrinking loop in a uniform field (flux falls; the induced current's field props it up) — and the disk-vs-bowl surfaces spanning one loop: same $\Psi$.

> [!trap]
> - Orient $C$ first; $d\mathbf{S}$ follows, and the sign of $\mathcal{E}$ is relative to it. "Counter-clockwise" needs a viewpoint ("seen from $+z$").
> - Moving loop: $-d\Psi/dt$ with the *total* derivative; $-\int\partial_t\mathbf{B}\cdot d\mathbf{S}$ is only the transformer part.
> - $I = \mathcal{E}/R$ neglects the self-emf $-L\,dI/dt$ ([[concepts/inductance|Inductance]]); fine when $L\,dI/dt\ll\mathcal{E}$.
> - $\mathcal{E}$ is work per unit charge, in volts, not a force.
> - Perfectly conducting parts of a loop add nothing to $\oint\mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{l}$; resistors and moving conductors carry it.

> [!exam] On exams
> (1) *Compute an emf*: orient $C$, $d\mathbf{S}$ by the right-hand rule, write $\Psi(t)$, differentiate, state the sign and current direction — for a moving loop, check by $\oint(\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B})\cdot d\mathbf{l}$ (Example 2 and the rotating loop are the templates). (2) *Direction only*: Lenz — magnet approaching, loop shrinking, field decaying? Say what the flux does and which field would oppose it. Points are lost for $d\mathbf{S}$ the wrong way (sign) and for calling $\mathcal{E}$ a force.
