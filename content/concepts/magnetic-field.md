> [!key] Definition
> $\mathbf{B}$ is defined by the velocity-dependent part of the force on a charge,
> $$
> d\mathbf{F} = I\,d\mathbf{l}\times\mathbf{B},\qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad \mathbf{F} = q\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B}\quad\text{on a charge }q\text{ moving at }\mathbf{v},
> $$
> because a current element is moving charge: $I\,d\mathbf{l} = (\text{charge per length}\cdot v)\,d\mathbf{l} = q\mathbf{v}$ summed over the carriers in $d\mathbf{l}$. $\mathbf{B}$ is the **magnetic flux density**, in tesla: $\text{T} = \text{N/(A·m)} = \text{Wb/m}^2$. This completes the Lorentz force $\mathbf{F} = q(\mathbf{E}+\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B})$ of [[1-electrostatics/01-fields-forces-and-the-maxwell-roadmap|Lecture 1]] ([[concepts/lorentz-force|Lorentz force]]). $\mathbf{H} = \mathbf{B}/\mu_0$ in free space, $\mathbf{B} = \mu\mathbf{H}$ in a medium, in A/m: its line integral around a loop is the current through it. $\mu_0 = 4\pi\times10^{-7}$ H/m; $\mu = (1+\chi_m)\mu_0 = \mu_r\mu_0$ ([[concepts/permeability|Permeability, susceptibility, and B = μH]]). Boundary conditions pair $\mathbf{B}\leftrightarrow\mathbf{D}$ (normal components jump only by surface charge: $\rho_s$ for $\mathbf{D}$, never for $\mathbf{B}$) and $\mathbf{H}\leftrightarrow\mathbf{E}$ (tangential components jump only by surface current: $\mathbf{J}_s$ for $\mathbf{H}$, never for $\mathbf{E}$).

**Physics.** Current makes $\mathbf{B}$, and $\mathbf{B}$ acts only on moving charge. The force is perpendicular to $\mathbf{v}$, so static magnetic fields **do no work**: they bend trajectories (radius $mv/\lvert q\rvert B$) without changing speed. A *stationary* charge near a current-carrying wire feels nothing — the wire is neutral.

**The observation.** Ampère's 1820 experiment: currents in the same direction attract, opposite directions repel, force per unit length $\propto1/r$. The force on a short piece of wire 2 is proportional to $I_2\,d\mathbf{l}_2$ and to wire 1's current, and perpendicular to the wire — so it is a cross product with a field that wire 1 produces: the definition above.

**Where the force comes from: a charged wire, in the right frame** ([[2-magnetostatics/12-magnetic-force-biot-savart-and-amperes-law#2-where-the-force-comes-from-a-charged-wire-in-the-right-frame|Lecture 12 §2]]). Ideal wire: positive ions at rest, electrons drifting left at $v$, current $I$ to the right. In the lab $\lambda_+ = -\lambda_-$ and $I = v\lambda_+$ ($\lambda$ is the notes' symbol for line charge density, $\rho_l$ elsewhere on this site). A test charge $q$ moving *with* the electrons sees, in **its own frame**, the ions moving right at $v$ and the electrons at rest. Lorentz contraction (a moving ruler is shorter by $1/\gamma$, $\gamma = 1/\sqrt{1-v^2/c^2}$) acts on the two line charges differently:

> [!derivation]- The relativistic bookkeeping (course notes, footnote 3)
> - In the lab the *electrons* move, so their spacing is already contracted: the lab density $\lambda_-$ is $\gamma$ times their own-frame density $\lambda_-'$. Hence in the electron frame $\lambda_-' = \lambda_-/\gamma$ — sparser.
> - In the electron frame the *ions* move, so their spacing is contracted there: $\lambda_+' = \gamma\lambda_+$ — denser.
> - Net line charge seen by the test charge: $\lambda' = \gamma\lambda_+ + \lambda_-/\gamma = \lambda_+\big(\gamma - 1/\gamma\big) = \gamma\lambda_+\dfrac{v^2}{c^2}$, positive.
>
> With $\lambda_+ = I/v$: $\lambda' = \gamma I v/c^2 = \gamma Iv\,\mu_0\epsilon_0$, using $1/c^2 = \mu_0\epsilon_0$. The line-charge field of [[1-electrostatics/03-gauss-law-at-work|Lecture 3]] is then $\mathbf{E}' = \dfrac{\lambda'}{2\pi\epsilon_0r}\hat r = \dfrac{\gamma Iv\mu_0}{2\pi r}\hat r$, and the force on the (here stationary) test charge is $\mathbf{F}' = q\mathbf{E}'$. Transforming a transverse force back to the lab divides by $\gamma$: $\mathbf{F} = \mathbf{F}'/\gamma = q\dfrac{Iv\mu_0}{2\pi r}\hat r$ — the $\gamma$'s cancel exactly, so the result holds for any $v$.

The lab force is radial, $\propto qv$ and $I/r$. Writing it as $q\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B}$, $\mathbf{v}$ along the wire, $\mathbf{B}$ *around* it, identifies **the field of a long straight current**:

$$
\mathbf{B} = \frac{\mu_0I}{2\pi r}\,\hat\phi,\qquad \mu_0 = 4\pi\times10^{-7}\ \text{H/m},\qquad \mu_0\epsilon_0 = \frac1{c^2}.
$$

$\hat\phi$ by the **right-hand rule**: thumb along $I$, fingers curl along $\mathbf{B}$. The field lines are circles around the wire; they do not begin or end.

> [!intuition] Why such a tiny effect produces an everyday force
> The relative charge imbalance is $\lambda'/\lambda_+ = v^2/c^2$: for 1 A in a 1 mm² copper wire the drift speed is $7\times10^{-5}$ m/s, so $v^2/c^2\approx6\times10^{-26}$. But $\lambda_+$ is enormous — about $1.4\times10^4$ C/m, balanced to that precision by electrons. The product, $\lambda'\sim10^{-21}$ C/m, makes a field of order $10^{-9}$ V/m at a centimetre — exactly $vB$ with $B = \mu_0I/(2\pi r) = 2\times10^{-5}$ T. Magnetism is what is left of the Coulomb force when a nearly perfect cancellation is spoiled by relativity. (Copper numbers: the site's own.)

**Signs.** A positive test charge moving *with the electrons* (against $I$) is repelled (a positively charged wire repels it). One moving *along* $I$ is attracted — so the electrons of a second parallel wire, moving against $I_2$ but negative, are attracted when $I_2\parallel I_1$: the two-wire attraction. A real resistive wire also has a small lab-frame surface charge ([[1-electrostatics/11-lorentz-drude-models-for-conductivity-and-susceptibility#6-what-drives-the-current-in-a-wire-a-footnote-worth-a-section|Lecture 11 §6]]); the argument above is for the idealized neutral wire.

**In matter** ([[3-maxwell-and-waves/17-magnetization-and-maxwells-equations-in-matter|Lecture 17]]). In general $\mathbf{H}\equiv\mathbf{B}/\mu_0-\mathbf{M}$, with $\mathbf{M}$ the [[concepts/magnetization|Magnetization M and magnetization current]], built so that $\oint\mathbf{H}\cdot d\mathbf{l}$ counts *free* current only. Free currents set $\mathbf{H}$ (independently of the medium when symmetry lets Ampère's law decide alone: sheet, solenoid, coax), and the medium sets $\mathbf{B} = \mu\mathbf{H}$, as free charge sets $\mathbf{D}$ and the medium $\mathbf{E} = \mathbf{D}/\epsilon$. The force law uses $\mathbf{B}$, not $\mathbf{H}$.

**The two laws.** Static: $\nabla\times\mathbf{H} = \mathbf{J}$ ([[concepts/amperes-law|Ampère's law]]) and, always, $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B} = 0$ (no magnetic charge; field lines close). Any current's field follows from [[concepts/biot-savart-law|Biot–Savart law]] or the [[concepts/vector-potential|Vector potential]]. Dynamic: $\nabla\times\mathbf{H} = \mathbf{J}+\partial\mathbf{D}/\partial t$ ([[concepts/displacement-current|Displacement current]]).

**Canonical fields (free space).**

| source | field | where derived |
|---|---|---|
| long straight current $I$ | $\mathbf{B} = \dfrac{\mu_0I}{2\pi r}\hat\phi$ (right-hand rule) | above; [[concepts/biot-savart-law|Biot–Savart law]] |
| sheet $\mathbf{J}_s$ | $\mathbf{H} = \tfrac12\mathbf{J}_s\times\hat n$, $\hat n$ toward the field point; tangential, reversed across the sheet, distance-independent | [[concepts/current-sheets-and-slabs|Current sheets and slabs]] |
| slab $J_0$, thickness $W$ | $H_y = J_0x$ inside, $\pm J_0W/2$ outside | [[concepts/current-sheets-and-slabs|Current sheets and slabs]] |
| long solenoid, $n$ turns/m | $\mathbf{B} = \mu_0nI\hat z$ inside, 0 outside | [[concepts/solenoids-and-toroids|Solenoids and toroids]] |
| coax, uniform core | $H_\phi = Ir/(2\pi a^2)$, $I/(2\pi r)$, $0$ | [[concepts/amperes-law|Ampère's law]] |
| loop of radius $a$, on axis | $B_z = \dfrac{\mu_0Ia^2}{2(a^2+z^2)^{3/2}}$; far field $\propto1/r^3$ (dipole $m = I\pi a^2$) | [[concepts/magnetic-dipole|The current loop and the magnetic dipole]] |

> [!trap]
> - $\hat\phi$ follows the right-hand rule about the *current*, not the axes: a current along $-\hat x$ circulates $\mathbf{H}$ in $-\hat\phi$ about $+\hat x$.
> - $\mathbf{B}$ in tesla; $\mathbf{H}$ and $\mathbf{J}_s$ in A/m, because $H_t$ jumps by $J_s$.
> - "$\nabla\cdot\mathbf{H} = 0$" holds only where $\mu$ is uniform; the law is $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B} = 0$.
> - The force is on *moving* charge only; a static charge beside a neutral wire feels nothing, and $q\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B}$ never changes a speed.

> [!exam] On exams
> Direction questions — which way $\mathbf{B}$ points, which way the force on a charge or wire — are cheap points: use the right-hand rule and $q\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B}$ (or $I\,d\mathbf{l}\times\mathbf{B}$) explicitly, not by instinct. Give units: T for $\mathbf{B}$, A/m for $\mathbf{H}$. "Parallel currents attract" with the frame argument in two sentences is a standard short-answer item.
