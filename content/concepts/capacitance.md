> [!key] Definition and the three geometries
> For two conductors carrying $+Q$ and $-Q$, with potential difference $V = V_+ - V_-> 0$ between them,
> $$
> Q = CV,\qquad C = \frac{Q}{V}\quad[\text{F} = \text{C/V}].
> $$
> $C$ depends only on the shapes, the separation and the filling's $\epsilon$ — not on $Q$ or $V$ (linear media). $V$ is the **drop** from the positive conductor to the negative one, so $C>0$ always.
>
> | geometry | $C$ | condition |
> |---|---|---|
> | parallel plates, area $A$, gap $d$ | $\dfrac{\epsilon A}{d}$ | $d\ll\sqrt A$ |
> | coax, radii $a<b$, length $L$ | $\dfrac{2\pi\epsilon L}{\ln(b/a)}$; per unit length $\mathcal{C} \equiv \dfrac{C}{L} = \dfrac{2\pi\epsilon}{\ln(b/a)}$ [F/m] | $L\gg b$ |
> | concentric spheres $a<b$ | $4\pi\epsilon\dfrac{ab}{b-a}$; isolated sphere ($b\to\infty$) $4\pi\epsilon a$ | — |

> [!recipe] Two routes to C
> **Given $V$** (the conductors' potentials): solve [[concepts/poissons-equation|Laplace's equation]] with those boundary values → $\mathbf{E} = -\nabla V$ → $\mathbf{D} = \epsilon\mathbf{E}$ → $\rho_s = \hat{n}\cdot\mathbf{D}$ on **one** conductor ($\hat{n}$ out of the conductor) → $Q = \int\rho_s\,dA$ → $C = Q/V$.
>
> **Given $Q$**: Gauss's law (or sheet superposition) → $\mathbf{D}$ → $\mathbf{E} = \mathbf{D}/\epsilon$ → $V = -\int_-^+\mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{l}$ from the negative conductor to the positive one (a positive number) → $C = Q/V$.
>
> Both pass through the same field pattern and give the same $C$. On an exam the given-$Q$ route is usually shorter. Never from memory alone — from a chain.

**Physics.** $\pm Q$ on two conductors produces a potential difference $V$; electrostatics is linear, so $C = Q/V$ is a constant of the geometry and the dielectric. Two conditions. **No fringing:** the infinite-plate (or infinite-coax) field pattern needs $d\ll\sqrt{A}$; otherwise field lines bulge at the edges and $C$ is somewhat larger. **Quasi-static:** $Q=CV$ holds for a time-varying capacitor only if $\sqrt{A}\ll c/f$; a large structure driven fast is a transmission line, not a lumped element ([[4-transmission-lines/index|Unit 4]]). The per-unit-length $\mathcal{C}$ (script C) is the one transmission-line theory uses; it depends on the radii only through their ratio.

**Parallel plates, both ways.** Plates of area $A$ at $x=0$ (grounded) and $x=d$ (at $V_0$); dielectric $\epsilon$ between.

*Given $V$.* $V(x) = V_0x/d$ (Laplace), so $\mathbf{E} = -\dfrac{V_0}{d}\hat{x}$ and $\mathbf{D} = -\dfrac{\epsilon V_0}{d}\hat{x}$. At the positive plate ($x=d$) the normal into the gap is $-\hat{x}$: $\rho_s = (-\hat{x})\cdot\mathbf{D} = +\dfrac{\epsilon V_0}{d}$ [C/m²], so $Q = \rho_sA = \dfrac{\epsilon AV_0}{d}$ and

$$
C = \frac{\epsilon A}{d}\qquad(d\ll\sqrt{A}).
$$

*Given $Q$.* The plates carry $\pm Q/A$ on their facing surfaces only (the field vanishes inside each conductor and outside the pair). Between them $\mathbf{D} = -\dfrac{Q}{A}\hat{x}$ (from $+$ to $-$), $\mathbf{E} = -\dfrac{Q}{\epsilon A}\hat{x}$, and the drop is $V = V(d) - V(0) = -\int_0^dE_x\,dx = \dfrac{Qd}{\epsilon A}$. Again $C = \epsilon A/d$.

**The coaxial cable.** Inner conductor radius $a$, outer conductor inner radius $b$, dielectric $\epsilon$ between, length $L\gg b$, so $\mathbf{E} = E_r(r)\hat{r}$.

*Given $V$* ($V(a) = V_0$, $V(b) = 0$). Laplace in cylindrical coordinates: $\dfrac1r\dfrac{d}{dr}\Big(r\dfrac{dV}{dr}\Big) = 0 \Rightarrow r\dfrac{dV}{dr} = A \Rightarrow V = A\ln r + B$. The boundary values give

$$
V(r) = V_0\,\frac{\ln(b/r)}{\ln(b/a)},\qquad
\mathbf{E} = -\frac{dV}{dr}\hat{r} = \frac{V_0}{\ln(b/a)}\,\frac{1}{r}\,\hat{r},\qquad
\mathbf{D} = \frac{\epsilon V_0}{\ln(b/a)}\,\frac{1}{r}\,\hat{r}.
$$

On the inner conductor ($\hat{n} = \hat{r}$): $\rho_s(a) = \dfrac{\epsilon V_0}{a\ln(b/a)}$, so $Q = \rho_s\cdot2\pi aL = \dfrac{2\pi\epsilon LV_0}{\ln(b/a)}$. (Outer: $\rho_s(b) = -\hat{r}\cdot\mathbf{D}(b) = -\dfrac{\epsilon V_0}{b\ln(b/a)}$, times $2\pi bL$ gives $-Q$ ✓.)

*Given $Q$* (or $\lambda = Q/L$ — the exam's symbol for what this site otherwise calls $\rho_l$). Gauss on a cylinder $a<r<b$: $D_r\,2\pi rL = Q$, so $\mathbf{D} = \dfrac{\lambda}{2\pi r}\hat{r}$, $\mathbf{E} = \dfrac{\lambda}{2\pi\epsilon r}\hat{r}$, and the drop from inner to outer is $V = \displaystyle\int_a^b\frac{\lambda\,dr}{2\pi\epsilon r} = \frac{\lambda}{2\pi\epsilon}\ln\frac ba$. Hence

$$
C = \frac{2\pi\epsilon L}{\ln(b/a)},\qquad \mathcal{C} \equiv \frac{C}{L} = \frac{2\pi\epsilon}{\ln(b/a)}\quad[\text{F/m}].
$$

**Concentric spheres.** Radii $a<b$ with $\epsilon$ between: Gauss gives $E_r = Q/(4\pi\epsilon r^2)$, the drop is $V = \dfrac{Q}{4\pi\epsilon}\Big(\dfrac1a - \dfrac1b\Big)$, and

$$
C = 4\pi\epsilon\,\frac{ab}{b-a}\ \xrightarrow{\ b\to\infty\ }\ 4\pi\epsilon a\quad(\text{an isolated sphere}).
$$

**Layers: series and parallel.** Layers crossed **in succession** (normal to $\mathbf{E}$): $\mathbf{D}$ common, drops add, $\dfrac1C = \sum_i\dfrac1{C_i}$ — **series**. Layers **side by side** (interfaces parallel to $\mathbf{E}$): $\mathbf{E}$ common, charges add, $C = \sum_iC_i$ — **parallel**. Plates with layers $d_1$ ($\epsilon_1$) and $d_2$ ($\epsilon_2$): $\dfrac1C = \dfrac{d_1}{\epsilon_1A} + \dfrac{d_2}{\epsilon_2A}$. Check against the two-layer example of [[concepts/static-fields-in-dielectrics|Static fields in dielectrics]]: $C/A = \big(1/\epsilon_0 + 1/(2\epsilon_0)\big)^{-1} = \tfrac23\epsilon_0$ F/m², and with $2\epsilon_0$ C/m² on the plates, $V = (2\epsilon_0)/(\tfrac23\epsilon_0) = 3$ V ✓. Coax with $\epsilon_1$ on $a<r<c$ and $\epsilon_2$ on $c<r<b$: $\dfrac{1}{\mathcal{C}} = \dfrac{\ln(c/a)}{2\pi\epsilon_1} + \dfrac{\ln(b/c)}{2\pi\epsilon_2}$ — Exam 1 problem 4(e), worked in [[problems/two-layer-coaxial-capacitor|Worked problem — Two-layer coaxial capacitor]]. Two dielectrics each filling *half the angle* of the annulus share $\mathbf{E}$ and add in parallel: $\mathcal{C} = \pi(\epsilon_1+\epsilon_2)/\ln(b/a)$.

**What depends on what.** Inserting a dielectric of $\epsilon_r$ multiplies $C$ by $\epsilon_r$. With $Q$ fixed, $\mathbf{D}$ stays and $\mathbf{E}, V$ fall by $\epsilon_r$; with $V$ fixed, $\mathbf{E}$ stays and $\mathbf{D}, \rho_s, Q$ rise by $\epsilon_r$. Stored energy $W = \tfrac12CV^2 = Q^2/2C$ ([[concepts/electrostatic-energy|Electrostatic energy]]); a lossy filling adds $G = (\sigma/\epsilon)C$ in parallel ([[concepts/conductance|Conductance and the leaky capacitor]]). A [[concepts/pn-junction|pn junction]] has $Q\propto\sqrt V$ and a small-signal $C = dQ/dV = \epsilon_0A/(W_1+W_2)$ that falls as $V^{-1/2}$.

**Examples.**

> [!example] Example — two concept checks that train the chain
> A coax filled with $\epsilon = 3\epsilon_0$ has its dielectric replaced by air.
> - *Same charge:* $Q$ same → $\rho_s$ same → $\mathbf{D}$ same → $\mathbf{E} = \mathbf{D}/\epsilon$ **triples** → $V = \int E\,dr$ **triples** → $C$ drops by 3. "The voltage is reduced by 3×" is **false**.
> - *Same voltage:* $V$ same → $\mathbf{E} = -\nabla V$ **same** → $\mathbf{D} = \epsilon\mathbf{E}$ drops by 3 → $\rho_s$ and $Q$ drop by 3 → $C$ drops by 3. "The E-field is reduced by 3×" is **false**.
>
> $C$ falls by $\epsilon_r$ either way (a property of the structure); *which* of $Q$ and $V$ moves depends on what is held fixed.

> [!trap] Signs, units, series
> - A negative $C$ means you used $V(-)-V(+)$; $V$ is the drop from the positive conductor to the negative one.
> - A slide writes $V = \int_0^d\mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{l}$; with $\mathbf{E} = -(Q/\epsilon A)\hat{x}$ that is *negative*. Correct: $V(d)-V(0) = -\int_0^d\mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{l}$, or "the drop from the positive plate to the negative plate is $\int_+^-\mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{l} = Ed$".
> - Integrate from the positive conductor to the other. The same slip gives negative coax capacitances (FA26 4(e) key, intermediate line; [[0-toolkit/04-errata-in-the-course-materials|errata page]]).
> - F versus F/m: a coax "capacitance" without a length is per unit length, $\mathcal{C}$.
> - Layers crossed in succession are in series, not parallel — the voltages add, the charge is common.
> - The outer conductor of a coax carries $-\lambda$ per unit length on its inner surface; "conductors are neutral, so zero" is wrong.

> [!exam] On exams
> FA26 problem 4 is the coax with two dielectric layers: $\mathbf{D}$ from Gauss (one expression for both layers), $\mathbf{E} = \mathbf{D}/\epsilon$ per layer, $-\lambda$ on the outer conductor, $V(b) - V(a)$ through both layers (negative — the inner conductor is positive), then $\mathcal{C} = \lambda/[V(a) - V(b)]$ in F/m. The series formula gives (e) in one line and checks (d). Gradable errors: a negative $\mathcal{C}$, F instead of F/m, a single $\epsilon$ over $\ln(b/a)$, layers added in parallel. Worked with new numbers in [[problems/two-layer-coaxial-capacitor|Worked problem — Two-layer coaxial capacitor]].
