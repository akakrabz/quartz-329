> [!key] The moving conductor as a battery
> A bar of length $\ell$ moving at $\mathbf{v}$ through $\mathbf{B}$ ($\mathbf{v}\perp\mathbf{B}\perp$ bar) carries an emf
> $$
> \mathcal{E} = \int_{\text{bar}}(\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B})\cdot d\mathbf{l} = vB\ell\quad[\text{V}],
> $$
> with its $+$ terminal where $\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B}$ pushes positive charge and its own resistance $R_1$ as internal resistance. The same number follows from $-d\Psi/dt$ for the loop the bar closes. The lab $\mathbf{E}$ is curl-free ($\partial\mathbf{B}/\partial t = 0$) and **the same in the moving bar as in the stationary load**; only the bar has the $\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B}$ term. With changing flux through a circuit, **a voltmeter reads $\int\mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{l}$ along its own leads**; two meters on the same nodes differ by the emf of the loop their leads enclose.

**Physics.** Cases 2 and 3 of [[concepts/faradays-law|Faraday's law]]: constant $\mathbf{B}$, moving $C$. The flux rule $\mathcal{E} = \oint(\mathbf{E}+\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B})\cdot d\mathbf{l} = -d\Psi/dt$ ([[concepts/electromotive-force|Electromotive force (emf) and path-dependent voltage]]) splits into two resistive pieces when the rails are perfect conductors — $\mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{l} = 0$ along them — and the $\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B}$ integral alone already equals $-d\Psi/dt$, so the two $\mathbf{E}$ integrals cancel: that is why the lab field is equal in bar and load. In the bar's own frame there is no $\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B}$ but an electric field $\mathbf{E}' = \mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B}$ instead; the current is the same. The voltmeter paradox is the other face of the same law: once $\nabla\times\mathbf{E}\ne0$ somewhere, $\int_A^B\mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{l}$ depends on the route, and "the voltage between A and B" stops being a number. This deliberately demolishes [[1-electrostatics/05-curl-free-fields-and-the-electrostatic-potential#4-path-independence|Lecture 5 §4]]; what survives — the voltage *of a path*, and why circuit theory still works — is on [[concepts/electromotive-force|Electromotive force (emf) and path-dependent voltage]].

> [!example] Example 4 — a bar on rails (course notes)
> Perfectly conducting rails 2 m apart, load $R_2$ at $x = 0$, bar of resistance $R_1 = 1\ \Omega$ sliding at $\mathbf{v} = 3\hat x$ m/s, constant $\mathbf{B} = 1\hat y$ T (into the page in the figure's $xz$ view). $C$ counter-clockwise as drawn: up the bar, back along the top rail, down through $R_2$. Right-hand rule: $d\mathbf{S} = -\hat y$, so $\Psi = -1\times2\times3t = -6t$ Wb and $\mathcal{E} = -d\Psi/dt = +6$ V. The motional term alone gives the same: $(\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B})_{\text{bar}} = 3\hat x\times1\hat y = 3\hat z$ V/m, times the 2 m bar.
>
> Faraday's law as the sum of the two resistive pieces (perfect rails: $\mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{l} = 0$ along them):
> $$
> \int_{\text{bar}}(\mathbf{E}+\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B})\cdot d\mathbf{l} + \int_{R_2}\mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{l} = 6\ \text{V}.
> $$
> The $\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B}$ integral is already 6 V, so the $\mathbf{E}$ integrals cancel: $(E_{z,\text{bar}} - E_{z,R_2})\times2 = 0$ — **the lab electric field is the same in the moving bar as in the stationary load.** With $\mathbf{B}$ constant, $\nabla\times\mathbf{E} = -\partial\mathbf{B}/\partial t = 0$: the lab $\mathbf{E}$ is the curl-free, quasi-static Coulomb field of charge piled up on the rails.
> - $R_2 = 2\ \Omega$: $I = 6/3 = 2$ A; 2 V across the bar, 4 V across the load. Bar: $(E_z + 3)\cdot2 = 2\Rightarrow E_z = -2$ V/m; load: $-2E_z = 4\Rightarrow E_z = -2$ V/m ✓.
> - $R_2 = \infty$ (open): $I = 0$; all 6 V across the gap, $E_z = -3$ V/m everywhere, and in the bar $\mathbf{E} + \mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B} = 0$ exactly, as it must with no current.
>
> Circuit picture: the bar is a **battery of emf $vB\ell = 6$ V with internal resistance $R_1$**, $+$ terminal at the top (where $\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B}$ pushes positive charge). The difference between two readings is the emf of the loop their leads form. This is the end of the path-independent voltage of Lecture 5; it returns only where the flux through the leads is negligible.</figcaption></figure>

> [!example] Example 5 — a loop around a solenoid
> A long solenoid with linearly ramping current, so $-d\Psi/dt = 8$ V constant, passes through a small loop of a 1 Ω resistor (right) and a 3 Ω resistor (left) in series. $I_c = 8\ \text{V}/4\ \Omega = 2$ A; drops of 2 V and 6 V in the direction of the current. From the notes:
> - no battery; the loop is excited *inductively*;
> - a voltmeter across the resistors reads 2 V or 6 V depending on whether its probes pass **right or left of the solenoid**: $\mathbf{E}$ outside the solenoid is not conservative *around* it, so $\int\mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{l}$ between the nodes depends on the path;
> - with constant $dI/dt$ the exterior $\mathbf{E}$ is time-independent and $\mathbf{B}$ at the loop is zero: the emf comes from the changing solenoid current, not a changing local $\mathbf{B}$. $\mathcal{E} = -d\Psi/dt$ is the bookkeeping, not the mechanism.

> [!example] Example 6 — the same loop, dissected
> Square loop, area 1 m², $R_1 = 2\ \Omega$ on one side, $R_2 = 1\ \Omega$ on the opposite side, in $\mathbf{B} = (12-3t)\hat z$ T; nodes A and B at opposite corners. $\Psi = 12-3t$ Wb, $\mathcal{E} = 3$ V, $I = 3/3 = 1$ A counter-clockwise. A to B along the $R_1$ side goes *with* the current: drop $V_1 = +2$ V. Along the $R_2$ side, *against* it: $V_2 = -1$ V. They differ by $3$ V, the loop emf. A voltmeter from A (red lead) to B reads 2 V with leads along the $R_1$ path, $-1$ V along the $R_2$ path, $+0.5$ V along the diagonal: the triangle of diagonal and $R_2$ side encloses half the area, so Faraday gives $1.5\ \text{V} = V_{\text{meter}} + 1\ \text{V}$.

*Slides.* The rails as a battery symbol in a circuit diagram; the rotating loop (generator) is on [[concepts/faradays-law|Faraday's law]]; a rod rotating on a circular rail is the practice variant ([[practice/14-faradays-law-and-induced-emf#146-rotating-rod-on-a-circular-rail|14.6]]).

> [!trap] "Voltage" in the presence of a changing flux
> - There is no "voltage between A and B", only the voltage *of a path* (red lead to black for a meter); two meters on the same nodes with differently routed leads differ by $-d\Psi/dt$ through the loop their leads enclose. Neither is "wrong".
> - $d\mathbf{S}$ follows the sense chosen for $C$; in Example 4 that makes $\Psi$ negative and $\mathcal{E}$ positive. Choosing $d\mathbf{S}$ by the field's direction instead flips the sign.
> - Perfect rails contribute nothing to $\oint\mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{l}$; the whole emf sits in the moving bar, the whole drop in bar and load.
> - A loop *translating* through a *uniform* static field has no emf; the bar on rails works because the loop *grows*.

> [!exam] On exams
> *Bar and voltmeter*: the moving conductor is a battery of emf $vB\ell$ with internal resistance; find $I$ from the series circuit, then the voltage across each part; voltmeter readings are path integrals, differing by the emf of the loop the leads form. Show the two routes to $\mathcal{E}$ agree ($-d\Psi/dt$ and $\oint(\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B})\cdot d\mathbf{l}$). Points are lost for $d\mathbf{S}$ the wrong way, forgetting the rails contribute nothing, and claiming one meter is "wrong".
