> [!key] The 3D vector wave equation
> $$
> \nabla^2\mathbf{E} = \mu\epsilon\,\frac{\partial^2\mathbf{E}}{\partial t^2},\qquad \nabla^2\mathbf{H} = \mu\epsilon\,\frac{\partial^2\mathbf{H}}{\partial t^2},
> $$
> i.e. $\dfrac{\partial^2E_x}{\partial x^2}+\dfrac{\partial^2E_x}{\partial y^2}+\dfrac{\partial^2E_x}{\partial z^2} = \mu\epsilon\dfrac{\partial^2E_x}{\partial t^2}$, and likewise for every Cartesian component of $\mathbf{E}$ and $\mathbf{H}$. Conditions: $\rho = 0$, $\mathbf{J} = 0$, constant $\mu$, $\epsilon$. The $\mathbf{H}$ equation follows from the curl of Ampère–Maxwell with $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{H} = 0$ and Faraday; the notes derive only the $\mathbf{E}$ one.
>
> **The 1D scalar wave equation and d'Alembert's general solution.**
> $$
> \frac{\partial^2E_x}{\partial z^2} = \mu\epsilon\,\frac{\partial^2E_x}{\partial t^2} = \frac{1}{v^2}\frac{\partial^2E_x}{\partial t^2},\qquad v\equiv\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu\epsilon}}\ [\text{m/s}],\qquad c = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu_0\epsilon_0}} = 2.998\times10^8\ \text{m/s}.
> $$
> $$
> E_x(z,t) = A\,f\Big(t-\frac zv\Big) + B\,g\Big(t+\frac zv\Big),
> $$
> $f$, $g$ *any* waveforms, $A$, $B$ constants. The first term travels toward $+z$, the second toward $-z$: the minus sign goes with $+z$ (the slide circles "$-z$" with "+z direction" and "$+z$" with "−z direction"). The notes call these the **d'Alembert wave solutions**. $H_y$ obeys the same equation. Handy: $c\approx30$ cm per nanosecond, 300 m per microsecond, 300 km per millisecond.

**Physics: fields without sources.** Take a region that is **homogeneous** (same $\mu$, $\epsilon$ everywhere), **lossless** ($\sigma = 0$) and **source-free**:

$$
\begin{gathered}
\nabla\cdot\mathbf{E} = 0,\qquad \nabla\cdot\mathbf{H} = 0,\\[4pt]
\nabla\times\mathbf{E} = -\mu\frac{\partial\mathbf{H}}{\partial t},\qquad \nabla\times\mathbf{H} = \epsilon\frac{\partial\mathbf{E}}{\partial t}.
\end{gathered}
$$

Conditions: $\rho = 0$, $\mathbf{J} = 0$ (so $\sigma = 0$), $\mu$ and $\epsilon$ constant in space and time — a linear medium ([[concepts/permeability|Permeability, susceptibility, and B = μH]], [[concepts/permittivity|Permittivity, susceptibility, and D = εE]]) or vacuum. A *static* field cannot exist there alone: zero divergence and curl everywhere, dying off far away, means zero (Helmholtz, [[1-electrostatics/04-divergence-and-curl|Lecture 4]]); a capacitor gap holds one only from outside charges. A *time-varying* field can: electromagnetic waves, radio to gamma rays. Their sources (time-varying $\rho$, $\mathbf{J}$, linked by the [[concepts/continuity-equation|continuity equation]]) are elsewhere; once launched, the fields carry on alone. The slides' chain (slide 3, terms circled and joined by arrows in class):

1. a time-varying source current $\mathbf{J}(t)$ produces a time-varying $\mathbf{H}$ around it (Ampère–Maxwell);
2. a time-varying $\mathbf{B} = \mu\mathbf{H}$ produces an $\mathbf{E}$ that curls ([[concepts/faradays-law|Faraday]]);
3. a time-varying $\mathbf{D} = \epsilon\mathbf{E}$ produces an $\mathbf{H}$ that curls (the [[concepts/displacement-current|displacement current]]);
4. back to step 2.

Steps 2–4 need no current: $\cdots\to\mathbf{H}\to\mathbf{E}\to\mathbf{H}\to\cdots$ sustains itself and moves away from the source. That recursion *is* the wave. Without the displacement current step 3 is missing: induction, but no propagation.

> [!derivation]- The 3D equation (notes): curl Faraday's law
> $$
> \nabla\times(\nabla\times\mathbf{E}) = -\mu\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\big(\nabla\times\mathbf{H}\big) = -\mu\epsilon\frac{\partial^2\mathbf{E}}{\partial t^2}.
> $$
> The left side is $\nabla\times(\nabla\times\mathbf{E}) = \nabla(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{E}) - \nabla^2\mathbf{E}$ ([[0-toolkit/02-vector-calculus-cheatsheet#identities-valid-for-any-smooth-fields|toolkit]]), whose first term vanishes since $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{E} = 0$. Each step uses an assumption:
> - **$\mu$ constant**: it leaves the curl, and $\partial\mathbf{B}/\partial t = \mu\,\partial\mathbf{H}/\partial t$ (swapping $\nabla\times$ and $\partial/\partial t$ is always allowed).
> - **$\mathbf{J} = 0$, $\epsilon$ constant**: $\nabla\times\mathbf{H} = \epsilon\,\partial\mathbf{E}/\partial t$. In a conductor $\mathbf{J} = \sigma\mathbf{E}$ adds $\mu\sigma\,\partial\mathbf{E}/\partial t$, and the wave decays (Lectures 22–23).
> - **$\nabla\cdot\mathbf{E} = 0$** needs $\rho = 0$ *and* constant $\epsilon$, since $\nabla\cdot(\epsilon\mathbf{E}) = \epsilon\nabla\cdot\mathbf{E} + \mathbf{E}\cdot\nabla\epsilon$.
> - **Cartesian components**: only there does $\nabla^2\mathbf{E} = \hat x\nabla^2E_x + \hat y\nabla^2E_y + \hat z\nabla^2E_z$ split into scalar Laplacians; not in cylindrical or spherical components.

**The displacement current is the whole story.** Without $\partial\mathbf{D}/\partial t$ the right side is zero: $\nabla^2\mathbf{E} = 0$, Laplace's equation, no waves. The term Maxwell added for charge conservation is the term that produces light.

**Necessary, not sufficient.** A solution of the wave equation must also be divergence-free, and its $\mathbf{E}$, $\mathbf{H}$ must satisfy the first-order curl equations *together*: check $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{E} = 0$ and get $\mathbf{H}$ from Faraday's law ("Why not $\mathbf{E}$ along $z$" below).

**The planar problem: only $E_x(z,t)$ and $H_y(z,t)$.** The notes and the slides reach the reduced problem by different routes.

*The notes' route: try the simplest field.* $\mathbf{E} = \hat x\,E_x(z,t)$, depending on $z$ and $t$ only. Gauss holds ($\partial E_x/\partial x = 0$). Faraday gives $\nabla\times\mathbf{E} = \hat y\,\partial E_x/\partial z$, so $\mathbf{H} = \hat y\,H_y(z,t)$ (a static field left out). The curl equations collapse to:

**The two component equations.**

$$
\frac{\partial E_x}{\partial z} = -\mu\,\frac{\partial H_y}{\partial t},\qquad -\frac{\partial H_y}{\partial z} = \epsilon\,\frac{\partial E_x}{\partial t}.
$$
First: $y$-component of Faraday, $(\nabla\times\mathbf{E})_y = \partial E_x/\partial z$. Second: $x$-component of Ampère–Maxwell, $(\nabla\times\mathbf{H})_x = \partial H_z/\partial y - \partial H_y/\partial z = -\partial H_y/\partial z$ — hence the minus sign. A field varying along $z$ forces its partner to vary in time: the chain above as equations.

*The slides' route: a current sheet as the source.* An infinite sheet $\mathbf{J}_s = -J_s(t)\,\hat x$ [A/m] in the plane $z = 0$, free space on both sides.

- *No $x$ or $y$ dependence*: the sheet looks the same from every $(x, y)$ ("→ 0" inked on slides 8–9).
- *$\mathbf{H}$ along $\pm\hat y$*: for steady $J_s$, [[concepts/current-sheets-and-slabs|Lecture 13]]'s $\mathbf{H} = \tfrac12\mathbf{J}_s\times\hat n = \pm\tfrac12J_s\,\hat y$ for $z\gtrless0$ ($\hat n$ toward the field point).
- *$\mathbf{E}$ along $\hat x$ only*: Faraday's $y$-component, $\partial E_x/\partial z = -\mu_0\,\partial H_y/\partial t$, gives $E_x\neq0$; its $x$-component, $-\partial E_y/\partial z = -\mu_0\,\partial H_x/\partial t = 0$, makes $E_y$ uniform (and, by Ampère–Maxwell, constant), a field that would outlive the source, so set to zero. Gauss off the sheet, $\partial E_z/\partial z = 0$, removes $E_z$ likewise. So $\mathbf{E} = E_x(z,t)\,\hat x$.

The slides thus *derive* what the notes *assume*. Off the sheet the component equations hold with $\mu_0$, $\epsilon_0$. Steady $J_s$ gives uniform fields each side, the static sheet; time-varying $J_s$ couples $E_x$ and $H_y$, and waves leave both faces ([[concepts/current-sheet-radiation|Radiation from a current sheet]] for amplitude and the jump of $H_y$ by $J_s$). Slide 10's ink: "$\mathbf{E}$ always opposite of $\mathbf{J}_s$".

**Why not $\mathbf{E}$ along $z$?** $\mathbf{E} = \hat z\,f(t\mp z/v)$ solves the 1D wave equation, yet $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{E} = \partial E_z/\partial z = \mp f'/v\neq0$ unless $f$ is constant, violating Gauss with $\rho = 0$. Its curl is zero, so Faraday gives it no $\mathbf{H}$ partner, and Ampère–Maxwell then forces $\partial E_z/\partial t = 0$. Necessary-not-sufficient in action: a source-free plane wave has no field component along its travel ([[concepts/plane-waves|Plane waves]]).

**The 1D equation.** Cross-differentiate (slide 15): $\partial/\partial z$ of the first, swap the derivatives, substitute the second:

$$
\frac{\partial^2E_x}{\partial z^2} = -\mu\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\Big(\frac{\partial H_y}{\partial z}\Big) = -\mu\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\Big(-\epsilon\frac{\partial E_x}{\partial t}\Big).
$$

(The 3D equation with $\mathbf{E} = \hat xE_x(z,t)$ gives it directly: the notes' route.)

**Cosines, by substitution (notes).** $E_x = \cos\big(\omega(t - z/v)\big)$ gives $\partial^2E_x/\partial z^2 = -(\omega/v)^2E_x = -\omega^2\mu\epsilon\,E_x$ and $\partial^2E_x/\partial t^2 = -\omega^2E_x$: it works for every $\omega$, as does $\cos\big(\omega(t + z/v)\big)$. The sign inside sets the direction of travel.

**Any waveform, by factoring (slides).** With $\tau\equiv z/v$ (slides: $\tau = z\sqrt{\mu_0\epsilon_0}$ in free space, so 1 m is $\tau = 3.34$ ns), the equation is $\partial^2E_x/\partial\tau^2 = \partial^2E_x/\partial t^2$, and it factors like $x^2 - y^2 = (x+y)(x-y)$:

$$
\Big(\frac{\partial}{\partial\tau}+\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\Big)\Big(\frac{\partial}{\partial\tau}-\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\Big)E_x = 0 .
$$

$\partial_\tau E_x = -\partial_tE_x$ is solved by $E_x = A\,f(t-\tau)$, since $\partial_\tau f(t-\tau) = -f'$ and $\partial_t f(t-\tau) = +f'$ (chain rule, inked on slide 19); $\partial_\tau E_x = +\partial_tE_x$ by $E_x = B\,g(t+\tau)$. Adding, with $\tau = z/v$, gives d'Alembert's solution in the key box.

> [!derivation]- Why every solution has this form, not just these two families
> Put $\xi = t-\tau$, $\zeta = t+\tau$. Then $\partial_\tau = -\partial_\xi + \partial_\zeta$, $\partial_t = \partial_\xi + \partial_\zeta$, so $\partial_\tau + \partial_t = 2\partial_\zeta$ and $\partial_\tau - \partial_t = -2\partial_\xi$, and the wave equation becomes
> $$
> \frac{\partial}{\partial\xi}\Big(\frac{\partial E_x}{\partial\zeta}\Big) = 0 .
> $$
> So $\partial E_x/\partial\zeta$ is a function of $\zeta$ alone; integrating in $\zeta$, $E_x = G(\zeta) + F(\xi)$ with $F$ arbitrary. This is d'Alembert's argument for the vibrating string; the slide's "two first-order equations" is its operator shorthand.

**Why any waveform works (notes).** Constant $\mu$, $\epsilon$ make the equations *linear and time-invariant*: sums and time shifts of solutions are solutions. So $\sum_nA_n\cos\big(\omega_n(t - z/v) + \theta_n\big) = f(t - z/v)$, $f(t) = \sum_nA_n\cos(\omega_nt + \theta_n)$, solves it, and by Fourier analysis (ECE 210) $f$ can be any practical signal. Key: **every frequency travels at the same $v$** ($\mu$, $\epsilon$ frequency-independent), so a pulse keeps its shape — unlike in conductors (Lectures 22–23) or a dispersive dielectric (Lorentz model, [[concepts/conductivity-and-susceptibility-models|Lorentz–Drude models of σ and χe]]).

**What "travels" means.** $f(t - z/v)$ is constant wherever $t - z/v$ is: a feature at $z_0$ at time $t_0$ is at $z_0 + v(t - t_0)$ at time $t$, so the pattern slides rigidly toward $+z$ at $v$. For $g(t + z/v)$ it is at $z_0 - v(t - t_0)$, toward $-z$.

Notation: the slides' $\tau = z/v$ is a time-like *coordinate*; in the notes' margin plots (and Lecture 19) $\tau$ is a *pulse duration*, as in $\triangle(t/\tau)$. Neither is the collision, relaxation or circuit $\tau$ of earlier lectures.

**Examples.** Speeds: vacuum $2.998\times10^8$ m/s (30 cm/ns); polyethylene ($\epsilon_r = 2.25$) $2.00\times10^8$ m/s; $\epsilon_r = 4$, $1.50\times10^8$ m/s; $\epsilon_r\approx9$, $10^8$ m/s ([[concepts/intrinsic-impedance|Intrinsic impedance]] for the matching $\eta$). Polyethylene coax: $1/\sqrt{\mathcal{L}\mathcal{C}} = 1/\sqrt{\mu\epsilon} = 2.00\times10^8$ m/s ([[problems/coax-inductance-and-the-lc-product|Worked problem — Inductance of a coax, and the 𝓛𝓒 product]]); line voltage and current obey the same 1D equation (Unit 4). Reading direction and speed off an argument (slide 24's three waves): [[concepts/plane-waves|Plane waves]].

> [!trap]
> - $\mathbf{E} = \hat z\,f(t - z/v)$ solves the 1D equation but has $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{E} = -f'/v\neq0$.
> - Minus goes with plus: $f(t - z/v)$ and $f(z - vt)$ travel toward $+z$.
> - In $t - z/v$ the coefficient of $z$ is the *slowness* $1/v$ (s/m), not the speed.
> - $\nabla^2$ per component: Cartesian components only.
> - Partial derivatives throughout; the slides' $d/dt$ means $\partial/\partial t$.
> - $\sigma\neq0$ adds $\mu\sigma\,\partial\mathbf{E}/\partial t$: waves decay and change shape (Lectures 22–23). Distortion-free travel needs a lossless medium.

> [!exam] On exams
> **The derivation**, assumptions named (homogeneous, lossless, source-free, constant $\mu$, $\epsilon$), displacement current located as the term that makes it a wave equation; show a given $f(t\mp z/v)$ or cosine satisfies it; **constants**: $c\approx3\times10^8$ m/s $= 300$ m/µs.
