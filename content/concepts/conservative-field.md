> [!key] Four equivalent statements about a vector field $\mathbf{E}$ (on all of space, or any region without holes)
> 1. $\nabla\times\mathbf{E} = 0$ everywhere — **curl-free**.
> 2. $\oint_C\mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{l} = 0$ around every closed loop — zero **circulation**.
> 3. $\int_A^B\mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{l}$ depends only on the endpoints $A$, $B$, not on the path — **path-independent**.
> 4. There is a scalar function $V$ with $\mathbf{E} = -\nabla V$ — the field is a gradient (a **potential** exists).
>
> (1)⟹(2) is Stokes' theorem; (2)⟺(3) is "two paths from $A$ to $B$ make a loop"; (3)⟹(4) is the definition $V(\mathbf{r}) = -\int_{\text{ref}}^{\mathbf{r}}\mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{l}$; (4)⟹(1) is the identity $\nabla\times\nabla V = 0$. The minus sign in (4) makes $V$ the familiar voltage ([[concepts/electrostatic-potential|Electrostatic potential V]]).

**Physics — why static E is conservative.** The point-charge field $\mathbf{E} = \dfrac{Q}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\dfrac{\mathbf{r}}{r^3}$ has components $x\,g(r)$, $y\,g(r)$, $z\,g(r)$ with $g = (4\pi\epsilon_0)^{-1}Q\,r^{-3}$:

$$
(\nabla\times\mathbf{E})_z = \frac{\partial}{\partial x}\big(y\,g(r)\big) - \frac{\partial}{\partial y}\big(x\,g(r)\big)
= y\,g'(r)\frac{x}{r} - x\,g'(r)\frac{y}{r} = 0 ,
$$

likewise for the other components: **every radial field $g(r)\,\mathbf{r}$ is curl-free.** Curl is linear and every static field superposes Coulomb fields, so **all electrostatic fields are curl-free, without exception**. (The notes check the infinite-line field $\dfrac{\rho_l}{2\pi\epsilon_0}\dfrac{x\hat{x}+y\hat{y}}{x^2+y^2}$ the same way: $\partial_x\!\left(\dfrac{y}{x^2+y^2}\right) - \partial_y\!\left(\dfrac{x}{x^2+y^2}\right) = \dfrac{-2xy+2xy}{(x^2+y^2)^2} = 0$.) Time-varying $\mathbf{B}$ breaks this (Faraday, [[2-magnetostatics/14-faradays-law-and-induced-emf|Lecture 14]]): the induced part of $\mathbf{E}$ has curl, and "voltage" then depends on the path.

**The laws of electrostatics.**

$$
\nabla\cdot\mathbf{D} = \rho,\qquad \nabla\times\mathbf{E} = 0,\qquad \mathbf{D} = \epsilon_0\mathbf{E}\ (\text{vacuum}).
$$

**Helmholtz theorem**: a field vanishing at infinity is fixed by its divergence and curl, so these two equations *are* electrostatics: $\rho$ fixes the field ([[concepts/helmholtz-theorem|Helmholtz's theorem]]). Lecture 5 writes $\mathbf{E} = -\nabla V$; Lecture 7 combines both into Poisson's equation $\nabla^2 V = -\rho/\epsilon_0$ ([[concepts/poissons-equation|Poisson's and Laplace's equations]]). When they apply (quasi-statics): [[concepts/electric-field|Electric field E]].

**Why V exists at all: exact differentials (Lecture 5 §3).** Any $V$ gives a curl-free $\mathbf{E} = -\nabla V$:

$$
\nabla\times(\nabla V) =
\begin{vmatrix}\hat{x}&\hat{y}&\hat{z}\\[2pt] \partial_x&\partial_y&\partial_z\\[2pt] \partial_xV&\partial_yV&\partial_zV\end{vmatrix}
= \hat{x}\,(\partial_y\partial_zV - \partial_z\partial_yV) + \cdots = 0
$$

(mixed partials commute). The useful converse: **if $\nabla\times\mathbf{E} = 0$, a potential exists**, because $\mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{l} = E_x\,dx + E_y\,dy + E_z\,dz$ is then an **exact differential** — the total differential $-dV$ of a single function — and can be "antidifferentiated". Two tiny fields:

- $\mathbf{E} = y\,\hat{x} + x\,\hat{y}$: $\mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{l} = y\,dx + x\,dy = d(xy)$, exact. So $dV = -d(xy)$ and $V = -xy + C$. (Check: $-\nabla(-xy) = y\hat{x}+x\hat{y}$ ✓.)
- $\mathbf{E} = y\,\hat{x} - x\,\hat{y}$: $y\,dx - x\,dy$ is *not* a differential. $\partial V/\partial x = -y$ forces $V = -xy + f(y,z)$, so $\partial V/\partial y = -x + \partial f/\partial y$, never the required $+x$. No $V$ — and $\nabla\times\mathbf{E} = -2\hat{z}\neq 0$.

$C$ lets you put $V=0$ anywhere: the **ground** or **reference point**. Only *differences* are physical. The recipe for $V$ from $\mathbf{E}$ (exact differential or staircase path): [[concepts/electrostatic-potential|Electrostatic potential V]].

**Path independence (Lecture 5 §4).** $\int_a^b\mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{l}$ is the limit of sums $\sum_j\mathbf{E}_j\cdot\Delta\mathbf{l}_j$ along a chosen path, $\Delta\mathbf{l}_j$ pointing from $a$ toward $b$; in general it depends on the path. For a curl-free field $\mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{l} = -dV$, and the $-dV$'s along any route sum to $V(a) - V(b)$ — the fundamental theorem of calculus (the gradient theorem, [[concepts/divergence-theorem|Divergence theorem]]).

**Circulation: the loop version of path independence (Lecture 6 §1).** With coinciding endpoints, path independence gives zero for *every* closed loop $C$:

$$
\oint_C\mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{l} = 0 .
$$

$\oint_C\mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{l}$ is the **circulation** of $\mathbf{E}$ around $C$. By Stokes, $\oint_C\mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{l} = \int_S(\nabla\times\mathbf{E})\cdot d\mathbf{S}$ for any $S$ spanning $C$, so zero circulation around every loop is the same as zero curl everywhere. In circuits: **KVL**, potential drops around a loop sum to zero. Curl is local circulation: $(\nabla\times\mathbf{E})_x = \lim\frac{1}{\Delta y\Delta z}\oint_{C_x}\mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{l} = \frac{\partial E_z}{\partial y} - \frac{\partial E_y}{\partial z}$ for a tiny loop in a constant-$x$ plane, and similarly ([[concepts/curl|Curl]]). Right-hand rule: fingers curl along the travel, thumb along $d\mathbf{S}$.

> [!intuition] What a voltmeter measures
> An ideal voltmeter on $p$ and $o$ reads $\int_p^o\mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{l}$ *along its own leads* — a meaningful "voltage between two points" only because static fields make it independent of lead routing. With Faraday's law ([[2-magnetostatics/14-faradays-law-and-induced-emf|Lecture 14]]) the induced $\mathbf{E}$ has curl, and the reading depends on where you drape the wires ([[concepts/electromotive-force|Electromotive force (emf) and path-dependent voltage]]).

**Examples.**

> [!example] Notes, Lecture 5 — two paths, two fields
> Integrate $\mathbf{E} = y\,\hat{x} \pm x\,\hat{y}$ from $o = (0,0,0)$ to $p = (1,1,0)$ along $C_u$ (up the $y$-axis, then across) and $C_l$ (along the $x$-axis, then up). On each leg one coordinate is fixed and its differential vanishes — strike those terms out visibly.
> $$
> C_u:\ \int_{y=0}^{1}(\pm x)\,dy\Big|_{x=0} + \int_{x=0}^{1}y\,dx\Big|_{y=1} = 0 + 1 = 1,
> \qquad
> C_l:\ \int_{x=0}^{1}y\,dx\Big|_{y=0} \pm \int_{y=0}^{1}x\,dy\Big|_{x=1} = 0 \pm 1 = \pm 1.
> $$
> $+$ sign: both paths give 1 — path independent, $V(p) - V(o) = -1$, as $V=-xy$ predicts. $-$ sign: $+1$ versus $-1$, no potential. The difference, 2, is the flux of $\nabla\times\mathbf{E} = -2\hat{z}$ through the square with the loop's (clockwise, $-\hat{z}$) orientation: Stokes' theorem, checked.

> [!example] Notes, Lecture 6 Example 1 — a rectangle in a slab field (circulation)
> Inside a uniformly charged slab centred on $x=0$, $\mathbf{E} = \hat{x}\,\rho x/\epsilon_0$ ([[concepts/symmetric-charge-distributions|Symmetric charge distributions]]), slab wide enough ($\lvert x\rvert<W/2$, $W/2\ge3$ m) to contain the loop. Rectangle $(1,0)$, $(3,0)$, $(3,4)$, $(1,4)$ in the $xy$-plane, in that order (counter-clockwise from $+z$):
> $$
> \oint_C\mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{l} = \underbrace{\int_{x=1}^{3}\frac{\rho x}{\epsilon_0}\,dx}_{4\rho/\epsilon_0} + \underbrace{\int_{y=0}^{4}\hat{x}\frac{3\rho}{\epsilon_0}\cdot\hat{y}\,dy}_{0} + \underbrace{\int_{x=3}^{1}\frac{\rho x}{\epsilon_0}\,dx}_{-4\rho/\epsilon_0} + \underbrace{\int_{y=4}^{0}\hat{x}\frac{\rho}{\epsilon_0}\cdot\hat{y}\,dy}_{0} = 0 .
> $$
> Vertical legs: $\mathbf{E}\perp d\mathbf{l}$, zero; horizontal legs equal and opposite. (The notes' rectangle $-3\le x\le3$ makes each horizontal leg zero alone; off-centre, the cancellation does real work.) Bookkeeping: $d\mathbf{l}$ is $\hat{x}\,dx$ or $\hat{y}\,dy$, always with a plus sign; the *limits* walk around $C$ in the chosen direction.

> [!trap]
> - $\nabla\cdot\nabla V$ is **not** zero. A slide says $\mathbf{E} = -\nabla V$ "satisfies Gauss's law by the identity $\nabla\cdot\nabla V = 0$"; the identity is $\nabla\times\nabla V = 0$ (the *curl* equation). $\nabla\cdot\nabla V = \nabla^2V$ is the Laplacian — Poisson's equation ([[1-electrostatics/07-poisson-and-laplace|Lecture 7]]).
> - "Curl-free" says nothing about $\rho$: a conservative field can have charge as its source ([[concepts/divergence|Divergence]]).
> - Do not antidifferentiate each component separately and add; one integral along one path ([[concepts/electrostatic-potential|Electrostatic potential V]]).

> [!exam] On exams
> FA26 problem 1: $\nabla\times\mathbf{E}$ of a polynomial field (zero), then $\int_A^B\mathbf{E}\cdot d\mathbf{l} = V(A) - V(B)$ *without* a path, because the curl was checked in part (a). Worked with other numbers in [[problems/curl-potential-and-charge-from-a-field|Worked problem — Curl, potential, and charge from a given field]]. Circulation problems: legs with $\mathbf{E}\perp d\mathbf{l}$ are zero; the limits carry the direction.
